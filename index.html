Briefing para Marketing

Coca-Cola consolida posiciones en food service al cerrar un acuerdo histórico con KFC en EE.UU., desplazando a Pepsi tras décadas; un movimiento que refuerza la relevancia del canal QSR como palanca de volumen y visibilidad de marca. Carlsberg apuesta por diversificarse más allá de la cerveza en Alemania, mientras que Alemania avanza en un impuesto al azúcar en bebidas no alcohólicas, señal de que la presión regulatoria y de consumidor hacia opciones más saludables sigue en ascenso globalmente. PepsiCo responde con estrategias de activación estacional —Halloween y NFL— y reformulación de snacks con menos azúcar, marcando la dirección del portafolio hacia perfiles más funcionales y menos procesados.

Briefing para Supply Chain

Alemania aprobó un borrador de impuesto al azúcar en bebidas gaseosas, lo que anticipa presión para reformulación de recetas y ajustes en costos de producción para operadores con exposición europea o que sigan esa tendencia regulatoria. PepsiCo enfrenta una crisis de abastecimiento en India: sus bebidas energéticas Sting podrían agotarse en dos meses por una disputa de etiquetado con la autoridad regulatoria FSSAI, exponiendo la vulnerabilidad del cumplimiento normativo como riesgo operativo en mercados emergentes. La reformulación de snacks de PepsiCo (Doritos, Cheetos, Lay's) con ingredientes más simples y nuevos formatos implica revisión de proveedores y procesos de envasado, una señal de complejidad creciente en la cadena de valor de portafolios multimarca.

Briefing para Ventas / Comercial

El acuerdo KFC-Coca-Cola en EE.UU. redefine el mapa de food service y es una señal de alerta para la competencia en canales institucionales y QSR: la exclusividad en cadenas de escala masiva genera ventajas de volumen y visibilidad difíciles de revertir. Walmart avanza en compromisos contra el 'surveillance pricing' (precios dinámicos por vigilancia de datos), lo que plantea nuevas reglas de juego en negociación de precios y promociones en canal moderno a nivel global. La entrada de bebidas energéticas BFY (better-for-you) como Neutonic y WellWithAll en Walmart marca la aceleración del segmento funcional en el canal masivo, presionando el espacio en góndola y la propuesta de valor de las marcas líderes establecidas.

Walmart’s promise on ‘surveillance pricing’ sets the tone for retail

Walmart se compromete públicamente a no implementar 'surveillance pricing' (ajuste dinámico de precios basado en datos personales), estableciendo un estándar en retail que impacta directamente a fabricantes de bebidas. Esta decisión responde a preocupaciones de confianza del consumidor tras años de inflación y escándalos de fijación de precios, generando presión sobre competidores para adoptar políticas similares. Para la industria de bebidas, esto define nuevas reglas de juego en pricing y distribución, afectando márgenes y estrategias comerciales con retailers.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
Trust lies at the center of the relationship among grocers, food and beverage manufacturers and shoppers. More than half a decade of upheaval – from <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/09/21/grocery-inflation-is-expected-to-rise-later-this-year/" target="_blank" rel="">inflation</a> and pandemic-driven shifts to online shopping and <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/07/15/doj-egg-price-fixing-settlement-criticized-as-too-small/" target="_blank" rel="">price-fixing</a> scandals involving major commodities – has put that trust to the test. That became clear in late September, when Walmart President and CEO John Furner penned a letter to the retail giant’s customers promising that the company was not using its technology to overcharge them with its rapidly growing fleet of electronic shelf labels (ESL). It was a surprising move for a company that has spent the last decade investing heavily in tech as a <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/06/24/walmart-amazon-escalate-grocery-delivery-war-as-same-day-demand-surges/" target="_blank" rel="">challenge</a> to its biggest competitor, Amazon. Walmart’s announcement follows a wave of <a href="https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/9/algorithmic-pricing-under-fire-state-restrictions-on-personalized-and-surveillance-pricing" target="_blank" rel="">state laws</a> and city ordinances restricting the use of personal data to set grocery prices. Maryland’s <a href="https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/HB0895?ys=2026RS" target="_blank" rel="">law</a> took effect Oct. 1, but it is not the most recently enacted measure: <a href="https://www.nj.gov/governor/news/2026/20260723a.shtml" target="_blank" rel="">New Jersey enacted</a> a related law in July, and <a href="https://wilson.seattle.gov/2026/09/23/seattle-becomes-first-city-to-stop-grocery-stores-from-using-ai-based-personal-data-to-set-prices/" target="_blank" rel="">Seattle’s mayor signed</a> a grocery-pricing ordinance Oct. 5. The important distinction is that most of these measures target personalized “surveillance pricing,” rather than banning all price changes driven by demand, inventory or time of day. It’s not just states that are giving a skeptical side-eye to the industry. Grocers are also taking heat from the Trump Administration, which launched an <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/12/09/trump-orders-probe-into-rising-food-prices/" target="_blank" rel="">investigation</a> into high grocery prices last December, noting at the time that, “in recent years, major players in America’s food supply chain have paid tens of millions to settle price-fixing lawsuits.” Trump talked big in his <a href="https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/addressing-security-risks-from-price-fixing-and-anti-competitive-behavior-in-the-food-supply-chain/" target="_blank" rel="">Executive Order 14364</a>, noting that some sectors of the food supply, “including meat processing, seed, fertilizer, and equipment” are vulnerable to price-fixing. The German government has approved a draft plan to introduce a tax on sugary drinks, with the proposed levy set to take effect on July 1, 2027, subject to parliamentary approval. According to the finance ministry, the tax aims to reduce sugar consumption, generate revenue for the healthcare system and protect public health, “particularly of children and adolescents”. The proposed tax would apply to drinks containing at least 5g of sugar per 100ml, with a rate of €0.26 per litre for drinks containing at least 5g and up to 8g of sugar per 100ml, rising to €0.32 per litre for those containing more than 8g. The measures would cover soft drinks, energy drinks, lemonades, iced teas and sweetened fruit drinks. Pure fruit and vegetable juices, non-alcoholic beers and wines, and alcoholic drinks would be exempt. Smaller manufacturers producing up to 70,000 litres annually would be exempt from the duty. A further exemption for businesses producing up to one million litres a year would require approval from the European Commission under state aid rules. For manufacturers subject to the levy, the additional duty could affect production costs and pricing strategies. Businesses will need to assess whether to absorb the tax, pass some of the cost on to retailers and consumers, or adjust their product portfolios in response. The German government expects the levy to generate around €690 million in 2027, rising to €925 million in 2028. The revenue is intended to help stabilise contributions to the statutory health insurance system. The proposal has faced criticism over its potential economic impact. German public broadcaster ARD’s Tagesschau news service reported that Hessian Minister-President Boris Rhein of the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) argued that the tax would make drinks more expensive and could threaten jobs in the beverage industry. The tiered structure could give manufacturers a financial incentive to reduce sugar content in products, particularly those close to the proposed thresholds. Companies could review existing recipes to assess whether reformulation would move products into a lower tax band or below the taxable threshold. The UK’s Soft Drinks Industry Levy, introduced in 2018, provides an example of how a tiered tax can influence product formulation. The UK government reported that the average sugar content per 100ml in affected soft drinks fell by 28.8% between 2015 and 2018. By early 2019, only around 15% of eligible soft drinks still contained enough sugar to be liable for the levy, down from 52% when the policy was first announced in 2016, according to the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR). Germany would join several European countries that already tax sugary drinks, including France, Portugal and Hungary. Hungary introduced a broader public health tax in 2011 covering certain products with high levels of sugar and other ingredients. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends taxing sugar-sweetened beverages as a public health measure, citing evidence that such taxes can increase prices and reduce purchases of taxed drinks.
