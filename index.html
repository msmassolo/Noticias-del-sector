How India is reversing the alcohol-free cocktail trend

India's bartenders are flipping the script, turning beloved non-alcoholic drinks like nimbu pani and panakam into top-tier cocktails.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
While much of the West has embraced alcohol-free twists on classic cocktails, India’s bartenders are doing the opposite. They’re turning beloved non-alcoholic drinks – from nimbu pani to panakam – into cocktails without losing their cultural identity. In most Western drinking cultures, non-alcoholic serves have spent years arriving with an apology. Virgin this. Zero-proof that. Even the word ‘mocktail’ suggests a knock-off – a parody of an original that still holds the power. In India, however, they are the main act, not the understudy. Long before anyone started debating the future of no and low, India had a thriving drinking culture built around regional beverages that were naturally non-alcoholic. Panakam. Nimbu pani. Jaljeera. Nannari. Aam panna. Bel sharbat. Rooh Afza. Thandai. Shikanji. Kala khatta. The list is very long and very local – shaped by climate, produce and the body’s seasonal needs. Panakam cools and aids digestion in the southern heat. Nannari, made from Indian sarsaparilla root, is prized as a cooling, detoxifying summer staple. Kashmiri kahwa warms with saffron, dried fruits and spice. Butter tea exists to temper brutal Himalayan winters. Nobody drinks these as an alternative to alcohol. They order them by name. It’s the same logic that underpins Indian vegetarian food – pav bhaji, idli, bhel and vada pav were never drafted in as meat-free stand-ins; they are the main event for vegetarians and carnivores alike. This was the inspiration behind John Distilleries-owned Malhar Gin’s Sherbet campaign – designing cocktails based on beloved local non-alcoholic beverages. “Across India, we have a rich tradition of cooling sherbets made from fruits, flowers, herbs, spices and seasonal ingredients,” says Heemanshu Ashar, global brand ambassador at John Distilleries. “Every region, community and season has its own recipes and traditions. These non-alcoholic drinks have been part of Indian hospitality culture for generations.” It’s logical then that if India were to offer the world its signature serves, why not start with what already lives in people’s memories, and build both a cocktail and a non-alcoholic serve from the same source? ‘Sherbets of Malhar’ launched in 2024 as a marketing campaign and has since evolved into an ongoing platform. The Malhar team researched more than 50 traditional sherbet recipes from across India and explored how they behaved with Malhar Classic Dry and Malhar Citrus Gin. Using modern mixology techniques, they began reinterpreting those flavours through bar takeovers, pop‑ups and guided tastings rather than a purely above-the-line campaign. The consumer response followed an interesting arc: “The most gratifying moment was watching skepticism turn into discovery and then into delight,” Ashar says. “Consumers began sharing personal memories connected to the flavours in the glass.” The idea of reinterpretation rather than replacement came up repeatedly in my conversations with bar owner, educator and industry veteran Yangdup Lama. “You can’t completely replicate anything that is original,” he says. “What we are trying to do is capture the core flavour profile.” Lama grew up on pahadi (mountain) butter tea – a rich Himalayan staple made with tea, salt and butter, poured into cups that were constantly refilled. At Brook Bar in Gurgaon, he reimagines it as a cocktail called Pho Cha: whisky fat‑washed with yak butter, a saline solution for savoury lift, served cold to suit the Delhi heat. “It’s like interpreting a hot coffee for an Espresso Martini,” he says. “You don’t expect a hot drink, but you do expect the flavour of espresso.” While most bartenders cite the Daiquiri as the classic test of balance and texture, for Lama, it is the humble nimbu pani – a simple lemon drink that appears across India under different names and formats. “If you can make a good nimbu pani with the right balance of lime and sugar, you can balance any drink,” he says. Vikram Achanta, founder of drinks consultancy Tulleeho, agrees with this logic. He points to Bandra Born’s Kadak Nimbu Sharbat in Mumbai – a serve that uses Mahua flower spirit to amplify a familiar flavour into a cocktail without stripping it of its original identity. In many international low‑and‑no programmes, the benchmark is the alcoholic ‘original’ – the Negroni, the Spritz , the Martini – from which something is then subtracted to create its alcohol-free counterpart. But for an Indian bartender spoiled for choice of non-alcoholic drinks growing up, the starting point is addition rather than subtraction. India’s tropical bounty – jamun, kokum, bael, jackfruit, Himalayan herbs and regional spices –are no longer novelty garnishes; they are being treated as cultural reference points. Lama believes this drive to look inward is happening across the globe, but India has an unusually deep archive to draw from. That archive is what Sherbets of Malhar is mining. Malhar’s citrus, herbal, spicy and floral flavours naturally overlap with the ingredients that dominate traditional sherbets, which makes the platform feel like an extension of the liquids rather than a bolt‑on. “It allows the gin to interact with flavours that consumers already recognise and enjoy while creating something contemporary and relevant for today’s cocktail culture,” Ashar says. In 2025, Malhar launched the Sherbets of Malhar Bartender Competition in partnership with Tulleeho. “Instead of asking participants to draw inspiration from global trends, the competition challenged them to revisit drinks that unlocked personal memories,” says Achanta. “One participant captured the essence of petrichor (the first smell of monsoon when sunbaked earth is soaked with rain) by washing Malhar with mineral-rich alluvial soil.” Entries came in from across India, and the most memorable serves were backed by specific stories – childhood summers, family recipes, village visits, local festivals. That focus on story matters for the trade because it gives venues a way to talk about non‑alcoholic culture without slipping into deficit language: without, minus, free‑from. It would be hubris to say that India is alone in starting with the non‑alcoholic original. There’s Singaporean bandung, Mesoamerican horchata and atole, sorrel across West Africa and its diaspora communities. The sorrel, made from hibiscus roselle, occupies a very similar place to sherbet: a multi- named, highly specific drink that defines the occasion, season and location long before anyone thinks about ABV. “You don’t order a hibiscus drink; you order sorrel, with all the spice, sugar, clove and ginger arguments that entails,” says Ellie Meek-Orr, a bartender at Doghouse Distillery who comes from West African origin. Rather than treating these drinks as just low‑and‑no options, bartenders are building on the memories they evoke, then folding in rum, whisky or agave spirits while keeping the name and the cultural frame intact. This is where Malhar’s sherbet platform feels less like a niche Indian exercise and more like the start of a wider shift. For brands and bars in any market with a wide diaspora, the opportunity is not to invent ever more elaborate ‘alternatives’ but to, perhaps, look at the global archive of named non-alcoholic drinks that carry enough memory and emotion to stand on their own. In this aspect, India’s evolving drinks culture can feel ahead of the conversation.

El negocio de la cerveza llega a la segunda mitad de 2026 con una paradoja que marca todo el sector: se vende menos volumen o, en el mejor de los casos, apenas se mantiene, pero los ingresos resisten por el mayor peso de las gamas premium, la cerveza sin alcohol y de baja graduación, las subidas de precios y la entrada en refrescos, mixers y otras bebidas. Los datos de producción y consumo apuntan en esa dirección. BarthHaas calcula que la producción mundial cerró 2024 en 1.875 millones de hectolitros. La concentración empresarial sigue siendo muy alta. Según el ranking de BarthHaas para 2025, AB InBev produjo 484,19 millones de hectolitros; Heineken, 235,65 millones; China Resources Snow, 110,30 millones; y Carlsberg, 99 millones. Si se comparan esas cifras con la producción mundial estimada para ese año, AB InBev controló alrededor del 25,5% del volumen; Heineken, el 12,4%; China Resources Snow, el 5,8%; y Carlsberg, el 5,2%. Molson Coors mostró una situación más ajustada: su volumen financiero cayó un 8,6%, mientras las ventas netas por hectolitro subieron un 4,8% y el coste de ventas por hectolitro avanzó un 5,8%. Poland proposes major expansion of sugar tax to target more beverages and concentrates Poland is proposing to significantly increase its sugar tax and widen its scope to cover more beverages, concentrates and drink-form dietary supplements, in a move the government says will strengthen public health measures and increase funding for healthcare. The draft amendment to Poland’s 2015 Public Health Act, published by the Polish government in June 2026, would increase existing rates while closing exemptions that the government says have allowed some manufacturers to avoid the levy. The proposal is currently at an early stage, with the government targeting adoption of the legislation in the third quarter of 2026. If approved, the changes are proposed to take effect from 1 January 2027. The government argues that the existing sugar tax, introduced in 2020, has become too low relative to beverage prices to meaningfully influence consumer behaviour. According to the draft, sales of carbonated drinks initially fell by 19% after the levy was introduced, but subsequently returned to pre-tax levels. Under the proposed changes, the fixed rate for beverages containing up to 5g of sugar per 100ml, or any quantity of specified sweeteners, would rise from PLN 0.50 to PLN 0.70 per litre. The variable charge for every gram of sugar above 5g per 100ml would double from PLN 0.05 to PLN 0.10, while the charge for caffeine or taurine would increase from PLN 0.10 to PLN 1.00 per litre. The maximum levy would also increase from PLN 1.20 to PLN 1.80 per litre. The proposed legislation would also broaden the tax beyond ready-to-drink beverages. All drink concentrates would become subject to the levy regardless of whether they are sold as liquids, semi-liquids, solids or syrups. A separate rate of PLN 3 per litre or kilogram of concentrate is proposed, reflecting the government's view that concentrated products can contain significantly more sugar than beverages ready for consumption. Drink-form dietary supplements would also be brought into scope, although products sold in packs of no more than 200ml would be excluded. The government has specifically highlighted highly sweetened fruit syrups that it says have shifted from being marketed as fortified foods to dietary supplements since the sugar tax was introduced. It argues that this has enabled manufacturers to avoid the levy while placing products with high sugar content in a category perceived by consumers as health-supporting. The proposal would additionally remove an existing exemption for beverages containing at least 20% juice and no more than 5g of sugar where they also contain caffeine, taurine or sweeteners. The government says some manufacturers have reformulated products to meet the current exemption by increasing juice content and reducing sugar in favour of sweeteners such as aspartame, sucralose and acesulfame K. Under the new rules, these products could become subject to the levy. The proposal would affect a range of products, including certain energy drinks, sweetened beverages and non-alcoholic beer. The government estimates that total NFZ expenditure reached PLN 220.2bn in 2025, while the direct costs associated with obesity – including prevention, diagnosis and treatment – could reach between PLN 4.4bn and PLN 15.4bn.
