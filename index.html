Why functional drinks aren’t translating into sales

Las bebidas funcionales enfrentan un desafío crítico en Reino Unido: el interés del consumidor supera significativamente las ventas reales en bares y restaurantes. Aunque la categoría crece en conciencia (posicionada en hidratación, energía y salud digestiva), representa aún una porción menor del mercado de bebidas no alcohólicas en canal on-premise. Este gap entre awareness y conversión en compras es una lección estratégica para operadores en mercados similares como Argentina, donde impulsar adopción requiere replanteamiento de distribución y comunicación para monetizar el interés del consumidor.

Texto completo del artículo
Consumer interest in functional drinks is outpacing category performance in Great Britain’s pubs, bars and restaurants, leaving brands with the challenge of turning growing awareness into actual purchases. Functional beverages refer to non-alcoholic drinks positioned around benefits for physical or mental health, wellbeing or specific bodily functions, such as hydration, energy and gut health. Despite growing interest, the category remains a small part of the on-premise soft drinks market. According to NielsenIQ (NIQ), functional drinks account for just 0.1% of soft drinks volume share in GB, despite distribution growing by 3.5%. That wider availability has not, however, translated into stronger sales. Volume rate of sale per outlet has declined by 16.7% year on year, pointing to a gap between getting products into venues and persuading consumers to buy them. “The challenge is more so execution rather than demand not being there,” says Cerys Littlejohn of NielsenIQ. One of the barriers is visibility. Unlike beer and cider, which benefit from prominent positioning on bars and draught pumps, functional drinks are often kept in fridges behind the bar, making them less visible to consumers. How brands communicate their benefits also matters. NIQ says consumers respond better to straightforward, easy-to-understand propositions such as hydration, energy, sleep and relaxation, rather than more complex or specialist functional claims. There is also some evidence that consumers are prepared to pay a premium for these benefits. NIQ’s GB BrandTrack survey, conducted in February 2026, found that 51% of respondents would pay more for a soft drink offering additional health benefits. Health benefits alone are unlikely to win over consumers. When choosing a drink, the fundamentals still count, with brands needing to balance their functional proposition with taste and value for money. According to NIQ, price and value for money, alongside taste and flavour options, are among the most important considerations for consumers. Around half of respondents identified each as important factors in their decision-making. Taste is also key to encouraging repeat purchases. As Littlejohn explains, consumers are not buying functionality for its own sake. “They also buy the products because it tastes good,” she says. So where might functional drinks find their place in the on-premise market? NIQ points to casual social occasions, including quick drinks and catch-ups with friends, as potential opportunities for brands to make these beverages a more natural part of the experience. Functional drinks could also appeal to consumers moderating their alcohol intake. However, NIQ says it is too early to determine whether they would directly replace traditional drinking occasions. For manufacturers, the opportunity is not simply to expand distribution. It is to make products visible, accessible and easy to understand, while delivering on the taste and value consumers expect. Until then, consumer curiosity may continue to run ahead of category sales.
