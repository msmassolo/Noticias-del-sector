Semaglutide, the active ingredient in the in-demand diabetes drug Ozempic, reduced heavy drinking days and alcohol-related harm in a phase 2 clinical trial of people with alcohol use disorder. An oral form of semaglutide significantly reduced heavy drinking days among adults seeking treatment for alcohol use disorder, although it did not meet the study’s primary endpoint for laboratory-assessed craving. Researchers said the mixed results support larger trials rather than establishing the drug as a treatment. An oral form of semaglutide reduced heavy drinking days, alcohol-related harm and everyday cravings in a small clinical trial involving people with moderate to severe alcohol use disorder. The phase 2 study, published in the American Journal of Psychiatry , enrolled 50 treatment-seeking adults and compared daily oral semaglutide with a placebo over eight weeks. Participants received 3mg of semaglutide each day for four weeks, followed by 7mg daily for the remaining four weeks. The researchers found that semaglutide significantly reduced the number of heavy drinking days compared with placebo. Participants taking the drug also consumed fewer drinks on the days when they drank. However, semaglutide did not significantly reduce the study’s primary outcome, alcohol craving measured in a laboratory after participants were exposed to their preferred drink. It also failed to produce a statistically significant reduction in the average number of drinks consumed per calendar day. During the final four weeks of treatment, participants receiving semaglutide recorded significantly fewer heavy drinking days than those taking the placebo. The drug also reduced drinks per drinking day, naturalistic alcohol cravings reported outside the laboratory and alcohol-related negative consequences. More people in the semaglutide group reduced their World Health Organisation risk drinking level by at least one category. Cannabis use days also declined among the small group of participants who reported using cannabis before the study, although this was an exploratory finding rather than one of the trial’s principal outcomes. The authors concluded that the results support continued investigation of semaglutide as a possible treatment for alcohol use disorder. Despite the reductions in several drinking measures, the trial did not find a significant difference between semaglutide and placebo in cue-induced craving at week six. Participants were asked to hold and smell their preferred alcoholic drink for three minutes before rating the strength of their craving. The scores did not differ significantly between the two groups. The researchers also found no statistically significant reduction in drinks per calendar day during the final four weeks, although the result approached the study’s significance threshold. The findings therefore present a more qualified picture than reports suggesting GLP-1 medicines simply remove the desire to drink. Interest in drugs such as semaglutide has grown following anecdotal reports from patients using GLP-1 receptor agonists for diabetes or weight management who said their interest in alcohol had diminished. Animal studies and observational research have also suggested that the medicines may influence reward pathways involved in alcohol consumption. For some producers, the shift away from red wine consumption has prompted them to make rosé from existing red vineyards, while others have experimented with blanc de noirs – white wines made from red grapes such as Malbec ( Trivento ) and Cabernet ( Changyu Moser XV ). Some may be grafting over or replanting red vineyards with white varieties. Large-scale vineyard removals have also taken place, particularly in red-focused Chile and Bordeaux, which have each uprooted nearly 30,000 hectares over the past three years. Both regions are now looking to reposition themselves. Chile is promoting the crisp whites and lighter reds from its cooler Pacific-facing vineyards under the Chile Pacífico banner , highlighting fresh Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir and coastal seafood pairings – a notable shift for a country better known for Carmenère and steak straight from the asado. Bordeaux, meanwhile, is promoting “chillable” reds, reviving its historic “claret” name for a new generation of lighter, fruit-forward wines designed to be served cold and appeal to younger consumers. Whether the strategy succeeds remains to be seen, although major brands including Calvet and Mouton Cadet have already embraced the concept. Clearly, forecasting what people will drink when the mercury climbs is hard to do accurately. But there’s a bigger concern for anyone making and selling wine: consumers might move away from the drink altogether if temperatures continue to rise. Indeed, some of the category’s wider struggles may themselves be linked to climate: at a certain point, wine’s alcohol content becomes less appealing. Lower-alcohol beverages – especially beer – and soft drinks typically gain ground during periods of extreme heat. But if temperatures continue climbing, the bigger challenge may not be which wines consumers choose – but whether they choose wine at all.
