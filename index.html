Walmart rolls back prices as Target remakes the food aisle

Walmart y Target reportaron resultados sólidos en Q3, con alimentos como motor principal pero estrategias divergentes. <a href="https://stock.walmart.com/" target="_blank" rel="">Walmart</a> and <a href="https://corporate.target.com/investors/financial-information" target="_blank" rel="">Target</a> both reported strong quarters in their November earnings calls, and while food did a lot of the heavy lifting, the two mass merchandise retail giants took different approaches to grocery in Q3. At Walmart US, food strength was led by pantry products, supported by seasonal events and better-for-you innovation, according to the company. Sam’s Club, the Walmart’s warehouse format, focused on price cuts and fresh departments in the third quarter. Walmart’s grocery sales grew in the mid-single digits, with US comparable sales up 2.9% year over year. Grocery comparable sales grew mid-single digits, while total Walmart US comparable sales, excluding fuel, rose 2.9%. Meanwhile, Target continued to benefit from a substantial overhaul of its center-store assortment, adding thousands of new items. Target’s food and beverage business grew to $6.4 billion, making up nearly a quarter (24%) of merchandise sales for the quarter. Walmart reported that its investment in price reductions paid off in market share in the third quarter. Walmart cut prices, which it calls “rollbacks,” on over 11,000 products by the end of Q2, while Sam’s Club cut prices in fresh food and other categories, explained Walmart CEO John Furner. The third quarter included roughly 7,700 price rollbacks, including non-grocery items. Both Q2 and Q3 exceeded the typical number of rollbacks, which normally run between 5,000 and 5,500 items. Walmart CFO John Rainey added that more than doubling the number of price rollbacks has resulted in some of the highest share gains in grocery in three years at Walmart. He also attributed the grocery market-share gains to the retail leader’s “customer value proposition” and “faster delivery speeds.” Walmart said its rollbacks were partly financed through <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/05/14/tariff-refunds-what-brands-need-to-know/" target="_blank" rel="">tariff refunds</a>. Target also experienced “strong momentum” in food sales in the third quarter, according to Cara Sylvester, executive vice president and chief merchandising officer. The strong sales growth of nearly 7% in Q3 was the result of the retailer’s center-store assortment reset, explained Sylvester, who <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/20/target-stock-rises-on-tariff-refund-and-q2-growth/" target="_blank" rel="">said in Q2</a> that Target aimed to make the retailer a destination for food products, “not simply a category a guest shops while they’re in our stores, but a reason they come to Target.” Target changed the presentation of nearly half the center store grocery assortment and introduced a range of new products, Sylvester said. The biggest gains came from snacks, candy and beverages, she noted. “Since the reset, those categories have seen net sales growth of nearly 10%,” she said. “We also introduced more than 2,000 new food items this quarter, up nearly 40% from last year.” “Bringing emerging brands, exciting flavors and better-for-you options guests might not find at the grocer down the street, and with 40 million more transactions containing food items so far this year compared with the same period last year, we’re seeing encouraging evidence that food is becoming an even more important part of the Target shopping trip,” according to Sylvester.

South Africa’s superfoods: Can untapped gold turn a profit?

Sudáfrica posiciona ingredientes autóctonos (baobab, marula, rooibos y granos antiguos) como oportunidades de innovación en bebidas funcionales y premium. La tendencia global de consumidores por ingredientes con narrativa de sustentabilidad abre una ventana para que fabricantes de bebidas incorporen estos superfoods, similar a cómo Brasil y Japón capitalizaron açaí y matcha. Para empresas de bebidas argentinas, representa un modelo de diferenciación mediante ingredientes diferenciados y storytelling de origen, relevante para segmentos premium y funcionales en expansión en mercados regionales.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
Brazil gave the world açaí and Japan turned matcha into a global obsession, so could baobab, marula, rooibos and ancient African grains be poised for their own moment in the spotlight? With consumers embracing everything from turmeric lattes and mushroom coffees to protein-packed ancient grains, the appetite for ingredients with a story to tell shows little sign of fading. And South Africa has plenty of stories waiting to be told, particularly as food manufacturers hunt for new flavours, textures and nutritional benefits. There’s money in the opportunity, too. SA’s indigenous crops and medicinal plants represent an estimated R12bn (approximately €620m/£530m) annual economy, according to the National Agricultural Marketing Council, with indigenous crops accounting for around R9bn (€465m/£400m). The estimates cover more than food ingredients but reveal the scale of a sector that’s historically struggled for formal recognition. Much of the trade takes place through informal markets and small-scale producers, outside the commercial support structures enjoyed by mainstream agricultural commodities. Matcha’s journey from Japanese tea ceremonies to chocolate, cookies and ice cream shows how far a traditional ingredient can travel, while açaí has made a similar leap through smoothie bowls and functional snacks. SA’s indigenous foods, however, have a more complicated history. Colonial agricultural policies, followed by apartheid between 1948 and 1994, favoured commercial crops such as maize and wheat while traditional African food systems received comparatively little investment. Now, as interest in biodiversity and climate-resilient agriculture grows, those perceptions are changing. Whether that attention can translate into a profitable food manufacturing industry is another matter entirely. If any African ingredient looks ready to give açaí a run for its money, it’s baobab. The fruit of Africa’s iconic upside-down tree has already found its way into health powders and snack bars, although mainstream adoption remains a bigger challenge. Its naturally dry, tangy pulp can be milled into powder without extensive dehydration, making it attractive for confectionery, bakery, beverages and fruit snacks. Vitamin C and fibre provide nutritional interest, while its sharp flavour offers something different from conventional fruit ingredients. British brand Aduna has developed baobab-based products, while suppliers including Nexira and SA’s EcoProducts participate in the wider value chain. Local producer Good Life Organic also showcased baobab powder and baking powder at Anuga 2025. Marula has a head start in international recognition, although most consumers probably know it better from their drinks cabinet than their biscuit tin. Amarula, owned by Heineken Beverages, sold approximately 1.2 million nine-litre cases worldwide in 2025, ranking seventh among the world’s best-selling liqueur brands. It’s a compelling example of how an indigenous African fruit can underpin an international business, although marula’s potential extends well beyond alcohol into juices, confectionery, flavourings and edible seed ingredients. ADM is exploring marula, baobab and African custard apple through its Johannesburg flavour development facility. The company reports that 83% of SA consumers often or sometimes enjoy combining traditional flavours with innovative ingredients, suggesting the opportunity isn’t restricted to exports. Rooibos, meanwhile, doesn’t have to prove that indigenous ingredients can succeed internationally. It’s already done it. Exports reached approximately 10,930 tonnes in 2025, almost double the 5,900 tonnes recorded a decade earlier. Japan accounted for 33%, while the industry now supplies more than 50 markets. The next opportunity is extending rooibos beyond the teacup into extracts, desserts, confectionery, functional foods and savoury applications. “The more ways we find to use Rooibos, the more opportunities there are to create value around it,” says Adele du Toit, spokesperson for the SA Rooibos Council. “Every new application creates the possibility of a new product, a new business or even a new market.” Baobab and marula might have the glamour, but traditional grains and root crops could prove equally valuable in everyday food production. Sorghum, long associated with porridge and fermented drinks, is finding applications in bread, breakfast cereals, gluten-free foods and extruded snacks. Its drought tolerance adds another advantage as manufacturers search for crops capable of coping with unpredictable weather. In October, SA ingredient developer Synercore produced 100 loaves containing popped sorghum from its extrusion plant. The West Cape-based company said the ingredient added texture without requiring pre-soaking, simplifying bakery applications. The loaves weren’t a retail launch but were donated to Magda’s Soup Kitchen in Paarl as part of World Food Day. Amadumbe, a traditional taro-type staple, is also finding a place in the crisp aisle. KwaZulu-Natal producer Sibocali Foods, founded by Sibongile Mtsabe, has commercialised amadumbe crisps alongside cassava, sweet potato and banana snacks. Its 40g chilli-flavoured amadumbe crisps sell online for R17.99 (€0.93/£0.80), while the business aims to create opportunities for small-scale farmers. Other African ingredients, including Bambara groundnut, finger millet and marama bean, are attracting research interest for gluten-free foods and protein-enriched products. At the University of Pretoria, researcher Daddy Kgonothi has <a href="https://www.up.ac.za/news/qa-young-researcher-turns-phd-work-patent-pending-food-science-innovation" target="_blank" rel="">developed a patent-pending modified marama bean protein concentrate intended to improve gluten-free baked goods.</a> Research published in the Journal of Food Science in August examined <a href="https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1750-3841.71382" target="_blank" rel="">five indigenous seed flours, including marula and Lowveld chestnut,</a> at wheat-flour substitution levels of 10%, 15% and 20%. Both showed promising nutritional and baking characteristics, although higher inclusion levels affected aspects of dough stability and bread structure. Marula flour contained 26.8% protein and 34.5% fat, highlighting its nutritional potential alongside the formulation challenges posed by oil-rich ingredients. While the nutritional argument might win over consumers, it won’t, however, necessarily convince a production manager. An ingredient that compromises texture, shortens shelf life or increases costs could quickly lose its appeal. Specialist ingredients might command a premium in health-food bars or functional beverages, but the economics become tougher when manufacturers need tonnes of consistent-quality material. Processing capacity, standardised specifications, regulatory approvals and reliable agricultural supply will determine which crops graduate from promising experiments to commercially viable products. There’s another side to the superfood gold rush that doesn’t always make it onto the packaging: who gets paid when an ingredient harvested by local communities becomes fashionable thousands of miles away? In July 2025, SA formally recognised <a href="https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/minister-john-steenhuisen-unlocks-r12-billion-indigenous-economy-boost-06-nov" target="_blank" rel="">indigenous crops and medicinal plants as agricultural products</a> under the Marketing of Agricultural Products Act, potentially opening access to greater commercial support. Then-Agriculture Minister John Steenhuisen welcomed the change. “For too long our people who harvest and trade traditional medicines and indigenous crops have been operating in the economic shadows,” he said. “Outdated legislation is a hinderance to the rapid absorption of new technologies, remedies, practices and innovations – which are essential if we are to improve yield, health and profitability in the agricultural sector.” Yet recognition doesn’t automatically guarantee fair returns. <a href="https://www.gov.za/news/speeches/minister-barbara-creecy-launch-rooibos-traditional-knowledge-industry-wide-benefit?utm_source=chatgpt.com" target="_blank" rel="">A landmark 2019 rooibos agreement</a> established benefit-sharing arrangements between the industry and indigenous San and Khoi communities, with more than R12.2m paid into community trusts in 2022. However, <a href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03057070.2023.2301640" target="_blank" rel="">University of Cape Town research</a> published in September 2025 highlighted continuing inequalities, finding that small-scale indigenous growers were receiving disproportionately little from the industry’s commercial success. The culinary industry is also rediscovering these ingredients. Johannes Richter, chef and co-owner of The LivingRoom, the award-winning fine-dining restaurant at Summerhill Guest Estate near Durban, believes SA has barely begun exploring its indigenous culinary potential. “I honestly feel that we are only really seeing the tip of the iceberg and the beginning of what potential these ingredients hold,” he told IOL. “Sadly, so much knowledge has been lost over time, information that many people now no longer have access to, and the fact that little academic research has been conducted when it comes to our local ingredients. “All this in addition to the fact that African ingredients have always been seen as secondary to the imported crops that are used commercially in general, though I am happy to see this changing.” His enthusiasm reflects a wider opportunity, but restauranteurs and food manufacturers operate on very different economics. A chef can experiment with seasonal ingredients in small quantities, while industrial producers need consistent supply, predictable specifications and prices that leave room for profit. Rooibos has already demonstrated that SA can build a substantial international business around an indigenous ingredient. Baobab and marula have established commercial footholds, while sorghum and lesser-known crops are beginning to demonstrate what they can offer modern food production. The real test isn’t whether these ingredients can attract attention; they’ve already managed that. It’s whether the industry can turn them into products people buy regularly, at prices that work for manufacturers and reward the people growing and harvesting them. There’s gold in South Africa’s superfoods, but unless more of the value stays close to where the ingredients originate, the biggest profits could end up being made thousands of miles from the farms and communities that made the opportunity possible. La presión regulatoria y fiscal sobre bebidas alcohólicas avanzó en Polonia, Francia y Brasil, configurando un entorno de mayor costo regulatorio para la industria a nivel global.

Top 5 eventos del período

  • Coca-Cola HBC avanza en la adquisición de CCBA por USD 2.600 millones, la mayor operación de M&A de la semana en el sector de bebidas.
  • Molson Coors emitió un recall de Coors Light, Molson Canadian y Blue Moon en Canadá por contaminación con fragmentos metálicos, afectando tres de sus marcas masivas.
  • PepsiCo cerró un acuerdo de 10 años como proveedor exclusivo de bebidas en más de 700 hoteles Hyatt en América, asegurando volumen institucional a largo plazo.
  • Tetra Pak completó una inversión de €30,2 millones en su planta de La Rioja, Argentina, la señal de inversión en infraestructura local más relevante de la semana.
  • Total Wine reportó ventas de bebidas sin alcohol para adultos superiores a USD 75 millones con crecimiento del 125% entre FY2022 y FY2026, confirmando la consolidación estructural del segmento no alcohólico en retail.
Finanzas

Monster Beverage y Celsius Holdings concentraron el debate de valuación de la semana, con ventas récord en ambas compañías pero presión creciente en márgenes por el alza en costos de aluminio, que ya llevó a Monster a implementar aumentos de precios en EE.UU. Molson Coors enfrentó un recall de Coors Light, Molson Canadian y Blue Moon en Canadá por fragmentos metálicos, evento de reputación con potencial impacto financiero, mientras PepsiCo profundizó su reestructuración con 1.000 despidos en América del Norte en respuesta a la caída sostenida en volúmenes.

Marketing

PepsiCo lanzó etiquetas animadas como iniciativa de innovación en packaging y amplió el alcance de Doritos mediante alianzas con KFC y Subway, buscando diversificar puntos de contacto con el consumidor más allá del canal tradicional. El reporte de tendencias de Bacardi y la edición limitada de Johnnie Walker Blue Label para el Año del Chivo consolidaron la apuesta de las grandes licorerías por experiencias culturales y personalizadas como eje de diferenciación frente al avance de las bebidas sin alcohol.

Supply Chain

El alza en los costos de aluminio se convirtió en el principal driver de ajuste de precios de la semana, con Monster Beverage ejecutando aumentos y Celsius evaluando cambios para 2027, una señal que podría contagiarse a otras categorías de bebidas enlatadas en la región. En el plano regulatorio, Brasil anunció un impuesto a vinos importados desde febrero de 2027 de hasta 11,6%, Polonia firmó aumentos de accisas para el mismo año y Francia aprobó mayor carga fiscal sobre premixes, incrementando la presión sobre la cadena de costos en múltiples mercados.

Ventas / Comercial

Mercado Libre y Carrefour lanzaron entregas de supermercado en 45 minutos en Argentina, intensificando la competencia en el canal de entrega rápida donde Rappi proyecta quintuplicar su volumen en diez años, lo que redefine la ejecución en el último kilómetro para las marcas de consumo masivo. En Brasil, Black Friday desplazó al invierno como la mayor temporada de ventas de vino, mientras que en EE.UU. las ventas de cerveza moderaron su caída en noviembre temprano y los cócteles RTD crecieron 4,1%, señalando resiliencia en segmentos premium y de conveniencia.
