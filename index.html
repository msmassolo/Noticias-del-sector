Whisky’s next opportunity: From Highballs to RTDs

Las marcas de whisky están expandiendo su portafolio hacia formatos RTD (Ready-To-Drink) premium y combinaciones de sabores contemporáneos para atraer nuevos consumidores sin abandonar su posicionamiento tradicional. Esta estrategia busca capturar segmentos de bebedores más jóvenes y ocasionales que demandan conveniencia y innovación. El desafío central es mantener la autenticidad y herencia de la categoría mientras se moderniza la experiencia de consumo, abriendo oportunidades significativas en canales modernos y fuera de ocasiones de consumo clásicas.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
Few spirits are as steeped in heritage and tradition as whisky. But while its craftsmanship and character remain central to its appeal, brands are exploring lighter serves, contemporary cocktails and premium ready-to-drink (RTD) formats to reach new consumers. New serves and flavour combinations are creating opportunities to take whisky beyond its traditional associations with sipping neat or enjoying a classic cocktail. The challenge is finding the balance between attracting new drinkers and retaining the heritage, authenticity and flavour that make whisky distinctive. For some, the answer starts with rethinking the serve itself. For <a href="https://www.beveragedaily.com/Article/2026/10/21/why-drinks-brands-need-to-rethink-the-rules-of-drinking/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.beveragedaily.com/Article/2026/10/21/why-drinks-brands-need-to-rethink-the-rules-of-drinking/">Hamish Campbell</a>, executive and creative director at Denomination, the opportunity lies in challenging perceptions of when and how whisky should be enjoyed. “Whisky isn’t really my lunchtime drink. But why can’t it be? Why can’t we have a lighter, sparkling lemon serve, perhaps using a mid-proof whisky?” he says. Campbell points to Japanese whisky highballs as an example of how the category could explore this space. Their lighter profile offers a different experience to the heavier, smokier styles traditionally associated with Scotch, while Japanese craftsmanship and design help create a distinctive identity. “I think you could have a soda water and lemon serve, perhaps with a shorter pour of whisky and a little ginger, as a refreshing lunchtime drink,” he adds. Simon Freeth, global head of bars at ROKA, also sees potential in bringing Japanese highball culture to a wider audience. He points to Suntory’s successful revival of the whisky highball in the late 2000s and early 2010s, which helped position it as a refreshing alternative to beer and sake, particularly alongside food in Japanese izakayas. “A well-made whisky highball allows you to appreciate the individual characteristics of the whisky, even with the dilution and carbonation,” Freeth says. “It makes the spirit more approachable to someone discovering it for the first time, but can be equally enjoyable for an experienced whisky drinker.” He believes there is still room for the format to grow in the UK, adding: “The growing interest in cleaner, less-sweet flavours, particularly within premium soft drinks and functional beverages, alongside the growth of RTDs, could help make lighter whisky serves more familiar and accessible.” Food pairing offers another way to bring whisky into more casual social settings. For Freeth, the focus should be on finding new ways to enjoy the spirit rather than necessarily creating entirely new products. At ROKA Dubai, the group’s highball programme focuses on refreshing serves designed to complement its robata dishes, using house-made cordials, hydrosols and sodas inspired by Japanese ingredients and flavours. Freeth also points to the influence of tequila cocktails, particularly the Paloma, on whisky serves. Whisky, citrus and grapefruit soda have proved popular with ROKA’s guests, offering a familiar flavour combination that can introduce the spirit to consumers who might not typically drink it. “Some may continue enjoying whisky in cocktails, while others might become curious to explore the spirit further,” he says. “Both are opportunities for the category.” But attracting new consumers doesn’t mean compromising whisky’s identity. Freeth believes brands should put the drinking experience ahead of traditional associations with age, prestige and heritage. “Let someone discover whisky through a great cocktail or refreshing highball without feeling they need to understand everything about the spirit beforehand,” he says. “If they enjoy it, that can naturally lead to curiosity and a desire to explore further.” Storytelling still has an important role to play, particularly in hospitality, but should enhance the experience rather than dominate it. Sharing the right story at the right moment can help build a connection with consumers without making the category feel intimidating. The RTD category is providing another route for whisky brands to reach consumers, with retail figures suggesting growing interest in whisky-based mixed drinks. Waitrose reported a 73% increase in sales of whisky-based RTDs, while wider UK off-trade RTD sales grew 17% in value and 12% in volume in 2025, according to WSTA figures sourced from NielsenIQ. Recent launches reflect the breadth of innovation, from Teeling’s Phoenix Twist, a whiskey-based canned cocktail combining ginger, lime and yuzu, to Wee Smoky’s range of bartender-developed premium cocktails. Meanwhile, Whitebox Cocktails is expanding into full-strength canned serves, including a Manhattan and Whiskey Sour. The launches highlight two routes for the category: more approachable, flavour-led drinks and premium, spirit-forward cocktails. Ultimately, whisky’s next opportunity may not lie in reinventing the spirit, but in expanding how consumers experience it. By combining its established heritage with new flavours, formats and serves, brands could give more people a reason to discover whisky.

After Going Mainstream, Has Pumpkin Beer Become a Regional Specialty Again?

La cerveza de calabaza está experimentando una transición de tendencia mainstream a especialidad regional en EE.UU. Jack's Abby Brewing reportó récord de ventas en su festival Oktoberfest, con la cerveza de calabaza 'Pumpkin Crop' posicionándose en tercer lugar de ventas, compitiendo exitosamente contra cervezas estacionales tradicionales. Este movimiento refleja un cambio en preferencias de consumidores hacia bebidas premium y especializadas, relevante para estrategias de portafolio y estacionalización en mercados de bebidas alcohólicas.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
Earlier this month, Jack’s Abby Brewing in Framingham, Mass., cranked up the oom-pah tunes for Oktoberfest revelry featuring stein-hoisting contests, plump pretzels, and lots and lots of lager . “We set our all-time sales record for the beer hall,” says Sam Hendler, CEO of parent company Hendler Family Brewing. Unsurprisingly, golden Munich Fest and Copper Legend Oktoberfest lagers took the top draft sales slots. Third place welcomed an underdog: Pumpkin Crop, a spiced autumnal lager. “That’s different from 10 years ago when it was all German lagers at Oktoberfest,” Hendler says. Now people want to party with pumpkin beer . When it comes to drinkers losing their gourd, “Massachusetts is a pretty good bellwether for New England.” Pumpkin beer’s popularity in New England dates to Colonial America when resourceful brewers embraced pumpkins as a cheap and plentiful fermentable. During craft beer’s late-20th century rise, the region’s breweries rebooted pumpkin ale as a spiced fall seasonal that tasted like boozy pumpkin pie. “You can almost call it the original pastry beer,” says Phil Markowski, the brewmaster at Two Roads Brewing in Stratford, Conn. Pumpkin beer hit a rough patch in the mid-2010s when market saturation and summertime releases sparked a backlash. Many pumpkin beers disappeared, but the style held strong in New England, fall’s wonderland of pumpkin patches, apple orchards, and leaf-peeping. “These iconic seasonal moments create appetite and excitement for beers that echo the flavors and feelings of fall,” says Jon London, the head of beer at Boston Beer Company , which makes cinnamon-spiced Sam Adams Jack-O pumpkin ale. Instead of dying on the vine, pumpkin beer today thrives as a regional specialty with deep-rooted preferences for time-tested brands. Born in 1996, Shipyard Brewing ’s Pumpkinhead Ale still shines from its Portland, Maine headquarters down the East Coast. Across the country, Seattle’s Elysian Brewing first brewed the spiced Night Owl Pumpkin Ale in 1997, and the brand returns every fall. “We love traditions and are creatures of habit, aren’t we?” says Jeff Richards, the senior marketing director at Elysian. “Pumpkin beer is one part of how we share in the spirit of the season.” No region loves pumpkin beers more than New England. Over the past five years, Google Trends data show a concentration of interest in New England, with a pronounced spike this year. “Fall seasonal beers are more popular in New England than other areas of the country,” a Total Wine rep reported via email. Pumpkin beer is “built into the culture,” says Rob Day, the vice president of marketing for Barrel One Collective , which encompasses 15 mainly New England brands including Harpoon, Smuttynose, Wachusett, and Long Trail. “People look forward to these moments.” Barrel One sells seven different pumpkin beers and ciders, many with regional affinity. New Hampshire enjoys Smuttynose Pumpkin Ale , New Yorkers order Five Boroughs Pumpkin Ale , and Massachusetts drinkers crush Wachusett Pumpkin Ale . “In each state, there’s a different pumpkin product in our portfolio that’s outperforming,” Day says. Sales of UFO Pumpkin are up 18 percent in Rhode Island, while Harpoon Pumpkin Cider is up 9.3 percent in Connecticut and Clown Shoes Pumpkin Sombrero is up 28 percent in New Hampshire. “Fall seasonal beers are more popular in New England than other areas of the country.” Pumpkin beer drinkers aren’t monolithic. For Hendler Family’s Massachusetts brands, flavor and ABV preferences vary across customer bases. Night Shift ’s potent and strongly flavored Pumpkin Piescraper appeals to a younger, more urban crowd, Hendler shares, while Wormtown ’s mellower, lower-alcohol Fresh Patch hits the blue-collar, more suburban customer base. “The pumpkin beer phenomenon works across all demographics in Massachusetts,” Hendler says. In Massachusetts, this fall’s most popular pumpkin beer is Shipyard Pumpkinhead, according to Circana data. “That’s our biggest market,” says Bruce Forsley, Shipyard’s president. Now 30 years old, Pumpkinhead accounts for almost half of Shipyard’s annual production and remains steady. “Craft beer has been struggling the last few years, but this segment remains healthy and robust with no immediate signs of decline,” Forsley says. Shipyard is singularly focused on expanding and supporting the Pumpkinhead franchise. Smashed Pumpkin, the 9 percent ABV imperial pumpkin ale, continues to grow, and there’s also a version aged in bourbon barrels. This year, Shipyard also introduced an NA Pumpkinhead that sold out in three weeks. “We vastly underestimated the NA beer market,” says Forsley, who plans to boost production next year. Thriving pockets of fandom expand beyond the Northeast, too, in part fueled by legacy brands. Southern Tier Brewing in Lakewood, N.Y., has ardent fans of Pumking Imperial Ale (born 2007) in New York State and adjoining regions; and Dogfish Head in Milton, Del., has legions of mid-Atlantic fans for Punkin Ale, first brewed in 1994. Despite national interest seemingly waning, pumpkin beer sales are actually increasing in several surprising pockets. Shipyard ships Pumpkinhead Ale to 42 states. Georgia is growing, and “Florida is a very good market because New England transplants are down there,” Forsley says. The challenge with wide distribution is a shorter selling season in warm-weather states. Pumpkin ale can be a tough sell when it tops 100 degrees in Texas, so distributors want to sell the beer in two or three weeks. “There’s not a traditional fall season,” he says. “There’s just the thought that, This is the time of year when I should have this beer .” Heatwaves, though, aren’t a dealbreaker. Could be mid-August, or maybe late July. “It’s based on depletion.” “Craft beer has been struggling the last few years, but this segment remains healthy and robust with no immediate signs of decline.” Boston Beer typically releases its fall seasonals, including Jack-O, during the first week of August. “That’s when people start craving fall,” London says. In a competitive sales landscape, “we want to be first to market with the seasonals people are excited about.” Selling pumpkin beer earlier gives breweries a headstart on the style’s unofficial October 31 end date. “Even though pumpkin carries into Thanksgiving in such a big way, Halloween kind of ends pumpkin beer and you’re on your way out,” Day says. Encouraging customers to party maximizes the compressed sales season. Texto completo del artículo
Drinks businesses in England and Northern Ireland have less than a year to prepare for the introduction of a Deposit Return Scheme (DRS), which will bring new packaging, labelling and compliance requirements ahead of its October 2027 launch. Due to come into effect on 1 October 2027, the scheme will introduce a 20p refundable deposit on eligible drinks containers, which consumers can reclaim by returning empty packaging to designated collection points. It will cover single-use polyethylene terephthalate (PET) plastic bottles and steel and aluminium cans between 150ml and three litres. For drinks manufacturers, the changes will extend beyond the deposit itself, requiring preparations around packaging specifications, product identification, registration and producer fees. The scheme aims to increase recycling rates, reduce packaging litter and support a circular economy by making it easier to collect used drinks containers and recycle them into new packaging. Deposit return schemes have achieved high container return rates in other European markets. <a href="https://www.tomra.com/reverse-vending/media-center/feature-articles/deposit-return-schemes-europe" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.tomra.com/reverse-vending/media-center/feature-articles/deposit-return-schemes-europe">Germany has a reported return rate of 98%</a> for eligible single-use drinks containers, while Lithuania saw its rate rise from below 34% to 92% within two years of introducing its scheme in 2016. <a href="https://exchangeforchange.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://exchangeforchange.co.uk/">Exchange for Change</a> has published material specifications for PET plastic, aluminium and steel containers in England, Scotland and Northern Ireland. These set out design requirements intended to ensure packaging can be identified, accepted by reverse vending machines and processed efficiently. Wales will also include glass drinks containers in its scheme, with transitional arrangements planned between 1 October 2027 and 30 September 2031. Preparing for the scheme will require manufacturers to review their packaging, labelling and production processes. Eligible containers will need to meet the relevant material specifications and carry a DRS logo and registered barcode so they can be identified and processed through the collection system. This could mean updating packaging artwork and barcodes, adjusting production schedules and coordinating with packaging suppliers and co-packers to ensure compliant containers are available ahead of launch. Manufacturers will also need to consider existing packaging stocks and products moving through the supply chain. Producer fees will be set at zero for the first 15 months of the scheme, from October 2027 to December 2028. Exchange for Change has proposed fees of 0.6p per aluminium or steel container and 2.3p per PET container from January 2029 to December 2032. These rates are due to be reviewed and reconfirmed in May 2027. Although the initial fee period will reduce one element of the immediate financial burden, manufacturers will still need to account for the work involved in updating packaging and meeting compliance requirements. Producers must register with Exchange for Change and report information about the eligible containers they place on the market. Manufacturers should review their packaging portfolios against Exchange for Change’s material specifications, establish their registration requirements and identify the product information needed for registration and reporting. They should also assess changes to packaging artwork and production lines, and speak to suppliers and co-packers about lead times for compliant packaging. Businesses should monitor the latest transition guidance as well. Exchange for Change has outlined provisional arrangements under which finished drinks containers manufactured and filled before 1 October 2027 can continue to be sold after launch without DRS labelling or fees. However, arrangements for some unused, non-compliant packaging remain subject to regulatory agreement, making it important for manufacturers to follow further updates.
