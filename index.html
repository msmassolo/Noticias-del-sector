PepsiCo Is Proving the Market Right

Elliott Management, un fondo de inversión activista, adquirió una participación de $4 mil millones en PepsiCo hace aproximadamente un año, señalando un cambio significativo en la percepción del mercado sobre la compañía. Esta inversión es estratégicamente importante porque los fondos activistas típicamente buscan reestructurar negocios con bajo desempeño, indicando que PepsiCo enfrenta presiones competitivas o de rentabilidad que requieren intervención. El movimiento refleja expectativas de cambios operacionales o estratégicos en uno de los mayores actores globales de bebidas y snacks, con implicaciones para la dinámica competitiva del sector.

Texto completo del artículo
Just over a year ago, Elliott Management announced it had acquired a $4 billion stake in PepsiCo. That alone showed how the company’s reputation had changed. Elliott is an activist investor, whose strategy is to fix underperforming businesses. The fact that the firm would even consider PepsiCo a target would have been unthinkable even a few years earlier; as we wrote at the time, its stake showed the change in how PepsiCo was viewed by the market. In a presentation disclosing the investment, Elliott provided a more succinct, direct appraisal: PepsiCo stock was trading “more like KHC than KO”. This was not a compliment. Elliott’s point was that investors were valuing PepsiCo not in line with Coca-Cola, its core rival whose stock ticker is ‘KO’, but rather with Kraft Heinz (‘KHC’), “a structurally lower-growth” business that had been struggling for several years. Elliott argued that better execution and strategic changes, including a refranchising of its bottling operations, could improve that sentiment and PepsiCo’s profit margins. In response, PepsiCo stock would again be valued like Coca-Cola, leading shares (and the value of Elliott’s investment) higher. Unfortunately for Elliott, the market’s attitude toward PepsiCo has become even worse. At the time the presentation was released, PepsiCo traded at about 18 times expected earnings per share over the next four quarters, a useful (if not always perfect) metric for judging market expectations for future growth. Coca-Cola stock was at 22 times — even that gap between the two multiples was the largest since the late 1990s — and Kraft Heinz barely 10 times. Elliott’s statement, in terms of the pure numbers, contained some hyperbole. That’s no longer the case. With PepsiCo stock just off a six-year low (and Elliott down over $400 million on its investment in thirteen months), PEP looks far more like KHC than KO. Coca-Cola now trades at 26x earnings, and PepsiCo just 15x, with Kraft Heinz at over 11x. It’s a stunning shift after more than two decades during which the market, in terms of valuation, treated PepsiCo and Coca-Cola as essentially equals, and as two of the best companies in the entire food and beverage space. But the reason that shift has accelerated, rather than reversed is because PepsiCo indeed now looks much more like Kraft Heinz than Coca-Cola. That’s true from a high level. After fiscal third quarter earnings last week, PepsiCo lowered its annual outlook for constant-currency adjusted earnings per share growth to just 1 percent to 2 percent. That’s the kind of growth challenged packaged food companies have often posted in recent years; meanwhile, after its second quarter report, Coca-Cola raised its guidance on the same basis to growth of 7 to 8 percent. But where the comparison to the likes of Kraft Heinz and other packaged food plays stands out is in the company’s challenges and its strategy. PepsiCo now looks much like packaged food companies have as they struggled to respond to a changing consumer environment. Those companies saw consumer tastes pivot away from major, corporate, legacy brands to more local and differentiated options. PepsiCo’s problems are slightly different, with worries focusing on a financially stretched consumer, but also clear pressures from the rise of GLP-1 drugs and the related snacking consumer shift toward protein over carbs. Despite those differences, PepsiCo seems to be running a similar playbook. A year ago, in an earnings release chief executive officer Ramon Laguarta promised a greater pace of innovation alongside cost cuts which in turn would fund investments in the business, particularly around price. This is the exact same strategy packaged food companies like Kraft Heinz and General Mills have offered (indeed, “innovation” has been a constant buzzword across the space for years). Like peers before it, PepsiCo also looked to acquisitions to move more quickly and aggressively into seemingly hot categories. And like so many of those peers , Pepsi’s acquisitions don’t seem to be working out.. It was clear last quarter that there were real signs of concern in Poppi , the probiotic soda company acquired last May. On the conference call with analysts following last week’s earnings report, Laguarta said Poppi needed more investment – a surprise and a disappointment given the company’s growth rates before the acquisition. (The CEO had previously said the brand simply needed to work through the transition to Pepsi’s distribution system.) The company has more quietly signaled trouble as well. The Poppi acquisition included a $300 million ‘earnout’ payment, due the former owners if the brand hit certain financial targets. Accounting rules require that PepsiCo book a liability for the payment, based on the likelihood it will be paid (with a slight discount applied based on the time elapsed before it is paid). In just three quarters, that liability has gone from $278 million three quarters ago to just $12 million. Clearly, so far this year, Poppi’s performance has weakened markedly. Mexican food producer Siete Foods , acquired in late 2024, for over $1.2 billion, doesn’t appear to be a standout either. Laguarta said last quarter the business was doing well, except “for some issues with some of the ingredients”. This quarter, Laguarta gave no commentary at all. That itself is notable: executives are keen to trumpet any success from billion-dollar deals. Meanwhile, the strategy for the legacy business sounds like mostly a bigger version of what was promised twelve months ago: pricing changes, new products, more cost cuts. In that way Laguarta again sounds like many of his CEO peers in recent years, arguing that the problem is execution rather than structural changes in the market. Most of those arguments have been proven wrong: “doing things better” is not a successful plan when more aggressive, permanent strategies need to be implemented. (Under new CEO Steve Cahillane, even Kraft Heinz seems to be figuring this out.) Investors – no doubt at this point including Elliott itself – don’t think that’s enough. Instead, the obvious fear is that for PepsiCo, many of its brands and products simply aren’t as valuable in a changing consumer market. That in turn means they’re not strong enough to consistently take pricing. Historically, that has meant, eventually, that profit margins and earnings don’t just flatten, but start to fall. This has been the case for Kraft Heinz, which generated $2.93 in earnings per share in 2021 and will likely be barely over $2 this year. For PepsiCo, the news isn’t that bad – yet. The international business is still holding up, and there are ways to improve the performance of Poppi and Siete, adapt the snacking portfolio, and get the North American beverages back on track. But there’s a reason its valuation relative to earnings now is much closer to that of a declining Kraft Heinz than the consistently growing Coca-Cola. Investors believe PepsiCo might be the next major food and beverage company to see its profits start to erode. It’s not hard to see why: at this point, PepsiCo looks and sounds like many of those companies. Champagne in a can: Can RTD attract new consumers?

Tavern lanza Cannes Royale, línea de cócteles de champagne en latas de 222ml, testeada en el Festival de Cannes Lions. El formato RTD (ready-to-drink) en single-serve busca capturar nuevas ocasiones de consumo y audiencias, aprovechando la portabilidad y conveniencia. Esta estrategia refleja la tendencia global de premiumización del segmento de bebidas listas para beber, desafiando el formato tradicional de champagne y expandiendo oportunidades en celebraciones informales y eventos sociales.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
Champagne may be synonymous with celebration, but could the category find new drinking occasions in a can? Tavern is exploring this through Cannes Royale, a range of five Champagne cocktails developed for the Cannes Lions International Festival of Creativity. Packaged in 222ml cans, the drinks were designed for the festival’s social setting, allowing attendees to enjoy a single serve while moving between events, talks and parties. The format proved popular at the event, with Tavern reporting that all 15 cases sold out on the first day. While the launch was designed specifically for Cannes rather than as a strategy to recruit new Champagne consumers, it raises a wider question for suppliers: could ready-to-drink (RTD) formats bring Champagne into new occasions, or will they primarily appeal to consumers already familiar with the category? Tavern’s launch comes against a mixed backdrop for the Champagne industry. According to <a href="https://www.champagne.fr/en/find-out-more/mediaroom/champagne-press/champagne-2025-shipment-review-and-outlook" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.champagne.fr/en/find-out-more/mediaroom/champagne-press/champagne-2025-shipment-review-and-outlook">Comité Champagne</a>, global shipments fell by around 2% in 2025 to 266 million bottles, down from 271.4 million in 2024. In the UK, shipments increased by 1.9% to 22.7 million bottles, while their value fell by 5.9% to €488.1m. Although the figures do not establish why UK shipment value declined, they point to contrasting trends in volume and value within the market. Cannes Royale was developed through Tavern Labs, the company’s internal brand development arm, specifically for the Cannes Lions festival. The team wanted to create something that felt distinctly French and suited to the South of France, choosing Champagne over rosé because of its celebratory associations. The 222ml can was selected as a single-cocktail serve suited to daytime events, talks and parties, allowing attendees to enjoy a premium drink without opening a full bottle. The wider RTD market offers some context for the potential opportunity. According to <a href="https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/analysis/2026/unlocking-the-rtd-opportunity/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/analysis/2026/unlocking-the-rtd-opportunity/">NielsenIQ (NIQ)</a>, RTDs account for just 0.9% of long alcoholic drinks and spirits spending in Britain, compared with 14% in Australia. NIQ estimates that increasing Britain’s share to 3.5% would generate an additional £645m in sales. The figures suggest there is room for the category to develop in Britain, particularly in hospitality venues, where RTDs remain underrepresented. NIQ’s BrandTrack research shows that RTD consumers skew towards younger adults, with 69% aged 18 to 34. Almost two-thirds (65%) drink in pubs, bars and restaurants weekly, spending an average of £154 per month in the on-trade, £39 more than the average consumer. This audience could present opportunities for suppliers developing products around different drinking occasions. Research from <a href="https://www.theiwsr.com/insight/dynamic-rtds-a-beacon-of-hope-for-beverage-alcohol/?entry=9231918" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.theiwsr.com/insight/dynamic-rtds-a-beacon-of-hope-for-beverage-alcohol/?entry=9231918">IWSR</a> also points to continued growth in the wider category. Across 10 major markets, RTD volumes increased by 2% in 2024, while value rose by 4%. RTDs accounted for 3.5% of total beverage alcohol servings in those markets, up from 1.1% in 2014. For Champagne suppliers, these figures provide a reason to explore new formats, but they do not guarantee that premium Champagne cocktails will generate sustained demand. Adapting Champagne for a ready-to-drink format also raises questions about how suppliers can maintain the quality and premium associations attached to the category. French appellation rules require Champagne to undergo its second fermentation in the bottle, meaning it cannot simply be packaged as straight Champagne in a can. Using Champagne as an ingredient in cocktails offers one way to bring the protected wine into a different format. Cannes Royale takes this approach, combining Champagne with French liqueurs and for the brand, ensuring the finished product lived up to its premium positioning was central to development. “It couldn’t just be a fun can with Champagne in it. The ingredients needed to feel genuinely French, the flavours needed to work together, and the finished drink had to feel premium,” says Mike Perry, founder and chief creative officer of Tavern in New York. “Something can sound incredibly French on paper but not necessarily taste good together.” The approach reflects the challenge for suppliers seeking to combine convenience with premium positioning. While format and branding may attract attention, the liquid itself must deliver on consumers’ expectations. While RTDs continue to gain share of beverage alcohol consumption, attracting new consumers is becoming more challenging as the category matures. According to IWSR’s RTDs Strategic Study 2025, just 5% of RTD drinkers across its 10 key markets were new to the category in 2024. Instead, growth was increasingly driven by existing consumers drinking more frequently, particularly younger drinkers. For Champagne cocktails, this raises the question of whether a new format can bring additional consumers into the category or simply offer existing drinkers another way to enjoy it. Perry says making Champagne more accessible was not the original objective behind Cannes Royale. “We weren’t setting out to solve a Champagne accessibility problem. We were trying to create a product that felt completely appropriate to Cannes and the South of France, but in a format people hadn’t seen before. That said, the end result naturally does remove some of the ceremony and commitment of opening a full bottle,” he says. Tavern reports that all 15 cases brought to the Cannes activation sold out on the first day, with attendees returning for additional serves and asking where they could buy the product. While this suggests the concept resonated with the event’s audience, it does not establish whether demand would continue beyond Cannes or attract consumers who would not otherwise purchase Champagne. For suppliers, the commercial opportunity will depend on whether such formats can move beyond event-led activations to generate repeat purchases and incremental demand, while retaining the quality and premium appeal associated with Champagne.

Meanwhile, the strategy for the legacy business sounds like mostly a bigger version of what was promised twelve months ago: pricing changes, new products, more cost cuts. In that way Laguarta again sounds like many of his CEO peers in recent years, arguing that the problem is execution rather than structural changes in the market. Most of those arguments have been proven wrong: “doing things better” is not a successful plan when more aggressive, permanent strategies need to be implemented. (Under new CEO Steve Cahillane, even Kraft Heinz seems to be figuring this out.) Investors – no doubt at this point including Elliott itself – don’t think that’s enough. Instead, the obvious fear is that for PepsiCo, many of its brands and products simply aren’t as valuable in a changing consumer market. That in turn means they’re not strong enough to consistently take pricing. Historically, that has meant, eventually, that profit margins and earnings don’t just flatten, but start to fall. This has been the case for Kraft Heinz, which generated $2.93 in earnings per share in 2021 and will likely be barely over $2 this year. For PepsiCo, the news isn’t that bad – yet. The international business is still holding up, and there are ways to improve the performance of Poppi and Siete, adapt the snacking portfolio, and get the North American beverages back on track. But there’s a reason its valuation relative to earnings now is much closer to that of a declining Kraft Heinz than the consistently growing Coca-Cola. Investors believe PepsiCo might be the next major food and beverage company to see its profits start to erode. It’s not hard to see why: at this point, PepsiCo looks and sounds like many of those companies.
