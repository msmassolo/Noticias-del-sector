PepsiCo Is Proving the Market Right

Elliott Management adquirió una posición de $4 mil millones en PepsiCo hace poco más de un año, señalando un cambio estratégico significativo en cómo el mercado percibe a la compañía. El interés de un inversor activista en PepsiCo refleja posibles oportunidades de optimización operativa y de valor que antes no eran consideradas. Este movimiento es relevante para la industria de bebidas de consumo masivo ya que señala presiones del mercado sobre la rentabilidad y eficiencia de los grandes players, con potenciales implicancias en estrategia, restructuración y alineación de portafolios de bebidas globales.

Texto completo del artículo
Just over a year ago, Elliott Management announced it had acquired a $4 billion stake in PepsiCo. That alone showed how the company’s reputation had changed. Elliott is an activist investor, whose strategy is to fix underperforming businesses. The fact that the firm would even consider PepsiCo a target would have been unthinkable even a few years earlier; as we wrote at the time, its stake showed the change in how PepsiCo was viewed by the market. In a presentation disclosing the investment, Elliott provided a more succinct, direct appraisal: PepsiCo stock was trading “more like KHC than KO”. This was not a compliment. Elliott’s point was that investors were valuing PepsiCo not in line with Coca-Cola, its core rival whose stock ticker is ‘KO’, but rather with Kraft Heinz (‘KHC’), “a structurally lower-growth” business that had been struggling for several years. Elliott argued that better execution and strategic changes, including a refranchising of its bottling operations, could improve that sentiment and PepsiCo’s profit margins. In response, PepsiCo stock would again be valued like Coca-Cola, leading shares (and the value of Elliott’s investment) higher. Unfortunately for Elliott, the market’s attitude toward PepsiCo has become even worse. At the time the presentation was released, PepsiCo traded at about 18 times expected earnings per share over the next four quarters, a useful (if not always perfect) metric for judging market expectations for future growth. Coca-Cola stock was at 22 times — even that gap between the two multiples was the largest since the late 1990s — and Kraft Heinz barely 10 times. Elliott’s statement, in terms of the pure numbers, contained some hyperbole. That’s no longer the case. With PepsiCo stock just off a six-year low (and Elliott down over $400 million on its investment in thirteen months), PEP looks far more like KHC than KO. Coca-Cola now trades at 26x earnings, and PepsiCo just 15x, with Kraft Heinz at over 11x. It’s a stunning shift after more than two decades during which the market, in terms of valuation, treated PepsiCo and Coca-Cola as essentially equals, and as two of the best companies in the entire food and beverage space. But the reason that shift has accelerated, rather than reversed is because PepsiCo indeed now looks much more like Kraft Heinz than Coca-Cola. That’s true from a high level. After fiscal third quarter earnings last week, PepsiCo lowered its annual outlook for constant-currency adjusted earnings per share growth to just 1 percent to 2 percent. That’s the kind of growth challenged packaged food companies have often posted in recent years; meanwhile, after its second quarter report, Coca-Cola raised its guidance on the same basis to growth of 7 to 8 percent. But where the comparison to the likes of Kraft Heinz and other packaged food plays stands out is in the company’s challenges and its strategy. PepsiCo now looks much like packaged food companies have as they struggled to respond to a changing consumer environment. Those companies saw consumer tastes pivot away from major, corporate, legacy brands to more local and differentiated options. PepsiCo’s problems are slightly different, with worries focusing on a financially stretched consumer, but also clear pressures from the rise of GLP-1 drugs and the related snacking consumer shift toward protein over carbs. Despite those differences, PepsiCo seems to be running a similar playbook. A year ago, in an earnings release chief executive officer Ramon Laguarta promised a greater pace of innovation alongside cost cuts which in turn would fund investments in the business, particularly around price. This is the exact same strategy packaged food companies like Kraft Heinz and General Mills have offered (indeed, “innovation” has been a constant buzzword across the space for years). Like peers before it, PepsiCo also looked to acquisitions to move more quickly and aggressively into seemingly hot categories. And like so many of those peers , Pepsi’s acquisitions don’t seem to be working out.. It was clear last quarter that there were real signs of concern in Poppi , the probiotic soda company acquired last May. On the conference call with analysts following last week’s earnings report, Laguarta said Poppi needed more investment – a surprise and a disappointment given the company’s growth rates before the acquisition. (The CEO had previously said the brand simply needed to work through the transition to Pepsi’s distribution system.) The company has more quietly signaled trouble as well. The Poppi acquisition included a $300 million ‘earnout’ payment, due the former owners if the brand hit certain financial targets. Accounting rules require that PepsiCo book a liability for the payment, based on the likelihood it will be paid (with a slight discount applied based on the time elapsed before it is paid). In just three quarters, that liability has gone from $278 million three quarters ago to just $12 million. Clearly, so far this year, Poppi’s performance has weakened markedly. Mexican food producer Siete Foods , acquired in late 2024, for over $1.2 billion, doesn’t appear to be a standout either. Laguarta said last quarter the business was doing well, except “for some issues with some of the ingredients”. This quarter, Laguarta gave no commentary at all. That itself is notable: executives are keen to trumpet any success from billion-dollar deals. Meanwhile, the strategy for the legacy business sounds like mostly a bigger version of what was promised twelve months ago: pricing changes, new products, more cost cuts. In that way Laguarta again sounds like many of his CEO peers in recent years, arguing that the problem is execution rather than structural changes in the market. Most of those arguments have been proven wrong: “doing things better” is not a successful plan when more aggressive, permanent strategies need to be implemented. (Under new CEO Steve Cahillane, even Kraft Heinz seems to be figuring this out.) Investors – no doubt at this point including Elliott itself – don’t think that’s enough. Instead, the obvious fear is that for PepsiCo, many of its brands and products simply aren’t as valuable in a changing consumer market. That in turn means they’re not strong enough to consistently take pricing. Historically, that has meant, eventually, that profit margins and earnings don’t just flatten, but start to fall. This has been the case for Kraft Heinz, which generated $2.93 in earnings per share in 2021 and will likely be barely over $2 this year. For PepsiCo, the news isn’t that bad – yet. The international business is still holding up, and there are ways to improve the performance of Poppi and Siete, adapt the snacking portfolio, and get the North American beverages back on track. But there’s a reason its valuation relative to earnings now is much closer to that of a declining Kraft Heinz than the consistently growing Coca-Cola. Investors believe PepsiCo might be the next major food and beverage company to see its profits start to erode. It’s not hard to see why: at this point, PepsiCo looks and sounds like many of those companies.
