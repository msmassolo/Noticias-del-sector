El impuesto no detiene a la Coca-Cola: Arca Continental vende más refrescos que agua en 2026

Arca Continental, segundo embotellador de Coca-Cola en México, incrementó ventas de bebidas azucaradas un 4.2% en volumen durante 2026 a pesar del aumento del impuesto IEPS. La estrategia comercial logró mantener crecimiento en colas y refrescos mientras la categoría de agua (sin gravamen) perdió participación. El dato revela capacidad de traslación de costos fiscales y posicionamiento de marca en un entorno regulatorio restrictivo, con implicancias directas para la estrategia de portafolio y pricing en mercados con presión fiscal similar.

Texto completo del artículo
La estrategia comercial de Arca Continental le ha permitido mantener el crecimiento de sus bebidas azucaradas pese al aumento del Impuesto Especial sobre Producción y Servicios (IEPS) que entró en vigor este año. El segundo mayor embotellador de Coca-Cola en México incrementó sus ventas de colas y refrescos, mientras que el agua, una categoría que no está sujeta a este gravamen, perdió terreno.Durante el periodo reportado, los volúmenes consolidados de colas de Arca Continental crecieron 4.2%, mientras que los de refrescos avanzaron 7%. En contraste, las ventas de agua retrocedieron 0.8%. El comportamiento de estas categorías contribuyó a que el volumen total de bebidas de la compañía aumentara 4.6%.Los resultados muestran que el incremento de los impuestos a las bebidas con azúcar no se ha traducido, hasta ahora, en una contracción generalizada de su consumo. Desde el 1 de enero, el IEPS estableció una cuota de 3.08 pesos por litro para las bebidas con azúcares añadidos y de 1.50 pesos para aquellas que contienen edulcorantes sin calorías.En lugar de trasladar el impacto fiscal a una pérdida generalizada de volumen, Arca Continental ha recurrido a ajustes en su estrategia comercial, particularmente en la combinación de presentaciones y precios para mantener sus productos al alcance de los consumidores.“Los formatos retornables y personales incrementaron en mezcla, funcionando como palancas comerciales dentro de una arquitectura de precio-empaque diseñada para favorecer la asequibilidad y respaldar la rentabilidad del negocio”, señaló la compañía en su reporte financiero.Esta estrategia también se reflejó en sus resultados económicos. Las ventas netas de Arca Continental sumaron 66,613 millones de pesos, un crecimiento de 5.9% frente al mismo periodo del año anterior. Su flujo operativo (Ebitda) alcanzó 13,625 millones de pesos, 6.2% más, mientras que la utilidad neta retrocedió 4.8%, a 5,054 millones de pesos.“Estos resultados reflejan la capacidad de nuestro modelo de negocio para adaptarse con velocidad y flexibilidad a las condiciones de cada mercado, fortaleciendo la ejecución comercial y operativa, al tiempo que mantenemos una gestión financiera prudente”, señaló Arturo Gutiérrez, director general de Arca Continental.México muestra un comportamiento distinto por categoríaEn el mercado mexicano, el desempeño fue más desigual. Los volúmenes de colas crecieron 1.4%, pero los refrescos de sabores registraron una contracción de 1.3%. El agua tuvo el retroceso más pronunciado, con una caída de 12.3%, muy por encima de la disminución de 0.8% observada en el consolidado de la compañía.La categoría de bebidas no carbonatadas, por su parte, avanzó 5%, impulsada por segmentos como las bebidas energéticas, el té y los sueros. Este comportamiento muestra que el crecimiento de la compañía no depende exclusivamente de las bebidas carbonatadas, aunque las colas mantienen un papel relevante en su desempeño comercial.En términos financieros, las ventas netas de Arca Continental en México alcanzaron 31,278 millones de pesos, un incremento de 3.7%. El flujo operativo llegó a 7,735 millones de pesos, lo que representó un crecimiento de 7.5%.En Estados Unidos, otro de sus mercados relevantes, la compañía registró una tendencia similar a la observada en México, aunque con diferencias en la magnitud de los movimientos. Los volúmenes de colas aumentaron 2.9% y los refrescos de sabores avanzaron 0.6%, mientras que el agua retrocedió 10.8%.

BrauBeviale 2026, running from November 10 to 12, brings together beer, mineral water, soft drinks, juice, wine, sparkling wine and spirits makers, alongside the ingredients, processing and packaging technologies supporting them. But perhaps the clearest trend at this year’s show is drinks without alcohol. Non-alcoholic beer continues to command attention in western markets, but the opportunity now stretches far beyond traditional alcohol alternatives. BrauBeviale’s conference programme and exhibitors, for example, put the likes of RTD mocktails, non-alcoholic wines and other emerging drinks formats in the spotlight as manufacturers look to build broader portfolios for changing drinking occasions. That evolution is also visible in beer itself. The show looks beyond simply removing alcohol, asking where brewers can take the category next, while a dedicated session on dispensing non-alcoholic beer points to the practical considerations emerging as the category matures. Functionality is another theme moving rapidly into the mainstream. Functional beverages, shots, bitters and kombucha brands feature among the many other drinks at the show, reflecting the increasingly blurred lines between refreshment and products designed around wider wellness needs. A dedicated Discovery Bar tasting brings those categories together, giving visitors the chance to explore emerging beverage concepts alongside more established categories. And innovation is spreading into unexpected parts of the drinks market. Malt-based soft drinks feature on the conference agenda, illustrating how producers are exploring ingredients and processes traditionally associated with brewing to create products outside conventional beer occasions. Then there is sugar. Reducing sugar without sacrificing the experience consumers expect remains a key formulation challenge, with the programme exploring routes towards lower-sugar beverages alongside wider innovation in ingredients and production. But some of the biggest changes are happening behind the bottle. AI, automation, energy efficiency and smarter production form a substantial part of this year’s conversation. Conference sessions will examine how robotics and AI could help reduce operating and energy costs, how manufacturers can extract greater value from brewery data, and how electrification could reshape heat-intensive beverage production. The Brewery 2030+ Forum will push that thinking further, asking what tomorrow’s brewery should look like, while discussions around energy and resource efficiency bring sustainability firmly into the production conversation. Together, these trends point towards a beverage industry becoming more flexible in what it produces, more adventurous in what it puts into the bottle, and much smarter about how it makes it. For the beverage industry, that means plenty to watch: from the continuing evolution of no-and-low and functional drinks, all the way to the technologies promising to change how beverages are formulated, processed and produced.

So much has its popularity grown that it’s been hailed as a potential third pillar of soft drinks, alongside its established counterparts: full-calorie varieties and diet soda options. Financial incentives, rebates and price protection all come into consideration, alongside investment in dispensing equipment, maintenance, marketing support and service guarantees to ensure products remain available. “Often the beverage company supplies the equipment, and that can be tens of millions of dollars depending on the chain,” he adds. For suppliers, the value extends beyond securing a restaurant account. Partnerships offer opportunities to increase brand visibility, encourage product trial and build consumer loyalty outside the foodservice channel. Harms points to Dr Pepper as an example. Around 20 years ago, he says, the brand was considerably smaller, with a stronger regional presence in Texas and Oklahoma. Expanding its distribution into restaurant and foodservice accounts helped raise its profile beyond those markets. “By gaining visibility in these channels, it would grow the brand outside of the channel overall, and we’ve seen that play out,” he says. Restaurants also give beverage companies an opportunity to associate their products with everyday experiences, from eating out to visiting a theme park or cinema. They can introduce consumers to products they might not otherwise try, too. KFC is among several accounts to have changed hands in recent years. Harms points to Costco, Culver’s, Arby’s and Panda Express as examples of restaurants and retailers that have switched from PepsiCo to Coca-Cola. But the competition runs both ways. In 2024, PepsiCo won Subway back from Coca-Cola after nearly 21 years, demonstrating that even the longest-standing partnerships are not guaranteed. “There’s no beverage deal that’s safe. There’s no beverage deal that’s off limits for the competitor to come in and try to win it over,” Harms says. The two companies also have different strategies for winning partnerships. One advantage for PepsiCo is its wider portfolio of snack brands, which allows it to offer restaurant partners opportunities beyond beverages. Harms points to the company’s relationship with Taco Bell, recently renewed for another 10 years, as an example. The partnership includes Doritos-branded tacos, bringing PepsiCo’s Frito-Lay portfolio into the deal. “Pepsi does like to bring a lot of their snack brands to play. So it’s not just a beverage partnership, but also how can they bring pieces to it,” he says. The companies also differ in their bottling structures. While Coca-Cola has sold much of its bottling operations to independent companies, PepsiCo retains the majority of its bottling network. Harms says this can give PepsiCo an advantage in coordinating its packaged drinks across different territories. As restaurant beverage menus become more diverse, future pouring rights deals may be shaped less by flagship cola brands and more by suppliers’ ability to offer the right products across different categories. Harms observes that consumers are generally less likely to order a soda alongside a burger, taco or pizza than they were 20 years ago. As a result, suppliers are looking at ways to help restaurant partners drive more beverage purchases through investment in functional drinks, energy and other emerging categories. “There’s no one-size-fits-all anymore, so the beverage companies are having to be very nimble, very innovative,” Harms says. He points to McDonald’s partnership with Red Bull for energy drinks, despite its longstanding relationship with Coca-Cola, as an example of restaurants looking beyond their traditional supplier to meet demand in specific categories. However, he believes major suppliers retain an advantage through their scale, distribution networks, pricing and marketing capabilities. Coca-Cola, PepsiCo and Keurig Dr Pepper are all investing in functional and innovative beverages, with energy and functional drinks likely to play an increasingly important role in quick-service and fast-casual restaurants. Ultimately, Harms believes innovation will be central to winning future partnerships. “The next beverage agreements, pouring rights partnerships, will not be about red versus blue. It’ll be about which company is able to innovate.” Despite changing consumer preferences, Harms does not believe traditional soft drinks are going anywhere. He expects cola to remain a staple for decades to come, even as consumers increasingly look for different things from their beverages. “I think you’re seeing a bifurcation of what people want from their beverages. They want function, or they want indulgence,” he explains. Products such as zero-sugar and diet colas allow established brands to respond to changing preferences while retaining the familiar taste of cola. Harms points to Diet Coke’s continued growth since 2017 as an example of the category’s resilience. “Even Diet Coke has really been growing year after year,” he says, adding that zero-sugar products combine the functional benefits consumers are looking for with the indulgence of enjoying a Coca-Cola. For Harms, the future is not about choosing between established soft drinks and new innovations, but having a portfolio that caters to both. “I think the winning beverage company of the future is going to be focused on all of these things,” he says.
