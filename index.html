From Michelin-starred kitchens to the beverage aisle, award-winning chef Robert Sandberg is bringing flavor back. Made with 50% real fruit juice and no added sugar, Pulped is now available nationwide on Amazon. LOS ANGELES (October 30, 2026) - In a world where every drink promises to fix your life, flavor seems to have been forgotten. Enter Pulped, a new chef-founded sparkling juice brand challenging the beverage industry's obsession with function. Created by award-winning Swedish chef Robert Sandberg alongside co-founders Jennifer Sunnfors, former CMO at Mela Watermelon Water, and Oskar Bakke, award-winning photographer. Pulped puts great ingredients and exceptional flavor ahead of wellness claims. The idea came from a simple frustration. After trying countless drinks on the market, the founders struggled to find natural options they genuinely enjoyed. Everything seemed to be loaded with sugar, sweetened with stevia or artificial alternatives, or marketed around the latest wellness trend. Sandberg has spent his culinary career in some of the world's most celebrated restaurants, including Noma, Frantzén and Kong Hans Kælder. Now he's bringing that same obsession with flavor and balance to something considerably more accessible, a can of sparkling juice. "Nobody needs another drink promising to fix their life," says Sandberg. "I built Pulped the way I build a dish: start with great fruit, find the right botanical, add a pinch of salt so it all pops." Made with 50% real squeezed fruit juice, subtle botanicals and sea salt, Pulped contains no added sugar, stevia or artificial sweeteners. Its sweetness comes entirely from the fruit. Pulped comes in three flavors, each pairing real squeezed fruit with a botanical the way a chef pairs a sauce with a plate: Sandberg is joined by co-founders Jennifer Sunnfors, former CMO of Mela Watermelon Water and an early team member at Barebells and NOCCO, and Oskar Bakke, an internationally recognized photographer and creative director who leads the brand's visual identity and storytelling. "We're going back to basics," said Jennifer Sunnfors, co-founder of Pulped. "With Pulped we wanted to compete on taste: a drink made for flavor first, with an ingredient list you can actually understand. No added sugar, no weird aftertaste." Pulped is quickly gaining momentum across Los Angeles, with a growing presence in independent cafés, restaurants and specialty retailers, including Laurel Supply, Bob's Market, Bread & Butter, The Hive, Goodboybob, Bluey's and Sweet Butter Kitchen. The brand is also available nationwide on Amazon, with single-flavor packs and a variety pack. Esta regulación impacta directamente la estrategia de packaging y posicionamiento de marcas de bebidas en el mercado europeo, afectando tanto a productores locales como a exportadores que comercialicen en la UE, con implicancias en diferenciación competitiva y compliance normativo.

Texto completo del artículo
New restrictions on environmental marketing claims are putting terms like “natural” and “eco-friendly” under the microscope, prompting questions about what wineries can and can’t include on bottle labels Wine producers selling in the European Union face new restrictions on how they communicate environmental claims, from sustainability labels to marketing language. Under Directive (EU) 2024/825 , vague claims such as “ clean ,” “green,” and “ eco-friendly ,” among others, are subject to stricter requirements, forcing producers to reassess how they describe their products. With the September 27 enforcement of Directive 2024/825, shoppers browsing European wine shelves should no longer have to guess or Google what terms like “sustainable” or “green” mean as they relate to their wine. The directive, also called Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition (EmpCo), was originally introduced for all consumer products in 2024 as an amendment to two existing directives, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and the Consumer Rights Directive . However, many producers who could be affected by the directive may not know it exists. “My general view is that most people haven’t even really got an awareness that this regulation is there, much less what it implies for them,” says Peter Stanbury, the research director of the Sustainable Wine Roundtable (SWR), an independent sustainability advocacy organization based in London. “There’s a big knowledge gap out there at the moment.” Don’t miss the latest drinks industry news and insights. Sign up for our award-winning newsletters and get insider intel, resources, and trends delivered to your inbox every week. ​The newly prohibited acts include irrelevant claims, such as advertising a product as gluten-free when no product of that kind contains gluten, generic terms such as “eco-friendly” without clearly indicating what that means, and sustainability badges that aren’t backed by third-party certification, among other things. A 2020 study estimated that more than 50 percent of environmental claims in the EU were vague or misleading. Producers won’t need to overhaul production under the new directive. Instead, they can target and remove misleading information that may appear on the bottle label. “The directive is primarily focused on consumer communication,” says Ignacio Sánchez Recarte, Ph.D., the secretary general of the European Committee of Wine Companies (CEEV) . “Operators will mainly need to review their marketing, labeling, and environmental claims and ensure that these are clear, specific, and properly substantiated.” “One thorny compliance area is old stock, which is not grandfathered in,” says Michael R. Littenberg, head of environmental, social, and governance at global law firm Ropes & Gray LLP . “Impacted companies are using stickers or additional point of sale information.” How violations will be enforced is up to individual EU nations, but widespread violations across multiple EU countries could be subject to fines of up to four percent of annual turnover. ​“You could end up with effectively 27 member states having different frameworks, country by country,” says Stanbury. European Union directives give each member state the end goal, and each nation must then decide how to implement the law and penalties. Some countries may lean on regulators to enforce, while others, like Germany, for example, rely on civil action from business associations or environmental groups. The greater challenge is that, along with banning vague sustainability terms on wine labels, those terms also can’t be expressed in any consumer-facing communication—whether in marketing materials or in tableside conversations with sommeliers, for example. ​As the sole retail wine and liquor chain in Sweden’s government-run alcohol monopoly, Systembolaget sees nearly every type of wine pass through its inventory. “The principles behind Directive 2024/825 are consistent with the approach we have taken for several years when assessing sustainability claims and certifications,” says Emma Östansjö, a Systembolaget sustainability specialist. “We have a dedicated unit that reviews claims on products to ensure they comply with relevant legislation and are not misleading to consumers.” ​In practice, this means substantiating a claim with something concrete. Third-party certifications like Demeter are one route, with verifiable practices (such as “hand-harvested” or “dry-farmed”) or quantifiable data (“25 percent less carbon footprint since 2023”) also acceptable. Producers may make specific claims if they can validate them. Certification schemes must be based on “independent third-party verification,” have requirements that are “publicly available,” and must “allow for the use of a corresponding sustainability label,” according to a questions and answers document released by the European Commission in September 2026. But with terms like “ natural ,” it’s often unclear if that refers to farming, winemaking, or both. According to the Commission, if “natural” is used in a brand or product name “in a way that is likely to create an environmental association for the average consumer,” it may count as an environmental claim. The winemaker will have to spell out exactly what it means and be able to prove it if investigated; otherwise, it’s treated as a generic claim, which is banned unless a winemaker provides substantiation. As with all other cases, regulators will make a decision on a case-by-case basis. Another concern is that EmpCo could dilute the quality of third-party audit programs, and, as the number of certifications grows, the question of which certifications are “legitimate” could soon be raised. That’s a concern of Stanbury, who has seen some vanity certifications crop up during his tenure at SWR. While he thinks the bar should be raised in some cases, in others, he sees a low barrier to entry as a way to encourage participation, while “gradually increasing the [sustainability] requirements over time.” ​Although the EmpCo directive applies to all consumer goods sold within the EU, wine is particularly vulnerable as a product that is often communicated about in terms that are hard to define or pin down. Environmental claims have also become part of how wineries communicate everything from vineyard practices and production methods to packaging and carbon footprints. But wine gets no special treatment, with “restrictions and requirements as any other product,” says Littenberg. Terms like “natural” and “low intervention” present a particular challenge because they lack universally accepted definitions and can refer to farming practices, winemaking techniques, or both. Systembolaget views these terms “as descriptions of a production approach,” says Östansjö. CEEV is against using the term, “natural,” with Sánchez Recarte calling it “misleading and contrary to EU legal framework. This directive could bring a positive framework as it would clean the market from terms not supported by science or that are misleading—and the term ‘natural’ would be one of these.” A handful of third-party-verified natural wine certifications do exist and would therefore theoretically make the terminology legal, including Vin Méthode Nature . Arnold Holzer, the winemaker at Eschenhof Holzer in Austria, believes there’s no better name for his certified organic wine, Natural Orange. “ In our view, [the name of the wine] accurately describes the product,” he says. “According to Austrian law, the name is compliant.” His wines passed inspection under existing Austrian law about a year and a half ago. Holzer has no plans to change the use of the word “natural.” “We also want a certain level of assurance to protect our product,” Holzer says. “I believe that organic certification, like ours [ EU Organic Certification ], should be a minimum standard. Additional certifications, in combination, could be beneficial, but it’s a subjective question for each winery whether it’s economically worthwhile.” Spanish winegrower Lorenzo Valenzuela also stands by his decision to call his line of wines, Barranco Oscuro , vinos naturales, though the wines themselves do not include those words on the label. “We defend natural wine as 100 percent pure juice fermented on its own without giving the main role to technology or controlled processes,” he says. “We don’t need labels saying natural wine—we need advised consumers, transparency, and freedom of choice.” For Fabio Bartolomei, the winemaker at Spain’s Vinos Ambiz , wines don’t have to be labeled as “natural” in order to communicate his winemaking philosophy. “ I’ve always thought the debate around ‘natural wine’ is more a linguistic, semantic debate than a wine debate,” says Bartolomei, who opted to stop using the term more than 15 years ago. B y listing fermented grape juice as the only ingredient, and juxtaposing it with a list of excluded additives, including yeast, colorants, preservatives, acids, sugar, and wood chips, Bartolomei signals that he makes natural wine without saying it outright. “ I believe the wine and alcohol industry shouldn’t be exempt from labeling requirements like any other product people put into their bodies,” he says. EU wine labels are now required to carry nutrition and ingredient information, often as a QR code. This could be a crucial moment for winemakers to decide how to communicate the full extent of their winemaking. For Eva Parga Dans, Ph.D., a professor at the Instituto de Productos Naturales y Agrobiología on the Spanish island of Tenerife, winemakers may be able to accomplish this “through [the sharing of] transparent information about production practices, ingredients and additives, and potentially through analytical measurements.” Sánchez Recarte argues that because more than 99 percent of wine producers in the EU are small and medium-sized enterprises, across-the-board certification isn’t the only solution. “The directive does not make third-party certification mandatory for all environmental claims,” he explains. “It specifically requires certain sustainability labels to be based on certification schemes, while other environmental claims can be supported through appropriate evidence and substantiation.” Simply put, labels, badges, and seals must be verifiable by a third party, like Demeter, and environmental claims such as “made from recycled content” can only be used with proper documentation. “Many food and beverage companies have been preparing for EmpCo for several months or more, adjusting marketing materials and campaigns, labels, packaging, etc., in advance of the September 27 required effective date,” says Littenburg. “Many smaller companies are still playing catch-up.” ​U.S.-based producers and exporters may already recognize a similar anti- greenwashing approach; it resembles the Federal Trade Commission’s Green Guides , a playbook for responsibly marketing environmental impact to consumers, which was last revised in 2012. It’s a guidebook for businesses, and while it isn’t enforceable by law, defying it can trigger an FTC investigation. Both measures have the same end goal of banishing confusing greenwashing claims, but they function differently. “EmpCo is prescriptive, banning some sustainability communications and imposing requirements on others, while the Green Guides are non-binding guidance, although some states have incorporated the Guides into their consumer protection laws,” says Littenberg. But for U.S. producers exporting wine to the EU, compliance in the United States offers no protection once their bottles cross into the market. Producers will need to provide adequate substantiation to remain compliant with EU regulations. For some U.S.-based sustainability certifiers, regulations and guardrails are just another step in the right direction. “Napa Green supports initiatives that reduce consumer confusion regarding sustainability claims,” says Megan Scott, the operations director at Napa Green .

The third OneBiome lab at the Shanghai R&I Center expands the international scope of the company’s existing work in this space – with Danone’s R&I Center in Utrecht, the Netherlands, also playing a major role in specialised nutrition and microbiome R&D. Isabelle Esser, chief human resources, R&I, quality and food safety officer at Danone, explained how the ambition is to develop a more intricate understanding of gut health, sequencing of bacteria and the relationship between the diverse inhabitants of consumers’ gut microbiomes. “China is very advanced in terms of sequencing and genomics,” she said, adding that linking this specific expertise with the company’s work at the labs in Paris, Utrecht and Singapore allows the company to benefit from a strong international network. Danone has a longstanding history of working with biotics and gut health – but this scientific landscape has made substantial strides forward over the last decade. Esser emphasised how in the last 10-15 years, the advances of science in sequencing, genomics and metabolomics and artificial intelligence, unlocking hugely vast datasets, enables the reduction of what were previously ten-year sequencing processes to matters of days or even minutes. The growing trend of longevity and the concept of extending your ‘healthspan’ – the number of years of your life spent in good health – is having a major influence on functional food and beverage development. At the new Shanghai facility, Danone’s microbiome research will further fuel innovation in this emerging segment. “We want to go much further into quality of life, healthspan, longevity, and how the gut microbiome is linked with biomarkers of ageing,” Esser explained. She noted that the growing understanding of how this relates to health and quality of life has opened “a vast new area of possibilities” in terms of product and solution development. Advanced digital health technologies, such as those Danone’s labs are equipped with, are bridging historical knowledge gaps and increasingly “making the invisible visible,” she enthused. One such technology included at the new Shanghai laboratory allows for a full-body muscle scan, enabling insights into how certain nutrition solutions impact the quality of the muscle as it changes with age. This can help shape the development of new multi-functional products, such as those combining biotics, proteins and amino acids, that can ensure consumers have the right lean quality of muscle to age healthfully, Esser added. Meanwhile, Lassel pointed out that when this data is combined with data on brain and gut activity, the company is able to “leverage our science [to be] at the service of our brands in a way that is quite integrated…this is quite creative because we have [many] different types of experts, so what used to be linear or sequential processes become much more accelerated.” China, where more than 20% of the country’s population is aged over 60, is a particularly relevant market for this research. And looking broadly at the global picture, Esser noted the significance of the ageing population’s focus on healthspan. “In the late 1990s and early 2000s it was all about living longer,” she told FoodBev . “Now, more and more, people realise that just living longer is not good enough…people don’t want to live longer in pain, in hospital, or in not being able to be autonomous, in the community and doing things they enjoy.” Healthy ageing innovation is being powered by a broader trend impacting the food and beverage industry: the rising awareness of the impact food has on health, with Esser commenting: “While we knew it for hundreds of years before, we’d maybe forgotten it a bit – that food isn’t just about getting calories in and not being hungry, it’s about the impact on health both in the short-term and long-term, and I think we are going back to that.” “This is what we see as a huge trend…affecting the food and beverage industry, and [within it] you’ve got elements like protein, the fibre gap, GLP-1 use for weight management…but it all comes from this trend of food becoming, again, the medicine it was before,” she continued. The use of GLP-1 receptor agonist medications for weight loss has continued to surge this year, with new oral formats further amplifying this trajectory. The US remains the leading market, with Gallup research stating that 11% of US adults nationwide were taking the medications for weight management purposes as of mid-2026. In addition to unlocking new opportunities in development of protein products – due to users frequently losing muscle mass rapidly alongside the intended fat loss – the GLP-1 surge is a driving force behind Danone’s innovation in gut health too. The company acquired The Akkermansia Company last year, a Belgian company that developed the proprietary biotic strain Akkermansia muciniphila MucT. “This biotic is absolutely key in maintaining muscle mass from people who are going through GLP-1 [treatment] or weight management,” said Esser. “This has been clinically proven in a publication in Nature, but it’s not just about Akkermansia per se, it’s about how you use it to have an impact on the microbiome and support muscle protection…all of that is part of the products we want to bring to market.” The new Shanghai hub builds on Danone’s local expertise and forms part of the company’s longstanding presence in China. Since 2020, the company has published 56 scientific papers, filed 27 patents and conducted nearly 30 local studies. The new hub’s approach, bringing together complementary expertise from academia, healthcare and innovation partners, aims to help Danone respond faster to evolving health and nutrition needs across China and the wider APAC region. Its end-to-end capabilities, from research through to industrialisation, are designed to ‘create a two-way bridge’ between China and the rest of the world, bringing ‘global science closer to local needs’ while expanding innovations developed in China across Danone’s network worldwide. “This is very important, the ability to exchange and communicate with our academia network and suppliers – we really want to become a kind of lighthouse when it comes to nutrition, gut microbiome… and how much nutrition can make a difference,” concluded Lassel. “We’re enthusiastic about the impact and the difference we can make here in China, and leveraging and working closely with our colleagues all over the world.” FoodBev Events is hosting its first ever Specialised Nutrition World Congress in Amsterdam, the Netherlands, taking place from 17-18 March 2027.
