Fairtrade sales soar as foundation warns of pay challenges

Las ventas de productos Fairtrade en minoristas británicos crecieron significativamente en 2025, con incrementos de 35% en té y 7% en café. Sin embargo, la Fairtrade Foundation advierte que el crecimiento de demanda debe acompañarse de mejores condiciones de pago para productores. Esta tendencia es relevante para empresas de bebidas que buscan diferenciación mediante certificaciones de sostenibilidad y acceso a consumidores premium dispuestos a pagar por origen verificado y prácticas justas.

Texto completo del artículo
Despite soaring demand for Fairtrade products, growers still faced significant financial challenges, the foundation warned Sales of Fairtrade-accredited food and drink in UK retailers rose across numerous categories last year. However, the Fairtrade Foundation has warned “growing demand needs to be matched by more businesses paying farmers properly”. Volume sales of Fairtrade tea climbed by 35% from 2024 to 2025, according to the foundation’s annual report, while coffee rose by 7%. Flower sales climbed by 41%, while Fairtrade cocoa rose an estimated 20% and banana volumes increased by 4%. But while “more people than ever are choosing Fairtrade”, the accreditation body said pay and conditions for growers remained challenged, particularly as farmers faced mounting climate pressures. It comes as Fairtrade held a Together for Fair Pay event in London last week as part of Fairtrade Fortnight, which brought together around 50 decision-makers and sustainability and buying professionals. The event asked businesses across the industry to commit to fairer pay for the farmers and workers who grow the UK’s favourite products, and to back that commitment with long-term investment in communities and supply chains. Fairtrade cited the work of long-standing Fairtrade supporter Co-op in this area, while Lidl used the event to present its Way To Go Tea – the first tea product of its kind to pay a Fairtrade-calculated Living Income Reference Price on volumes sourced. But it also warned “more businesses need to act” on the issue of fair pay and conditions. “The British public can see what’s happening to our food system, and they’re right to be worried,” said Fairtrade Foundation CEO Eleanor Harrison. “Farmers on the frontline of the climate crisis are being asked to adapt and build resilience while not being paid fairly for what they grow. That isn’t sustainable and it isn’t fair.” Lidl and Co-op were “showing what’s possible when businesses commit to paying farmers properly for the long term – but this cannot be left to a handful of companies”, she added. “Every retailer needs to commit to fair pay for farmers. For fairness and for our food security .” The Together for Fair Pay event built on the Fairtrade Foundation’s wider Fair Pay Campaign, also launched during Fairtrade Fortnight 2026, which calls for fairer pay for farmers and workers and stronger UK action on responsible business practices, including Human Rights and Environmental Due Diligence (HREDD) legislation. Fábricas Nacionales de Cerveza: cómo siguen las negociaciones luego del cierre de la planta de Minas

Fábricas Nacionales de Cerveza (FNC) cerró su planta en Minas, Uruguay, consolidando la producción en Montevideo tras negociaciones de dos meses con el sindicato. Esta semana hay una reunión crítica para definir la continuidad operativa de la empresa. El cierre impacta la estructura productiva regional y marca cambios estratégicos en la consolidación fabril del principal productor de cerveza en Uruguay, con implicancias en capacidad de producción y costos operativos.

Leer nota original
Texto completo del artículo
Luego del cierre de la planta de Minas, Fábricas Nacionales de Cerveza (FNC) y el sindicato continuaron el diálogo para analizar la viabilidad de la empresa en Uruguay. Actualmente la producción está concentrada en Montevideo y esta semana hay una reunión clave para definir cómo continúa la actividad. Tras dos meses de negociaciones, FNC comunicó el cierre de la fábrica minuana el 18 de agosto y a partir de ese momento comenzó una nueva etapa. El freno productivo en Lavalleja tuvo como consecuencia la pérdida de 59 puestos de trabajo, pero la empresa agregó que necesitaba elevar la cantidad y llegar a 80 desvinculaciones. En ese escenario, las partes comenzaron a intercambiar fórmulas para atenuar el impacto. El presidente del sindicato de Pilsen, Bruno Pastorino, dijo a El Observador que en estas semanas se trabajó sobre varias posibilidades, con avances en algunas de ellas. El dirigente mencionó que se llegó a un preacuerdo que incluye el retiro incentivado de 31 operarios de Minas de 53 años en adelante con una partida aproximada de 15 salarios y la incorporación de 28 trabajadores a la planta montevideana. “Nadie queda contento con el cierre de una fábrica, pero la premisa fue perder la menor cantidad de los puestos de trabajo”, dijo el presidente del sindicato. Previo a eso, la fábrica de Minas producía y envasaba cerveza en lata, mientras que la capitalina se encarga del proceso en botellas de vidrio. La intención de la compañía luego del cese de la actividad fue concentrar la actividad en Montevideo y en medio de las negociaciones anunció la posibilidad de realizar una inversión de US$ 14 millones para instalar una línea de producción de latas. “Esta línea permitiría elaborar nuevos productos y empaques para ofrecer nuevas propuestas a los consumidores y sería un paso clave para la continuidad de la industria cervecera nacional y la sustitución de importaciones”, informó la empresa a mediados de agosto a través de un comunicado. La producción de latas incluye las marcas uruguayas Pilsen, Patricia, Norteña y Zillertal y FNC tiene la opción de importar hasta el 20% del total. El sindicato plantea sustituir ese porcentaje de importación y que toda la producción sea nacional con la concreción de la inversión anunciada. Además quiere sumar la elaboración y envasado de Stella Artois. “La intención es evitar que la empresa vuelva a ser inviable dentro de dos años”, expresó Bruno. Otra posibilidad que planteó el sindicato en la mesa de discusión fue aplicar una reducción de la jornada laboral sin pérdida salarial en momentos puntuales de baja de actividad. El dirigente explicó que la idea es completar jornadas semanales de 40 horas durante dos meses en algunos sectores de la empresa. Todos estos temas volverán a ser analizados este jueves en una reunión tripartita que se efectuará en el Ministerio de Trabajo (MTSS). Los últimos dos años Fábricas Nacionales de Cerveza Los problemas en la cervecera (propiedad del grupo brasileño AmBev) en Uruguay no son nuevos. En junio de 2024 manejó por primera vez la posibilidad del cierre de la planta de Minas. Los motivos argumentados en ese momento fueron los altos costos productivos locales y la pérdida de competitividad frente a la cerveza importada, principalmente desde Argentina y Brasil. Luego de negociaciones con el gobierno y el sindicato se arribó a una solución, con el despido de 39 trabajadores, y la fábrica continuó abierta. Dos años después los problemas se profundizaron. En junio pasado anunció el envío de todo el personal minuano al seguro de paro, el comienzo de una parada de mantenimiento en Montevideo y la revisión general de su negocio en Uruguay. Los motivos fueron los mismos: la fuerte presencia de cerveza brasileña que ingresa al país con condiciones más favorables que la producción nacional; los altos costos energéticos y salariales y una fuerte carga tributaria que termina trasladándose a los consumidores en el precio de la cerveza local. Allí surgió una nueva etapa de negociación que tuvo como primer desenlace el fin de la actividad en Lavalleja y la concentración de la producción en Montevideo.

The French government has proposed a plan to extend its sugar tax on soft drinks to include high-sugar processed foods, a measure aiming to improve public health across the nation. In its 2027 budget, France’s government noted that industrially processed foods containing additives – often referred to as ultra-processed foods (UPFs) – are major contributors to excessive sugar consumption. It proposes the extension of its sugar levy, which currently covers sweetened non-alcoholic drinks, as part of measures to tackle the root cause of chronic diseases linked to high-sugar diets, such as diabetes and cardiovascular conditions. The proposed extension would operate on a sliding scale based on sugar content. The government said it aims to safeguard ‘high-quality food produced by our farmers, as well as artisanal products’. The budget states that 40% of adults and 80% of children in France consume too much sugar, while consumption of processed foods has risen by 170% since the 1980s. Commenting on the proposed measure, Paul Boivin, director general of national trade association Fédération des Entrepreneurs de Boulangerie (FEB), told FoodBev: “Reducing excessive sugar consumption is a major public health priority. Manufacturers of bakery and patisserie products have a responsibility to contribute, and we accept that responsibility. “However, FEB opposes the proposed extension of the tax. We believe the government should recognise and support the collective sugar reduction work already undertaken by our federation with its own administrations. For more than a year, our sector has worked with the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Agriculture on a voluntary agreement for industrial pastries and viennoiseries.” A voluntary agreement submitted by the FEB’s members is being submitted, aiming to reduce the average sugar content of their product offer by at least 5% by 2031, Boivin pointed out. This aims for ‘actual sugar reduction’ without compensating by using substitutes or additives. But Boivin argues that taxing products without recognising companies’ existing reformulation efforts “sends a contradictory message”, adding: “A tax alone does not guarantee a lasting improvement in diets. It is dishonest to suggest otherwise in an attempt to justify a tax in disguise.” He expressed concerns about small and medium-size enterprises’ capacity to invest, facing additional costs on top of recipe development, production trials and product testing. “The tax would also create an uneven playing field between pre-packaged products and products sold loose,” Boivin said. “Comparable products with the same sugar content could be treated differently simply because of how they are packaged and sold. We see no public health justification for such a difference in treatment.” Product quality can become significantly impacted when reformulating to reduce sugar, due to its functional role in areas beyond sweetening. Sugar can also contribute to texture, appearance and shelf life, and Boivin noted that preserving quality is critical in successfully reformulating while helping consumers become accustomed to less sweet flavours. “The risk is that the tax absorbs resources needed to achieve these changes. Businesses could have to absorb the cost through lower margins or pass some of it on to consumers,” he concluded. Legislation around sugar reduction has broadened substantially in Europe over the last decade, though most levies currently in place focus on sweetened beverages. The UK government announced plans to extend its Soft Drinks Industry Levy last year , covering additional beverage categories including dairy drinks and RTD coffees in addition to traditional fizzy drinks and fruit squash products. Additionally, the threshold for sugar levels was lowered from 5g to 4.5 per 100ml, with manufacturers given until 1 January 2028 to reduce sugar in their drinks to avoid the tax. The Soft Drinks Industry Levy has seen the average sugar content of drinks in scope drop by almost 50% since its introduction in 2018. However, the UK has not yet gone as far as to extend the levy to cover food products. Instead, measures to reduce sugar consumption in foods have focused on marketing and product placement restrictions. A UK report, published last March, called for a new sugar and salt tax on industrially processed foods such as biscuits, breakfast cereals and crisps, with Chris Hilson, lead author of the report at the University of Reading, calling an extension of the legislation “vital” in improving public health. Elsewhere, Poland has recently proposed an expansion of its sugar tax to cover more beverage formats , including concentrates and ready-to-drink dietary supplements. If approved, the changes would take effect from 1 January 2027. Germany has also proposed the introduction of a new soft drinks sugar tax from 2027, though manufacturers have criticised the proposal, stating that an accelerated timetable – with the legislation previously proposed for implementation in 2028 – could have significant financial consequences for businesses and leave them too little time to prepare.
