Flavor innovations, wellness benefits offer bottled water manufacturers opportunities

Hydration needs are keeping consumers reaching for bottled water data shows. In May, the International Bottled Water Association (IBWA) shared that for the tenth consecutive year, bottled water was the top packaged beverage sold, based on data from Beverage Marketing Corporation (BMC), Wintersville, Ohio. By volume share, bottled water sales in 2025 accounted for 45.5% of refreshment beverages. “It’s time to recognize bottled water as America’s official beverage of choice. Choosing water first helps keep Americans healthier because it contains one essential, zero-calorie ingredient: clean and refreshing water,” said Matt Herrick, International Bottled Water Association’s president and CEO, in a statement at the time of the release. “As our country works to reverse troubling trends in obesity, diabetes, and heart disease, encouraging Americans to choose water instead of sugary drinks is a simple step with real impact.” Gary Hemphill, managing director of research at BMC, offers greater details about the bottled water category. “Standard bottled water will always have a market, but the growth is in premiumization and functionality.” – Mitch Madoff, head of retail partnerships at Keychain “The bottled water category continues to experience modest growth, and is projected to grow 2.5% this year to more than 17 billion gallons,” he says. “It remains the largest beverage category in the U.S. on a volume basis.” Mitch Madoff, head of retail partnerships at Keychain, New York, also points to the notable performance for bottled water in the United States. “Bottled water has been one of the stronger-performing beverage categories over the last year, steadily stealing share from traditional soft drinks as consumers move toward zero-calorie, zero-sugar options,” Madoff says. “On Keychain, we’ve seen strong growth of flavored and sparkling waters specifically. A big part of that growth has come from the premium end. Brands like Voss, Acqua Panna, and Icelandic Glacial are seeing positive growth and momentum. Consumers want a real reason to reach for a specific water brand, so the ones with clear, differentiated identity are standing out on shelves.” In its January report, “Bottled Water in the US,” Netherlands-based Innova Market Insights notes that unflavored bottled water encompasses much of the U.S. market, but recognizes that the evolving market is offering an opportunity for flavored bottled waters that deliver on purposeful products. “Brands face a lot of competition in liquid refreshments in the U.S. and many different beverage products, flavors and functions that go beyond just hydration,” Innova’s report states. Earlier this year, oHy, Grand Rapids, Mich., announced the nationwide launch of two new flavors: Blackberry and Pineapple. With these additions, oHy's portfolio now spans six flavors: Blackberry, Pineapple, Strawberry Lemon, Cherry Lime, Lemon Lime and Raspberry. “From the start, we’ve been focused on innovating with purpose and meeting evolving consumer demand,” said Trent Hartwig, co-founder and CEO of oHy, in a statement. “Our ability to deliver hydrogen water at scale sets us apart in the industry, and these new flavors represent an important step forward. The launch of Blackberry and Pineapple is not only an expansion of our flavor lineup, but also serves as a foundation for future innovations across the functional hydration market.” Saratoga Spring Water, a brand of Primo Brands, also embraced flavor with the launch of Saratoga Collection, a new line of flavored sparkling waters. Saratoga Collection introduces four new flavors: Untitled Berry No.3, Abstraction of Lime, Anatomy of a Peach and Mango Dragon Fruit Perspective. Although flavor releases might be providing consumers with more bottled water options, experts note that it’s the category’s ability to support healthy hydration that is keeping it as a go-to beverage for consumers. “The electrolyte and supplement trend is reshaping the category pretty significantly and creating a two-fold effect,” Keychain’s Madoff says. “It’s driving demand for electrolyte-enhanced drinks, while also increasing demand for standard spring water, which consumers are pairing with their own electrolyte or supplement packets. So, both trends are feeding each other. “On Keychain, we’re seeing this reflected in the growth of brands like Propel+, an electrolyte-infused water from Propel,” he continues. “What it tells you is that people are willing to pay more for functional benefits even in a category as basic as water. Brands that can credibly offer that, like Smartwater’s Antioxidant Alkaline line or BodyArmor Flash I.V., are taking advantage of that.” BMC’s Hemphill notes how bottled water’s health attributes has benefited the category. “Bottled water’s overall positioning remains the same: it is the affordable, portable lifestyle beverage for today’s consumers,” he says. “It’s also the ultimate health beverage with no calories or sugar. This has continued to help propel category growth.” Innova’s report notes that the addition of value-added functional attributes has transformed water from a commodity to a wellness beverage. “These can include functional benefits such as beauty from within, gut health, energy, or others,” the report says. “Trendy ingredients like electrolytes, B vitamins, fiber, probiotics or prebiotics, and collagen add functionality to bottled water in the U.S. and make it better than just water.” Wave Kids is tapping into this with four new canned waters, featuring what it calls better-for-you ingredients. Berry Beach Party and Tropical Vacay are non-sparkling flavored waters offering hydration with organic coconut water powder and ocean minerals, along with organic agave, organic natural flavors, and organic fruit and vegetable juice for color. Meanwhile, Melon Berry Bliss and Apple Adventure are fizzy flavored waters with a dash of prebiotic fiber from organic acacia, plus organic agave, organic natural flavors, and organic fruit and vegetable juice for color. However, not all specialty factions of the bottled water market have fared as well, resulting in bifurcated performance. “Alkaline waters are seeing modest but positive growth, while purified and mineral waters are trending down,” Keychain’s Madoff says. “This pattern tells us that for specialty waters to stand out, they have to be offering something genuinely differentiated or be attached to a strong, larger brand. Smartwater Alkaline is a good example of this because the brand does a lot of the heavy lifting. Standalone specialty brands without that anchor are having a harder time.” Despite these challenges, Madoff still foresees premiumization to have a positive impact on bottled water going forward. “Standard bottled water will always have a market, but the growth is in premiumization and functionality,” he says. “Consumers are increasingly thinking about hydration as a part of a broader wellness routine, not just quenching thirst, and they’re willing to pay for products that back that narrative. OSF Flavors has launched a dedicated flavour solution for prebiotic and other functional sodas, designed to address bitterness, lingering sweetener aftertaste and off-notes typically associated with fibre-enriched, botanical formulations. The US-based flavour house developed the solution due to the ‘sensory baggage’ that can come with using prebiotic fibres, plant extracts and high-intensity sweeteners in formulations. Fibre can contribute bitterness, while stevia and other sweeteners can result in a lingering, ‘over-sweet’ finish, OSF noted. Additionally, commonly used botanicals such as guarana and ginseng can introduce earthy or medicinal off-notes, while flavours used to cover them up can result in unbalanced taste profiles. OSF pointed out that product developers often treat masking, sweetening and flavouring as three separate problems, only to find that fixing one disturbs the other two – all while working against an expiring retail reset date. With this in mind, its new solution is designed as a single, coherent pack built from three components to address these challenges in an integrated manner. A masking system to neutralise the bitterness of fibres and reduce off-notes from botanical actives for a clean, refreshing base A sweetness enhancer optimised for zero- and low-sugar formulations, calibrated to follow the sweetness curve of sugar with no aftertaste A range of flavours developed specifically for functional beverages, designed to pair with botanical extracts rather than working against them To demonstrate performance in real conditions, OSF formulated three prototypes, each containing 2% acacia fibre and one targeted functional ingredient, and each built for low-sugar or zero-sugar formulations. The prototypes included a functional cola drink with guarana, warm spices and citrus zest; a fruity energy drink with acerola, featuring tropical flavours and a touch of candy-flavoured sweetness; and a lime, mint and cucumber botanical drink with ginseng, featuring a refreshing flavour profile for premium, wellness-focused positioning. OSF said all three concepts demonstrated optimised sweetness perception, no bitterness, no aftertaste and a base flavour profile that stayed exactly the same as formulated once fibre and actives were added. Pierre Battu, managing director for Asia at OSF Flavors, said: “For product developers, the practical benefit of this solution is speed and predictability. A validated flavour, masking and sweetening system removes the longest and least predictable part of the development cycle, which frees bench time for the decisions that differentiate a brand: fibre level, active dosage, acidity and packaging.” He added that the prototypes are available on request as reference formulations, while OSF’s team can adapt the pack to a manufacturer’s own fibre source, functional ingredient, sweetener system and label strategy, including clean-label positioning. A technology may perform impressively in trials yet still fail when it reaches a commercial farm. According to Rob Meyers, PepsiCo’s VP of global agriculture procurement, the problem is rarely a shortage of ideas or an unwillingness among farmers to innovate. More often, it’s the difficulty of fitting one new practice into a complex farming system without creating fresh costs or risks elsewhere. PepsiCo made that challenge a central theme of its 2026 Global Agro University in Hannover, Germany, bringing more than 140 growers, suppliers, agronomists and agricultural experts from 35 countries together to examine how innovation works in practice. Sessions covered soil preparation, irrigation, plant nutrition, pest management, harvesting, storage and precision agriculture, combining classroom discussion with demonstrations in fields and storage facilities. The issue has direct commercial implications for the snacking giant. Potatoes are considered a specialty crop and those destined for Lay’s, Walkers and other crisp brands must meet exact requirements for quality, flesh colour and sugar content. Any disruption in the field can therefore affect processing performance, product consistency and the availability of suitable raw materials. Climate change, volatile input costs and shortages of skilled labour are adding to the pressure, but Meyers argues that manufacturers can’t respond simply by presenting growers with more technology. They need to understand why adoption is difficult; how local conditions alter the outcome; and what support farmers need before they can take the risk of changing an established system. Meyers challenges the persistent assumption that farmers are reluctant to embrace change. In his experience, growers who have remained successful have done so because they’ve repeatedly adapted their operations. “What strikes me is that farmers who are still successful today have had to innovate, often across multiple generations,” he says. “That spirit of innovation is at the core of a successful farmer.” He also rejects the idea that agriculture lacks possible solutions. The fundamentals of crop production remain constant – suitable soil, sufficient water, nutrients and sunlight – but growers now have a growing range of technologies and practices to help manage those resources more effectively. The difficulty begins when an innovation moves from a demonstration into an operating farm. A change to irrigation, machinery, crop monitoring or soil management can affect labour requirements, costs, production schedules and decisions across the rest of the operation. “Adoption at scale is one of the biggest challenges, whether we’re talking about new technologies or new practices,” says Meyers. “Any change in a farming system, even one small change, affects the whole system, so there’s a lot of uncertainty and risk for growers.” Those decisions are being made against an increasingly difficult backdrop. Severe weather events are becoming more frequent, growing seasons are less predictable and geopolitical disruption is raising the cost of agricultural inputs. Skilled labour is also becoming harder to secure in parts of the world. Potato producers feel these pressures particularly sharply because their crop doesn’t receive the same level of support in many countries as commodities such as corn, soybeans or wheat. “Potatoes are considered a specialty crop, and the potatoes we grow are a specialty within a specialty,” says Meyers. “We have very specific requirements for quality, flesh colour and sugar content to make a really good potato chip. Any disruption is therefore important for us to understand because we depend so greatly on growers and need to work with them to build resilience.” Even when a technology has already delivered results elsewhere, transferring it successfully is far from automatic. Meyers points out that conditions can vary between neighbouring fields, while the differences between countries may extend to climate, soils, infrastructure, skills and farm economics. “When technologies and innovations fail, it’s often not because the technology itself wasn’t good or didn’t have the ability to solve the problem,” he says. “The difficulty lies in incorporating it into the local system. Applying an idea under local conditions requires an understanding of those conditions and of the farming system around it.” That is why PepsiCo avoids simply “throwing an innovation over the fence” and expecting growers to make it work. Meyers believes manufacturers need to create an environment in which farmers can understand the change, learn from previous experience and manage the risks attached to implementation. PepsiCo uses demonstration farms, learning journeys, regional events and smaller local meetings to bring growers into contact with agronomists and other farmers who have already tested particular approaches. “We spend a lot of time bringing together growers, advisers, subject-matter experts and our own agronomists to discuss the changes they’re making, what they’re learning, how to do it well and which challenges to avoid.” Farmer-to-farmer learning carries particular weight because growers are often more willing to trust someone who has faced the same practical problems than a manufacturer promoting a solution. “Growers learn from each other and trust each other much more than they’re going to trust a food company – and rightly so in many respects. We try to create those opportunities for growers to come together because farming can be a solitary activity.” PepsiCo’s Global Agro University was designed to expand that exchange across markets. Participants examined 10 international case studies, including drip irrigation in China, grower capability development in Vietnam, mechanisation in Thailand and efforts to improve potato supply and quality in India. PepsiCo also opened parts of the programme to remote participants, with more than 200 people joining online. His strongest advice to other manufacturers is also his simplest: “Trust is a valuable asset. It’s very hard to win and extremely easy to lose, so it should be treated like any other asset – just as you would treat the land.” The technology may be ready, but it won’t transform a supply chain by itself.
