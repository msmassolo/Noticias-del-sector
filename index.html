Nearly 7% of the UK population have used GLP-1 weight-loss medications, according to <a href="https://foodfoundation.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://foodfoundation.org.uk/">The Food Foundation</a>, bringing the potential impact of these drugs on the food and beverage industry into sharper focus. For drinks manufacturers, one area to watch is how these medications could influence consumption occasions. Circana says some people taking GLP-1 drugs are turning to beverages as snacking options, potentially opening up new opportunities for brands beyond traditional refreshment. According to Circana, GLP-1 medications are influencing consumers’ nutritional priorities, with greater attention being paid to the quality and composition of what they eat and drink. Brands will need to demonstrate genuine value through their formulations and benefits, rather than simply attaching GLP-1 messaging to existing products.

South Africa’s superfoods: Can untapped gold turn a profit?

Texto completo del artículo
Brazil gave the world açaí and Japan turned matcha into a global obsession, so could baobab, marula, rooibos and ancient African grains be poised for their own moment in the spotlight? With consumers embracing everything from turmeric lattes and mushroom coffees to protein-packed ancient grains, the appetite for ingredients with a story to tell shows little sign of fading. And South Africa has plenty of stories waiting to be told, particularly as food manufacturers hunt for new flavours, textures and nutritional benefits. There’s money in the opportunity, too. SA’s indigenous crops and medicinal plants represent an estimated R12bn (approximately €620m/£530m) annual economy, according to the National Agricultural Marketing Council, with indigenous crops accounting for around R9bn (€465m/£400m). The estimates cover more than food ingredients but reveal the scale of a sector that’s historically struggled for formal recognition. Much of the trade takes place through informal markets and small-scale producers, outside the commercial support structures enjoyed by mainstream agricultural commodities. Matcha’s journey from Japanese tea ceremonies to chocolate, cookies and ice cream shows how far a traditional ingredient can travel, while açaí has made a similar leap through smoothie bowls and functional snacks. SA’s indigenous foods, however, have a more complicated history. Colonial agricultural policies, followed by apartheid between 1948 and 1994, favoured commercial crops such as maize and wheat while traditional African food systems received comparatively little investment. Now, as interest in biodiversity and climate-resilient agriculture grows, those perceptions are changing. Whether that attention can translate into a profitable food manufacturing industry is another matter entirely. If any African ingredient looks ready to give açaí a run for its money, it’s baobab. The fruit of Africa’s iconic upside-down tree has already found its way into health powders and snack bars, although mainstream adoption remains a bigger challenge. Its naturally dry, tangy pulp can be milled into powder without extensive dehydration, making it attractive for confectionery, bakery, beverages and fruit snacks. Vitamin C and fibre provide nutritional interest, while its sharp flavour offers something different from conventional fruit ingredients. British brand Aduna has developed baobab-based products, while suppliers including Nexira and SA’s EcoProducts participate in the wider value chain. Local producer Good Life Organic also showcased baobab powder and baking powder at Anuga 2025. Marula has a head start in international recognition, although most consumers probably know it better from their drinks cabinet than their biscuit tin. Amarula, owned by Heineken Beverages, sold approximately 1.2 million nine-litre cases worldwide in 2025, ranking seventh among the world’s best-selling liqueur brands. It’s a compelling example of how an indigenous African fruit can underpin an international business, although marula’s potential extends well beyond alcohol into juices, confectionery, flavourings and edible seed ingredients. ADM is exploring marula, baobab and African custard apple through its Johannesburg flavour development facility. The company reports that 83% of SA consumers often or sometimes enjoy combining traditional flavours with innovative ingredients, suggesting the opportunity isn’t restricted to exports. Rooibos, meanwhile, doesn’t have to prove that indigenous ingredients can succeed internationally. It’s already done it. Exports reached approximately 10,930 tonnes in 2025, almost double the 5,900 tonnes recorded a decade earlier. Japan accounted for 33%, while the industry now supplies more than 50 markets. El Instituto Nacional de Defensa de la Competencia y de la Protección de la Propiedad Intelectual (Indecopi) realizó inspecciones en establecimientos vinculados a Unión de Cervecerías Peruanas Backus y Johnston, como parte de una evaluación sobre el funcionamiento del mercado cervecero peruano. La actuación se habría originado por una denuncia presentada por Heineken, empresa que opera en el mercado peruano desde 2020. La agencia de comunicaciones de la marca de origen holandés reportó que las diligencias se desarrollaron durante varios días en Lima y otras regiones, con apoyo de la Policía Nacional del Perú. La evaluación comprendería las condiciones de comercialización de cerveza y el acceso de las distintas marcas a los puntos de venta, entre ellos bodegas, supermercados, restaurantes y bares. Uno de los aspectos bajo análisis serían las condiciones comerciales que se establecen entre fabricantes, distribuidores y establecimientos. Estas pueden incluir acuerdos de exclusividad, descuentos, entrega de equipos o programas de fidelización. Sin embargo, este tipo de prácticas no constituye necesariamente una infracción: su legalidad depende de las condiciones en que se aplican y de sus posibles efectos sobre la competencia. La dimensión de la referida evaluación se entiende por la estructura del sector. Según cifras de Euromonitor correspondientes a 2023, citadas en la información difundida por la agencia de Heineken, las empresas Backus y AmBev concentraban aproximadamente el 97.8% del volumen de cerveza comercializado en el país. Esto equivale a casi 98 de cada 100 litros vendidos. Además, la industria cervecera peruana comercializa alrededor de 1,400 millones de litros al año y genera ventas superiores a S/ 15,000 millones. No obstante, su volumen creció apenas 1% en 2025, luego de dos años consecutivos de contracción, según los datos incluidos en la misma fuente. En este contexto, el acceso a los canales de comercialización adquiere relevancia para las empresas que buscan ganar participación. La disponibilidad de espacios en las góndolas, refrigeradoras y establecimientos de consumo puede influir en la llegada de las distintas marcas al consumidor. Cabe precisar que las inspecciones realizadas por Indecopi no implican que la autoridad haya determinado la existencia de una infracción por parte de Backus. Las diligencias forman parte de la recopilación de información y podrían continuar con visitas a otros establecimientos, de acuerdo con la información difundida. Gestión se comunicó con Backus para conocer su posición sobre las inspecciones y los alcances de la actuación de Indecopi, y la compañía afirmó que colaboran plenamente con todas las autoridades en el ejercicio regular de sus funciones. “Actuamos en estricto cumplimiento de las leyes y regulaciones vigentes, con transparencia y responsabilidad”, aseguró.
