Dueña de Lay’s y Doritos prevé inversión millonaria para incrementar producción y nuevos lanzamientos

PepsiCo planifica inversiones millonarias en Perú para expandir capacidad productiva de snacks (Lay's, Doritos) y lanzar nuevos productos, aprovechando crecimiento de doble dígito en el mercado local. La estrategia responde a cambios en hábitos de compra del consumidor y limitaciones actuales de capacidad. Esta expansión es relevante para empresas competidoras en la región, indicando presión competitiva y oportunidad en el segmento de snacks de bebidas complementarias en Latinoamérica.

Texto completo del artículo
El apetito por los snacks sigue abriendo espacio para PepsiCo en Perú. La compañía está creciendo a doble dígito y el avance de la demanda comienza a poner la capacidad productiva en el centro de su estrategia. La multinacional evalúa dónde ampliar sus líneas, mientras prepara nuevas propuestas para un consumidor que también está cambiando sus hábitos de compra. ¿Cuánto está dispuesta a invertir PepsiCo para seguir creciendo en el país? Francisco Díaz, gerente general de PepsiCo Alimentos Perú, adelantó a Gestión que la compañía proyecta invertir más de US$160 millones en Perú durante los próximos tres a cinco años, luego de concretar una inversión de más de US$59 millones en su nuevo centro logístico, Lima Mixing Center. Los recursos contemplan proyectos vinculados con planta, logística y el frente comercial, en línea con la expectativa de mantener el crecimiento del negocio en el mercado local. “Nosotros estamos por ejecutar una inversión en nuestra planta de Santa Anita, donde concentramos más de 10 línea, para ampliar cerca de 20% la capacidad de producción. Y continuamente estamos monitoreando la capacidad de nuestras líneas para futuro ir invirtiendo donde vayamos teniendo esos cuellos de botella”, señaló Diaz. “También se viene, pensando más en el 2027, dentro del segmento naturales un pipeline de innovaciones por delante que creemos que tenemos muchas razones para ganar en ese segmento.” La apuesta productiva llega después de un 2025 en el que PepsiCo creció más de 20% en valor y en un 2026 en el que, según Díaz, el negocio viene avanzando nuevamente a un ritmo cercano al doble dígito. Ahora bien, el crecimiento también se refleja en volumen y en una mayor llegada de los productos a bodegas y hogares. Y es que el canal tradicional, compuesto principalmente por bodegas, concentra más del 70% de las ventas de PepsiCo en el país, mientras que el canal moderno representa alrededor de 15% y los mercados mayoristas entre 8% y 10%. “PepsiCo representa cerca del 10% de la venta de una bodega”, aseguró Díaz. Al mismo tiempo, formatos como los discounters, las tiendas de conveniencia y el comercio electrónico vienen creciendo a mayor velocidad. Sin embargo, para PepsiCo se trata de una tendencia de un shopper que visita cada vez más puntos de venta a lo largo de un periodo de tiempo. Por otro lado, la multinacional se prepara para el impacto del Fenómeno de El Niño, que, según Díaz, probablemente golpeará con más fuerza durante los meses de enero y febrero. Sus planes de mitigación abarcan distintos frentes. Uno de ellos es el trabajo con sus socios agricultores, con quienes la compañía busca garantizar la producción de papas. Díaz señaló que puede existir una menor productividad en las cosechas, por lo que PepsiCo está ampliando su red de productores para contar con la capacidad necesaria para atender su demanda. Otro frente es el logístico. La compañía está previendo rutas alternativas para poder llegar a cada uno de sus distribuidores que atienden las bodegas de provincias. También está cuidando su infraestructura con planes de mitigación para, en caso de lluvias, contar con un drenaje de aguas adecuado y evitar inundaciones. Y por último, PepsiCo trabaja también muy de cerca con sus distribuidores. “Todo este know-how y planes de mitigación que nosotros realizamos para nuestra infraestructura propia, también lo trasladamos a nuestro distribuidor para que pueda hacer lo mismo con su operación”, explicó Díaz. PepsiCo ve una oportunidad de crecimiento en los sabores más locales y más peruanos, una línea de innovación que, según Díaz, viene teniendo un buen desempeño. En ese marco, Lay’s prepara una novedad que sería uno de los lanzamientos más importantes de la compañía hacia el cierre de año, con un sabor pensado para el paladar peruano. “Estamos usando ingredientes locales, pero también sobre todo algo que está bien presente en las calles de Perú”, adelantó Díaz. Aunque no reveló de qué sabor se trata, aseguró que la propuesta tiene una “razón de ganar” y que podría ser “un golazo” para la marca. La fecha de lanzamiento, añadió, está prevista para dentro de unas cuatro semanas, cuando se desplegará una campaña de medios para comunicar el nuevo producto. En paralelo, la compañía viene probando nuevas formas de interacción con el consumidor. Su último lanzamiento con Doritos, una de sus marcas globales, lleva el concepto Incognito, en el que el consumidor debe probar el producto y descifrar qué sabor tiene. La propuesta busca jugar con otros consumidores y conectar con el posicionamiento de la marca: “atreverse a lo desconocido, atreverse y ser atrevido”.

Major food firms commit to healthy sales reporting

Cuatro grandes empresas de alimentos y bebidas (Carlsberg Britvic, Danone North Europe, KP Snacks y Nomad Foods) se comprometen a reportar voluntariamente la proporción de sus productos clasificados como saludables, siguiendo a Premier Foods. La medida busca presionar al gobierno británico para implementar reportes obligatorios sobre transparencia en salud. Este movimiento refleja una tendencia regulatoria creciente en Europa hacia mayor accountability en la composición saludable de productos, con potencial impacto en requisitos de reformulación y comunicación de marca en mercados regulados.

Texto completo del artículo
Four major food companies are to join Premier Foods by introducing reporting on the proportion of their food that is defined as healthy, The Grocer can reveal. Carlsberg Britvic , Danone North Europe, KP Snacks and Nomad Foods said they were making the move in the hope the government would finally push ahead with delayed plans to bring in mandatory reporting to improve transparency on health . The announcement is being made on the eve of The Grocer’s Health Summit in London, at which food industry bosses will call for ministers to step up moves to bring in mandatory reporting for major food companies to encourage a new wave of reformulation. The companies and FDF CEO Karen Betts have written to health secretary Yvette Cooper to say the introduction of reporting is “critical” to support other regulations such as the junk food promotions ban and advertising restrictions. Premier Foods , which is also among the signatories, revealed in June that it was pushing ahead with healthy food reporting , rather than waiting for the government to bring forward its delayed proposals. The letter to Cooper said mandatory reporting would provide “consistent data and evidence to inform future policymaking, as well as an incentive to encourage continued investment in healthier food and drink across industry”. However, the move is set to encounter controversy. The companies are calling on the health secretary to base the new reporting model on the 2004 nutrient profiling model, which the government has pledged to replace with a new 2018 model with far stricter guidelines on what food counts as HFSS – and which would redefine many of the companies’ products as unhealthy. The signatories emphasised that to meet the rapid timescale, manufacturers would need to use the current NPM, because the complexities associated with the more recent model meant they would need to develop a new set of measurements, which would delay reporting. Health campaign groups, while supporting the introduction of mandatory reporting, have urged ministers not to back down to industry calls to shelve the new NPM, warning it would risk jeopardising the “ obesity moonshot” announced under Wes Streeting’s 10-year plan last summer. The government has tasked the FSA with drawing up the new system of mandatory reporting, which would be based on a system negotiated with major supermarkets and suppliers by the Food Data Transparency Partnership (FDTP). However, controversy over the impact of the new NPM, which companies argue would jeopardise hundreds of millions spent in reformulation and lead to many reformulated products being delisted or banned from promotion and advertising, has led to delays. The letter to Cooper states: “Reporting by these companies will be based on the current nutrient profiling model score – the model which underpins the HFSS regulations. “Using this model will enable industry to achieve reporting at pace as it is already implemented into business systems and does not require new methodologies to be agreed with government.” Richard Hall, VP and general secretary at Danone North Europe, said: “We believe transparency is one of the best ways to drive progress on health and have long chosen to go beyond mandatory health and nutrition reporting requirements. “We welcome this group, which will play an important role in driving greater transparency and consistency in the wider food system. “The more we collectively measure and share, the easier it is to see what’s working, where barriers remain and the opportunities to accelerate progress towards better health outcomes.” FDF CEO Betts told The Grocer: “We share the government’s ambition to improve diets across the country, and we believe that the data and transparency that reporting will bring will incentivise companies to double down on progress, and give government a valuable tool to measure the impact of health policies. “Food and drink manufacturers have a strong track record of making everyday foods better for us, and we’re determined to keep that progress going. “That’s why we’re not waiting for regulation , but starting to report now on data that companies already hold. In this way, this group of early movers can test the best ways to collate and report data to support our broader sector and government in informing any future legislation.” In June Mr Kipling owner Premier Foods announced it was going it alone on health reporting, although critics claimed the move was in part aimed at putting pressure on ministers to drop the introduction of the tougher NPM goalposts. However, Premier’s director of environment , social and governance Nick Brown said: “Food companies can play a major role in improving the health credentials of the products they make and sell. “Through disclosure, we have the opportunity to demonstrate the progress we are making and where further progress can be achieved, which is why we are proud to have already disclosed against metrics aligned to the UK government’s Food Data Transparency Partnership.” Obesity Health Alliance (OHA) executive director Katharine Jenner said: “A few companies offering to voluntarily report what they themselves consider to be ‘their healthy sales’ is a positive signal, but as always, it’s important to read the small print. “Nothing should be ruled out at this stage – these companies should be prepared to work cooperatively with government, and to update what they report on once an open and fair consultation process has concluded what a proportionate and evidence‑based approach looks like. This could include using the new Nutrient Profiling Model, which is based on the latest NHS dietary advice, rather than the old model developed more than 20 years ago. “The OHA wants to see a level playing field across all large businesses, with comparable, metrics that tell us something meaningful about what is being sold to us and pave the way to set targets to improve the healthiness of sales. GLOBAL Food Navigator 14 Sep 2026 · 08:43

The race to perfect gluten-free

El mercado de alimentos y bebidas sin gluten en Europa crece de $6,64 mil millones (2025) a una proyección de $10,38 mil millones (2031), con una CAGR significativa. Grandes marcas globales como Campbell's y Arnott's están lanzando versiones sin gluten de productos icónicos, indicando una estrategia de expansión en un segmento de alto crecimiento. Esta tendencia refleja oportunidades de innovación en portfolios existentes y diferenciación competitiva en un mercado cada vez más demandado por consumidores; las bebidas están incluidas en este crecimiento, generando potencial para líneas de bebidas funcionales sin gluten en mercados como Argentina.

Texto completo del artículo
Gluten-free (GF) is big business. The European GF foods and beverages market is expected to grow from $6.64bn in 2025 to $7.12bn in 2026, and is forecast to reach $10.38 billion by 2031, according to Mordor Intelligence. New products are arriving into the space at pace, with major brands entering the category. Iconic Australian snack Arnott’s Shapes have just arrived in UK supermarkets in a GF version and Campbell’s in the US recently launched a GF version of its famous Goldfish crackers, stating that it was “one of the most requested innovations in the brand’s history”. But as the category moves into the mainstream, manufacturers face a challenge: making products that are safe, nutritious and affordable without sacrificing taste and texture. While GF foods are “closely associated with health claims and beliefs, many contain highly refined flour, are lower in fibre, and are high on the glycaemic index”, according to a <a href="https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/13/19/3152" target="_blank" rel="">2024 study</a>. “In comparison with regular food items, they are often inferior in taste and texture. To make up for this shortcoming, manufacturers may add fats, sugars, or other ingredients to their gluten-free products. These factors, and their very high price point, are said to be hindering the growth of the European gluten-free food market.” Mordor attributes sector growth to increasing diagnosis of coeliac disease (an autoimmune disorder in which the lining of the gut is damaged when exposed to wheat, barley or rye), growing adoption of wellness-focused lifestyles, and the rapid expansion of e-commerce. In 2024, the prevalence of biopsy-confirmed coeliac disease in Europe was 0.8%, while screening identified diagnostic markers in 1.3% of the population, according to Mordor. The incidence of coeliac disease is increasing at an annual rate of 7.5%, driven by the adoption of proactive testing protocols. However, the consumer base has extended well beyond those with coeliac to include those with non-coeliac gluten sensitivity. In 2024, the prevalence of this in Europe was 12%, with 40% adhering to gluten-free diets, says Mordor. This consumer segment focuses on symptom relief rather than strict compliance and will tolerate occasional cross-contamination. And, last but certainly not least, there are those who buy gluten-free as a lifestyle choice, and these consumers usually buy GF tied in with organic or allergen-free foods. For people with coeliac, safety is paramount. “They’re after a safe product, something they can eat that won’t cause a reaction and make them sick,” says Frank Vriesekoop, professor in food biotechnology at Harper Adams University. The European Union has a regulatory baseline for gluten-related claims to ensure that this is the case – under this framework, products using a ‘gluten-free’ claim must contain no more than 20 mg/kg (ppm) gluten. “Beyond that, most want a product that tastes and has the physical properties of the product that they used to consume,” says Professor Vriesekoop. “Bread and pasta are a focus because these are products in which gluten plays an enormously important role to get that the structure and the eating experience.” Easy availability is also high on the agenda. The <a href="https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/13/19/3152" target="_blank" rel="">2024 study</a> looked at gluten-free consumers across 13 European countries and found that caregivers of those on a GF diet had the highest purchasing frequency for most of the gluten-free offerings, and were the least satisfied with quality and availability. They experienced more problems with availability and engaged in more coping strategies to obtain the products they need. To create a convincing product, GF brands often have to use a long list of ingredients. And in the era of increasing clean-label demand, this might start to become a problem. Professor Vriesekoop believes there is room for cleaner-label gluten-free products, but while some ingredients could certainly be taken out, there are trade-offs, including cost. “It is possible to balance palatability and clean label, but it all depends how much are consumers are willing to pay for the product,” he says. For a start, fewer ingredients makes for a shorter shelf-life. “A shorter shelf-life makes products more expensive,” he says. “A large proportion of the population is not awash with money, so I don’t think it makes sense in the current climate to push for clean labels.” He is also clear that education about ingredients is vital. A longer list doesn’t necessarily mean that they’re harmful to health.<b> </b>“Many people that buy GF products have a degree of worry about the long lists of additives because they don’t understand them,” he says. A GF bread is never going to have the same simple ingredients list as a loaf of sourdough because manufacturers have to replace the functions of gluten. “They want to put a product on the market where they get the lowest amount of complaints about taste and texture.” Agentic commerce may also be a challenge for the sector. When AI starts to do more shopping for consumers, GF products may run into problems as the agents look for clean label. “I think some gluten-free products will, for a substantial amount of time into the future, still appear as ultra-processed foods because of the additional additives,” says Professor Vriesekoop. Bread is one of the hardest products to replicate. “There’s nothing that does the same thing in bread as gluten,” says Professor Vriesekoop. “We’ve been monitoring gluten-free consumers in the UK for over a decade now and the complaint that some GF bread tastes like cardboard always comes back.” It is possible to create bread with good taste and texture, and fewer additives, but it’s harder to produce at scale because the shelf-life is much shorter than commercially produced breads. Tara Taylor, founder of UK brand Good Grain Breads, knows this to be true. When she had to eliminate gluten from her diet, she didn’t like the commercial offerings and set about creating her own version. ”I taught myself how to bake backwards,” she says, starting out by creating loaves with common additives such as xanthan gum and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (a commonly used additive for improving elasticity, stability, volume and moisture retention in commercial GF breads), and then removing them and experimenting with different grains in various ratios. At first, she sold her loaves locally but they became so popular, she turned it into a brand that’s now sold in the UK in retailers such as Wholefoods Market and Ocado. “Scaling has been awful,” she says. “Baking one or two loaves of bread at home is not the same as baking 100 to 200 loaves.” She developed recipes with scale in mind, but even then, there is no easy way to increase shelf life. The bread is organic and made without oils, gums, additives and preservatives, and allergens. Even the output can be slower as it’s hard to keep the additive-free dough from sticking to the equipment. Alongside that comes compliance with regulations and traceability requirements, all of which add cost. The price point is higher than breads produced by large-scale manufacturers, but “there is a cap – people aren’t willing to pay £8 for a loaf of bread”. The bread is denser than some commercial offerings and “some people don’t want to pay for it; they want that aerated, fluffy bread that lasts for days”, but ultimately, there are enough that do want it to make a viable business. “We’re still going,” says Taylor. “We’re on our next round of scale up. That shows that there is a demand for it. People are becoming more aware of what’s in their food.” Whether it’s commercial or smaller scale, bread is thriving. Bakery products accounted for 45% of the European gluten-free market value in 2025, according to Mordor. GF brand Dr Schär leads the sector across Europe, with Warburtons in the UK and Promise based in Ireland also strong. Some US brands are sold in Europe, such as the flours made by Bob’s Red Mill. Acquisitions are also strengthening the market. In September 2026, Airos Delicatessen, a Spanish gluten-free bakery specialist, merged with Valencian breakfast-cereal producer Esgir, which produces gluten-free, sugar-free, and lactose-free breakfast cereals. The taste and texture of GF food has improved significantly in the past decade as the proliferation of GF brands and products has driven innovation and quality. “There are more producers on the market now,” says Professor Vriesekoop. “That means that it has actually provided its own competition. And with that, the quality has improved.” Huge progress has also been made in availability. “If we take the four big UK supermarkets, 10 years ago, they all had a section with gluten-free products and they had around 80 gluten-free stock keeping units (SKUs),” says Professor Vriesekoop. “That has now increased to around 400.” The question now is where the next stage of innovation heads. There are currently two strands of innovation in the GF sector. First is cleaner label alternatives to the current additives, such as the 2026 launch by Danish company Ferm Food of a fermented binder based on buckwheat and fava beans. The other is biotech innovations aimed at neutralising the gluten in food and drink, such as Prozymi Labs in the UK developing enzymes that neutralise the dangerous parts of gluten, meaning it would preserve the taste, aroma, and texture people associate with bread. Mordor predicts that beverages are expected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 8.12% through 2031, marking the fastest growth among product types, and this is mainly driven by GF beer innovation by biotech companies. The other shift that’s happening, though, is a drive to improve the nutritional value of GF foods. “The direction we’re going to go in is nutrition – ensuring that GF foods have enough proteins, fibre and micronutrients,” says Professor Vriesekoop. Wheat, rye and barley are nutrient-dense, and without them, GF products can lack nutrients such as fibre and certain vitamins. And the same food regulations don’t apply to GF. For example, in the UK, non-wholemeal wheat flour must<b> </b>include calcium carbonate, iron, niacin and thiamin (and folic acid from 13 December 2026).<b> </b>“That same law doesn’t apply to the cereal composition for gluten-free products,” says Professor Vriesekoop. This drive towards optimising nutrition is happening in academia as well as in the industry. For example, <a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1820013/full" target="_blank" rel="">a 2026 study</a> successfully developed gluten-free cereal bars that were nutritionally optimised and treated with a technology called cold plasma treatment to extend shelf-life. Overall, GF has come a long way and what was once a niche category is booming, but delivering on consumer demands for safe, delicious and nutritious without pushing up cost will require ongoing innovation and a careful balancing of priorities.

NielsenIQ: Inflation is Creating a ‘Barbell’ Food Market

NielsenIQ identifica una polarización del mercado de alimentos y bebidas hacia un modelo 'barbell': los consumidores optan por productos premium o por marcas de valor, abandonando el segmento medio. Entre 2021 y 2025, los precios de bienes de consumo rápido aumentaron 26%, modificando la percepción de asequibilidad y necesidad. Esta tendencia afecta directamente la estrategia de portafolio y posicionamiento de marcas, requiriendo decisiones críticas sobre dónde competir en una industria de bebidas donde la segmentación por precio es clave para rentabilidad.

Texto completo del artículo
From Baby Boomers to Gen Z, shoppers of all ages are both willing to pay premium prices for products they see as worth it while still looking for value in pricing, leaving those products in the middle in the dust. The price of fast-moving consumer goods jumped 26% between 2021 and 2025, reshaping how consumers think about affordability and necessity, making them more discerning. In the past five years, many consumers have changed the way they view food, shifting from considering it fuel to treating it as targeted medicine, putting greater emphasis on protein, fiber, gut health and mindful drinking. Also, interest has been shifting from highly processed foods to “real food” made from clean ingredients. “The U.S. consumer hasn’t stopped spending, but rather … become much more intentional about where [to] spend,” Ramon Melgarejo, president of consumer intelligence initiatives and e-commerce at NIQ, said in a press release . “Consumers are asking whether a product truly earns a premium or whether a lower-priced alternative will do the job.” “Products that fall in the middle are finding it harder to answer that question,” Melgarejo said. The report finds the average consumer is no longer a growth target. The report concludes future growth will depend on understanding when and why consumers are willing to trade and when and why they decide to save. Among younger consumers, premium soft drinks like sparkling white teas and complex mocktails, and snacks like truffle-infused potato chips and gourmet cheeses are especially popular. The findings from NIQ and World Data Lab show a “barbell” market where consumers simultaneously buy products delivering exceptional value and those in the premium category. The products sit next to each other in a single basket – think specialty beverages alongside value-priced pantry staples. And the behavior was not clocked just among younger consumers. The data indicate the shopping trend also is rising among older generations. The paradox is illustrated starkly in official statistics. While consumer confidence is weak as measured by various indices, spending continues to grow. Nielsen said brands that lack a premium aura – such as, functional foods and beverages, protein enhanced offerings and fiber enriched snacks – or a strong value advantage – such as private-label products, particularly from big-box stores – risk being left behind. “The broader story is one of increasingly selective consumer behavior, as shoppers of all ages become more deliberate and more willing to switch when products no longer justify their place in the basket,” said Liz Buchanan, president of North America, NIQ. Lo más importante de los últimos días Ver resumen del período · 9 días · Actualizado 2026-09-11

La semana estuvo dominada por una oleada de inversiones estratégicas de los grandes jugadores globales en infraestructura productiva y logística en América Latina, con Coca-Cola y PepsiCo liderando el despliegue de capital en la región. A nivel financiero, los resultados trimestrales mixtos de National Beverage, Brown-Forman y Monster Beverage marcaron la agenda bursátil, con señales de presión en volúmenes en categorías maduras como cerveza y spirits. Las tendencias de consumo siguieron orientadas hacia bebidas funcionales, sin alcohol y RTD premium, con movimientos de M&A alineados a esa dirección. En Argentina, el foco estuvo en la crisis de la cerveza artesanal, la caída de exportaciones vitivinícolas y la histórica inversión de Grupo Peñaflor.

Top 5 eventos del período

  • Coca-Cola comprometió USD 770M en Chile hasta 2030 e inauguró una nueva planta en México tras 30 años de ausencia, marcando su mayor ciclo de inversión en Latinoamérica en décadas.
  • PepsiCo inauguró un mega centro logístico de 24.000 m² en el Callao, Perú, con inversión de USD 59M, posicionándolo como hub de distribución regional.
  • Grupo Peñaflor anunció la mayor inversión en una nueva bodega argentina en 30 años, hito estratégico para la industria vitivinícola local en un contexto de caída de exportaciones.
  • National Beverage Corp. reportó caída del 16% en utilidad neta del Q1 FY2027 e ingresos por debajo de estimaciones, reflejando la presión que enfrenta el segmento de bebidas carbonatadas premium en EE.UU.
  • Danone adquirió Huel, líder en nutrición funcional, consolidando la convergencia entre bebidas y nutrición de alto desempeño como el movimiento de M&A más significativo de la semana a nivel global.
Finanzas

National Beverage Corp. reportó caída del 16% en ganancia neta del Q1 FY2027 alcanzando USD 47M, incumpliendo estimaciones de mercado en ingresos por aproximadamente USD 11M; en paralelo, Brown-Forman y Molson Coors mantuvieron cotizaciones estables tras resultados que cumplieron o moderadamente decepcionaron expectativas. Monster Beverage registró un Q2 2026 récord pero su acción retrocedió tras anunciar un split, mientras Campari y Constellation Brands también enfrentaron presión bajista en sus papeles durante la semana.

Marketing

PepsiCo lidera la tendencia de colaboraciones cross-category con marcas fuera del sector bebidas, ejemplificada por Pepsi y Mountain Dew en alianzas con marcas de cuidado personal, en una estrategia de 'odd-couple marketing' que gana terreno en EE.UU. Poppi, la marca viral de bebidas funcionales adquirida por PepsiCo, avanza en su expansión global con lanzamiento en el Reino Unido, consolidando la apuesta de la compañía por el segmento funcional como vector de crecimiento y relevancia cultural.

Supply Chain

Heineken UK, Molson Coors y Diageo aceleraron sus programas de descarbonización en planta mediante bombas de calor industriales, reduciendo consumos de gas entre 45% y 50%; simultáneamente, Diageo inauguró su planta de USD 415M en Alabama para fortalecer la cadena de suministro del sur de EE.UU. En el frente regulatorio, México proyecta recaudar 264 mil millones de pesos vía IEPS sobre bebidas azucaradas y alcohólicas en 2027, mientras los trabajadores de la destilería Cameronbridge de Diageo en Escocia votaron a favor de huelga, abriendo un riesgo operacional relevante.

Ventas / Comercial

El canal retail en EE.UU. sigue bajo presión para la cerveza convencional, con caídas del 4,5% en valor y superiores en volumen, mientras las bebidas sin alcohol aceleraron un 4,9% interanual en el mismo período, confirmando el trasvase de consumo. En distribución, la expansión del centro logístico de PepsiCo en Callao (USD 59M) y la ampliación del acuerdo de embotellamiento Carlsberg-PepsiCo en nórdicos y bálticos refuerzan la apuesta por eficiencia en la última milla; en sentido opuesto, un fabricante de bebidas en EE.UU. presentó Chapter 11 tras perder su acuerdo de distribución con Costco.
