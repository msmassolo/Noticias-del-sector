How airports are driving trial for beverage brands

Los aeropuertos se consolidan como canales estratégicos de distribución y prueba de productos para marcas de bebidas, con particular énfasis en consumidores jóvenes receptivos a innovación y propuestas wellness. OTG, operador aeroportuario, facilita merchandising y eventos de degustación que aceleran el trial de nuevas bebidas. Este canal representa una oportunidad clave para lanzamientos y posicionamiento de marcas en segmentos premium y funcionales, con audiencia cautiva y predispuesta a experimentar.

Texto completo del artículo
Airports evolved from traditional transportation hubs into retail and hospitality channels where travelers are increasingly receptive to new products, especially with a wellness angle, according to an expert. Younger travelers who are eager to try new products are increasingly a target demographic for airport retailers, added Melinda Smith, vice president of retail markets at OTG, an airport and hospitality operator that helps brands with merchandising and tasting events “Approximately 53% of Gen-Z and Millennials now fly more than three times per year,” Smith said, adding that these consumers are “highly receptive to discovering new brands, products and trends.” Younger shoppers’ behavior has influenced how airport retailers curate assortments on shelves. For example, OTG’s CIBO markets “continuously pulse products in and out of the assortment” on a monthly basis allowing operators to respond quickly to emerging trends and changing consumer demand instead of relying on annual or twice-yearly resets. Smith suggested the travel environment itself creates favorable conditions for discovery and trial due to surging airport traffic in recent years, TSA liquid restrictions and longer dwell times in airports. “Airports are no longer just a transportation hub to get from point A to point B but are increasingly commerce to the latest retail goods, entertainment and culinary experiences,” she said. Consumer preferences toward health, wellness and functionality across beverages are increasingly mirrored in airport retail. “Consumer beverage preferences are continuing to shift away from traditional carbonated soft drinks toward products that offer perceived health, wellness, functionality and convenience benefits,” Smith said. The most popular beverage categories are flavored sparkling water, bottled water, ready-to-drink coffee, enhanced hydration products and protein beverages. “The most significant beverage trends are increasingly centered around functional wellness, mindful low- and no-alcohol choices, and more complex flavor profiles,” Smith said. Interest in functional beverages continue to grow as consumers look for products with “energy, hydration, focus, recovery and other lifestyle benefits,” she explained. Energy drinks have grown about 8% year over year and now outpace traditional soft drinks, while overall functional beverage sales are experiencing double-digit annual growth, she added. She also pointed to growing opportunities for high-protein beverages and single-serve formats as the use of GLP-1 medications influences eating and drinking habits. “High-protein and functional beverages are particularly well positioned within this shift as consumers increasingly look for products that provide a specific benefit in addition to convenience and refreshment,” Smith said. Consumers are also seeking more adventurous flavor profiles, including combinations that blend sweetness, acidity, spice and heat, Smith said. Examples include “mango-habanero, guava-jalapeño, and pineapple-chili,” alongside tropical fruit blends, citrus-forward drinks and botanical beverages. For airport retailers, maintaining flexibility is essential because travelers may be seeking “functional benefits, higher-protein options, or unique flavors that provide an experience beyond traditional beverages,” Smith explained. While airport retail shares some of the same category management foundations as traditional retail, the operating environment is considerably more complex, according to Smith, who spent 23 years working in conventional grocery and retail channels. She noted that pricing often aligns with local market expectations through airport street-pricing requirements, where vendors cannot charge more than off-airport prices for food, drinks and retail but are allowed a small percentage markup for operating costs. Products also move through narrow controlled logistics networks involving security screening, warehousing and multiple handling touchpoints before reaching shelves, she added. Airport’s retail complexity extends to inventory management. A mix of common sense and a desire to actually move certain wines to help him set prices as opposed to pushing prices as high as possible has resulted in a program that maybe doesn’t boast a gaudy profit margin but does serve both guest and restaurant. “I’m still making money, but the percentage may not look like what I had to do on Long Island, which, you know, apologies to all my guests back there,” he says. One of the more straightforward ways to be more profitable is to purchase in larger quantities. While different states and different distributors can have wildly different approaches to volume discounts, most of the time if you’re willing to buy a bunch of cases of whatever, you’ll get a much better price. It can also insulate against product shortages, but of course it also requires something that not every restaurant or bar can have: storage space. Being able to place product at multiple different outlets within the same property or even the same city can also make bulk buying more viable. “Sometimes distributors will set up quantity discounts for us,” notes Amanda Reed, director of beverage at E3 Co. Restaurant Group in Seattle. “We’ve gotta buy five cases at a time or 10 cases at a time — that’s usually our limit for storage in [most of our] properties. But if we’re coming up on a really good deal and there is plenty of supply, I might just try to work something out where we commit to X amount, but we’re only gonna bring it in five cases at a time.” Part of the challenge is the tremendous upheaval in the wholesale category over the last couple of years. The complete collapse of RNDC from the second-largest beverage wholesaler to Chapter 11 bankruptcy in less than two years has played no small part, with tariffs contributing not just to higher costs but also more challenges getting products into the U.S. in the first place. Time, too, is at a premium, especially in a landscape where beverage professionals are often expected to perform any number of additional administrative and managerial tasks, even when that’s not exactly what attracted them to the job in the first place. “When you first become a buyer, you’re so excited to sit down with reps and get to be this figure of authority,” says Johannus Grevelink, beverage director for José Andrés Group. “The reality is that if you open up my inbox every day, I have 40 to 60 emails from people about their product and how it could be a great fit at José Andrés Group or at one of our outlets with the name spelled wrong. Sifting through all that — I don’t know anyone that could have the time to do that.” It’s relatively easy to understand much of what drives up prices at restaurants and bars, but there are plenty of less-obvious costs as well. Maintaining consistent placements doesn’t just make life easier for the beverage director or allow for lower prices on the product itself, it cuts down on training and printing costs, too. In an era of ever-increasing labor costs, gathering staff to taste and train on new products is expensive. But that investment — in time, money, and effort — can also help new products thrive, especially in light of the alternative. “If I place a new spirit, I can send an email to a GM who will forward it to the AGM, who can print the tech sheet for pre-shift and talk to the staff about it,” says Grevelink. “Realistically, now that information has come through the supplier to the buyer to the GM to the AGM to maybe like a floor manager who’s doing pre-shift on a Tuesday. My passion for that bottle is not gonna translate.” Regular staff education and training can help move product, of course, but paying that hourly wage across the entire service team has never been so pricey. Menu changes can also come with significant costs. “We don’t print our menus in house, we outsource,” says Reed. “So I have to be really selective about what I choose to put in the cocktails; I confirm that the inventory and availability is gonna be there. Once I’ve designed my cocktails, there’s a lot of back and forth between me and my reps about supply, because I don’t want to have to change one ingredient in the middle of a season when nothing else on the menu needs to be changed.” This, again, is where larger buys (where possible) can come in handy. A program that changes less frequently can get by with less staff education, fewer menu changes, and require less admin time from frequently-overworked beverage directors and other managers. Yet every such person interviewed for this piece bemoaned that they were not able to spend as much time on some of those aspects of the job — staff training and increasing their own product knowledge most of all. Just as the relationship with the guest is crucial to a great restaurant experience, everyone I spoke to was emphatic that their relationships with producers and suppliers was key to their work, both in terms of quality and in terms of job satisfaction. Cultivating and nourishing them is an active and intentional choice. “When we first opened at the Hope Farm, I was unable to get the large majority of any allocations that I wanted because we didn’t have that history,” says Jones. “Now thankfully, we do. I love meeting new winemakers. I love maintaining our connections with the ones that we love.” For Jones, it’s about far more than just getting access to product or even having something new and exciting to offer to guests. “I’m here to create a relationship with the producer, to host them in town and open a beautiful bottle of wine when they’re here, and to continue to build that relationship.” That kind of relationship also just makes the day-to-day operation of a restaurant smoother. “I’m trying to avoid constantly running out of stuff and having to find a new vendor to fill a placement,” says James Baugh, beverage director for TMC Hospitality. “We want to build a long-term partnership with someone who supports us and has available product for us. And sometimes there’s just days where you blow through some product and it’s nice to have someone you know to ask, ‘hey, can you drop me a couple of things?’” The overwhelming sense from beverage directors right now is that their job involves a consistent fight to keep their heads above water, to make sure that their programs can still meet guest needs while being leaner, more cost-conscious, and more resistant to staff and product turnover than ever before. They’re still passionate and motivated, but they’re also feeling the pressure and struggling to deal with it. PepsiCo y Red Bull escalan su batalla regulatoria en India contra la prohibición de etiquetar productos como 'energy drinks', una disputa con impacto directo sobre posicionamiento de marca y proyecciones de ingresos en uno de los mercados de mayor crecimiento global.

Supply Chain

AG Barr reportó pérdidas de £10 millones en ventas por problemas de cadena de suministro en H1, aunque mantuvo su guía anual tras resolver las disrupciones; Arca Continental completó una expansión de USD 42 millones en su planta de San Antonio para ampliar capacidad de producción Coca-Cola en Norteamérica. En el plano local, Coca-Cola Argentina lanzó Powerade Zero Mountain Blast, señal de adaptación del portafolio al contexto de sensibilidad de precio del consumidor argentino.

Ventas / Comercial

En Argentina, las ventas de supermercados y autoservicios acumulan siete meses consecutivos de caída con una baja del 2,1% interanual en julio, mientras el e-commerce de consumo masivo representa apenas el 6,7% de las ventas totales, limitando el potencial de compensación digital. A nivel regional, Backus Perú proyecta duplicar su facturación hacia 2028 via expansión de canales, y Ambev utiliza Zé Delivery como laboratorio de consumo en grandes eventos para testear innovaciones y defender participación en canales directos.
