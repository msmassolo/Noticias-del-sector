According to executive search specialist Issy Perez of Boyden, the current wave of CEO and C-suite appointments reflects an industry grappling with fundamental shifts in consumer behaviour, technology, portfolio strategy and growth prospects. The turnover reflects mounting pressure on food and beverage businesses as they contend with volume declines, GLP-1 disruption, private-label competition, tariff uncertainty and portfolio restructuring. The shift is not only changing who gets appointed, but also where companies look for leadership talent. For decades, the path to the top of a Big Food company was relatively predictable. Executives typically rose through brand management, operations or commercial leadership roles, building expertise in scale, manufacturing and marketing along the way. Today, boards are looking for something different. “Retirements set the timing, but the skills gap is shaping who gets chosen to succeed them,” says Perez. “The playbook that once rewarded scale, brand-building, and operational discipline is no longer enough. Boards now want leaders who can reshape portfolios, navigate consumer and regulatory uncertainty, and guide organisations through data and technological transformation.” The appointments themselves reflect these changing priorities. Many of the new CEOs bring stronger experience in digital transformation, international growth, portfolio management and consumer insight than traditional manufacturing-led leadership pathways. This reflects a growing recognition that many of the industry’s traditional growth engines, including established brands, product line extensions and pricing power, are losing momentum. With consumer habits rapidly evolving and performance proving harder to find, companies increasingly need leaders capable of reinvention rather than simple operational excellence. “In an environment marked by flat volumes and heightened scrutiny, steady delivery alone is no longer enough to be seen as a success,” says Perez. And boards are demanding results faster than ever. The market’s expectations of leadership have changed significantly, says Perez, who goes on to explain that “the window for patience is narrowing considerably”. Food and beverage manufacturers are facing growing pressure from investors to improve performance and unlock value. As a result, boards increasingly expect incoming executives to arrive with a clear turnaround plan. “Boards are looking for a credible transformation agenda and expect to see progress within the first 18 to 24 months,” Perez says. Put simply, boards are looking for a different kind of CEO. And as expectations of the CEO evolve, so do expectations of the wider executive team. Perez believes the current leadership transition will ultimately be seen as a defining moment for the food industry. “We can already see the shift in the kinds of leaders being appointed.” That change is being reflected right across the executive suite, with greater value placed on adaptability, commercial agility and change leadership. Leadership searches are becoming more global and diverse, while companies are increasingly willing to look beyond their own industries for talent. As Big Food adapts to a more complex business environment, the structure of leadership teams is evolving too. Perez points to the emergence of roles such as chief transformation officer (CTO), chief data and AI officer (CDAIO), chief growth officer (CGO), and senior leaders responsible for health, nutrition and sustainability with direct commercial influence. At the same time, traditional executive positions are expanding well beyond their historical remit. “The traditional roles evolving fastest are the CFO, now a strategic partner on portfolio and capital allocation, the CMO who needs to be as comfortable with data and commerce as with brand, and the supply chain leader whose role is now tied directly to resilience and cost,” she says. The lack of gender diversity at the top of Big Food has become increasingly apparent in recent years. Champagne may be synonymous with celebration, but could the category find new drinking occasions in a can? Tavern is exploring this through Cannes Royale, a range of five Champagne cocktails developed for the Cannes Lions International Festival of Creativity. Packaged in 222ml cans, the drinks were designed for the festival’s social setting, allowing attendees to enjoy a single serve while moving between events, talks and parties. The format proved popular at the event, with Tavern reporting that all 15 cases sold out on the first day. While the launch was designed specifically for Cannes rather than as a strategy to recruit new Champagne consumers, it raises a wider question for suppliers: could ready-to-drink (RTD) formats bring Champagne into new occasions, or will they primarily appeal to consumers already familiar with the category? Tavern’s launch comes against a mixed backdrop for the Champagne industry. According to <a href="https://www.champagne.fr/en/find-out-more/mediaroom/champagne-press/champagne-2025-shipment-review-and-outlook" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.champagne.fr/en/find-out-more/mediaroom/champagne-press/champagne-2025-shipment-review-and-outlook">Comité Champagne</a>, global shipments fell by around 2% in 2025 to 266 million bottles, down from 271.4 million in 2024. In the UK, shipments increased by 1.9% to 22.7 million bottles, while their value fell by 5.9% to €488.1m. Although the figures do not establish why UK shipment value declined, they point to contrasting trends in volume and value within the market. Cannes Royale was developed through Tavern Labs, the company’s internal brand development arm, specifically for the Cannes Lions festival. The team wanted to create something that felt distinctly French and suited to the South of France, choosing Champagne over rosé because of its celebratory associations. The 222ml can was selected as a single-cocktail serve suited to daytime events, talks and parties, allowing attendees to enjoy a premium drink without opening a full bottle. The wider RTD market offers some context for the potential opportunity. According to <a href="https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/analysis/2026/unlocking-the-rtd-opportunity/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://nielseniq.com/global/en/insights/analysis/2026/unlocking-the-rtd-opportunity/">NielsenIQ (NIQ)</a>, RTDs account for just 0.9% of long alcoholic drinks and spirits spending in Britain, compared with 14% in Australia. NIQ estimates that increasing Britain’s share to 3.5% would generate an additional £645m in sales. The figures suggest there is room for the category to develop in Britain, particularly in hospitality venues, where RTDs remain underrepresented. NIQ’s BrandTrack research shows that RTD consumers skew towards younger adults, with 69% aged 18 to 34. Almost two-thirds (65%) drink in pubs, bars and restaurants weekly, spending an average of £154 per month in the on-trade, £39 more than the average consumer. This audience could present opportunities for suppliers developing products around different drinking occasions. Research from <a href="https://www.theiwsr.com/insight/dynamic-rtds-a-beacon-of-hope-for-beverage-alcohol/?entry=9231918" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.theiwsr.com/insight/dynamic-rtds-a-beacon-of-hope-for-beverage-alcohol/?entry=9231918">IWSR</a> also points to continued growth in the wider category. Across 10 major markets, RTD volumes increased by 2% in 2024, while value rose by 4%. RTDs accounted for 3.5% of total beverage alcohol servings in those markets, up from 1.1% in 2014. For Champagne suppliers, these figures provide a reason to explore new formats, but they do not guarantee that premium Champagne cocktails will generate sustained demand. Adapting Champagne for a ready-to-drink format also raises questions about how suppliers can maintain the quality and premium associations attached to the category. French appellation rules require Champagne to undergo its second fermentation in the bottle, meaning it cannot simply be packaged as straight Champagne in a can. Using Champagne as an ingredient in cocktails offers one way to bring the protected wine into a different format. Cannes Royale takes this approach, combining Champagne with French liqueurs and for the brand, ensuring the finished product lived up to its premium positioning was central to development. “It couldn’t just be a fun can with Champagne in it. The ingredients needed to feel genuinely French, the flavours needed to work together, and the finished drink had to feel premium,” says Mike Perry, founder and chief creative officer of Tavern in New York. “Something can sound incredibly French on paper but not necessarily taste good together.” The approach reflects the challenge for suppliers seeking to combine convenience with premium positioning. While format and branding may attract attention, the liquid itself must deliver on consumers’ expectations. While RTDs continue to gain share of beverage alcohol consumption, attracting new consumers is becoming more challenging as the category matures. According to IWSR’s RTDs Strategic Study 2025, just 5% of RTD drinkers across its 10 key markets were new to the category in 2024. Instead, growth was increasingly driven by existing consumers drinking more frequently, particularly younger drinkers. For Champagne cocktails, this raises the question of whether a new format can bring additional consumers into the category or simply offer existing drinkers another way to enjoy it. Perry says making Champagne more accessible was not the original objective behind Cannes Royale. “We weren’t setting out to solve a Champagne accessibility problem. We were trying to create a product that felt completely appropriate to Cannes and the South of France, but in a format people hadn’t seen before. That said, the end result naturally does remove some of the ceremony and commitment of opening a full bottle,” he says. Tavern reports that all 15 cases brought to the Cannes activation sold out on the first day, with attendees returning for additional serves and asking where they could buy the product. While this suggests the concept resonated with the event’s audience, it does not establish whether demand would continue beyond Cannes or attract consumers who would not otherwise purchase Champagne. For suppliers, the commercial opportunity will depend on whether such formats can move beyond event-led activations to generate repeat purchases and incremental demand, while retaining the quality and premium appeal associated with Champagne.

The food and beverage industry is undergoing one of its most significant leadership transitions in decades. In the past 18 months alone, we’ve seen: And the list goes on... But, while CEO succession is a normal part of corporate life, the scale and nature of recent changes suggest something much bigger beneath the surface. According to executive search specialist Issy Perez of Boyden, the current wave of CEO and C-suite appointments reflects an industry grappling with fundamental shifts in consumer behaviour, technology, portfolio strategy and growth prospects. Rather than simply replacing retiring executives, boards are increasingly seeking leaders capable of reshaping companies for a new era. As Perez notes, “Some of this movement is routine, but the pace is not.” Roughly 15 of the world’s 50 largest consumer products companies changed CEOs in 2025. The turnover reflects mounting pressure on food and beverage businesses as they contend with volume declines, GLP-1 disruption, private-label competition, tariff uncertainty and portfolio restructuring. The shift is not only changing who gets appointed, but also where companies look for leadership talent. For decades, the path to the top of a Big Food company was relatively predictable. Executives typically rose through brand management, operations or commercial leadership roles, building expertise in scale, manufacturing and marketing along the way. Today, boards are looking for something different. “Retirements set the timing, but the skills gap is shaping who gets chosen to succeed them,” says Perez. “The playbook that once rewarded scale, brand-building, and operational discipline is no longer enough. Boards now want leaders who can reshape portfolios, navigate consumer and regulatory uncertainty, and guide organisations through data and technological transformation.” The appointments themselves reflect these changing priorities. Many of the new CEOs bring stronger experience in digital transformation, international growth, portfolio management and consumer insight than traditional manufacturing-led leadership pathways. This reflects a growing recognition that many of the industry’s traditional growth engines, including established brands, product line extensions and pricing power, are losing momentum. With consumer habits rapidly evolving and performance proving harder to find, companies increasingly need leaders capable of reinvention rather than simple operational excellence. “In an environment marked by flat volumes and heightened scrutiny, steady delivery alone is no longer enough to be seen as a success,” says Perez. And boards are demanding results faster than ever. The market’s expectations of leadership have changed significantly, says Perez, who goes on to explain that “the window for patience is narrowing considerably”. Food and beverage manufacturers are facing growing pressure from investors to improve performance and unlock value. As a result, boards increasingly expect incoming executives to arrive with a clear turnaround plan. “Boards are looking for a credible transformation agenda and expect to see progress within the first 18 to 24 months,” Perez says. Put simply, boards are looking for a different kind of CEO. And as expectations of the CEO evolve, so do expectations of the wider executive team. Perez believes the current leadership transition will ultimately be seen as a defining moment for the food industry. “We can already see the shift in the kinds of leaders being appointed.” That change is being reflected right across the executive suite, with greater value placed on adaptability, commercial agility and change leadership. Leadership searches are becoming more global and diverse, while companies are increasingly willing to look beyond their own industries for talent. As Big Food adapts to a more complex business environment, the structure of leadership teams is evolving too. Perez points to the emergence of roles such as chief transformation officer (CTO), chief data and AI officer (CDAIO), chief growth officer (CGO), and senior leaders responsible for health, nutrition and sustainability with direct commercial influence. At the same time, traditional executive positions are expanding well beyond their historical remit. “The traditional roles evolving fastest are the CFO, now a strategic partner on portfolio and capital allocation, the CMO who needs to be as comfortable with data and commerce as with brand, and the supply chain leader whose role is now tied directly to resilience and cost,” she says. These changes reflect the growing importance of technology, data analytics and portfolio management in driving future growth. However, one area where progress remains slow is gender diversity. The lack of gender diversity at the top of Big Food has become increasingly apparent in recent years. While industries such as finance, media and technology have made notable progress in appointing women to senior leadership positions, the food and beverage sector continues to lag behind. Female CEOs remain an exception among the world’s largest food manufacturers, raising questions about whether the industry’s leadership transformation extends beyond skills and experience to truly include diversity. There are, however, signs of progress. Looking ahead, Perez expects leadership teams to become leaner, more cross-functional and more diverse in their backgrounds. Most importantly, the CEO role itself is evolving. “The CEO will be just as much a portfolio architect and change leader as an operator.” The leadership changes sweeping Big Food are about more than succession planning. They reflect an industry adapting to new consumer demands, technological disruption and intensifying competition. The result is a leadership model that prioritises transformation over stability. As Perez says, boards are no longer searching for executives who can simply run food companies. They’re looking for leaders who can redefine them.
