From aspartame to allulose: The future of sweeteners takes shape

La industria de bebidas está en transición hacia nuevos edulcorantes como alulosa, ante la presión regulatoria para reducir azúcar y la demanda del consumidor por productos bajos en calorías. Este cambio impacta directamente estrategia de innovación de productos, costos de reformulación y posicionamiento competitivo en el mercado de bebidas.

Texto completo del artículo
From cereals to soft drinks, sweeteners appear in literally thousands of food and beverage products across the globe. And their popularity just keeps on growing, as manufacturers seek to reduce sugar content to meet regulatory targets and respond to growing consumer demand for lower-sugar products, without compromising on taste. But as sweetener use continues to rise, so too does their scrutiny, with studies linking them to everything from an <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/06/11/is-sweetener-xylitol-bad-for-the-heart/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/06/11/is-sweetener-xylitol-bad-for-the-heart/">increased risk of heart attacks and strokes</a> to <a href="https://www.confectionerynews.com/Article/2026/03/09/sweeteners-under-scrutiny-as-scientists-find-links-to-cognitive-decline/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.confectionerynews.com/Article/2026/03/09/sweeteners-under-scrutiny-as-scientists-find-links-to-cognitive-decline/">cognitive decline</a>. And this research hasn’t gone unnoticed by consumers, with some now actively <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/08/21/the-sweetener-trust-crisis-brands-cant-ignore/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/08/21/the-sweetener-trust-crisis-brands-cant-ignore/">moving away from products that contain them</a>. So what does the future hold for sweeteners. And can the industry strike the right balance between reducing sugar, meeting consumer expectations and addressing ongoing questions around health and safety? While many consumers want less sugar in the products they buy, their views on the alternatives are far from straightforward. “Consumers’ approach to what types of sweetness they will accept – or reject – is complex,” says Julian Mellentin, director of industry consultancy firm, New Nutrition. It can depend on individual health beliefs, the occasion and personal priorities. For example, many consumers favour ingredients like honey, dates, and fruit concentrates in their everyday foods and beverages, as they’re perceived to be natural. But those same consumers may be willing to accept artificial sweeteners in a high-protein snack following a workout if they perceive it to effectively deliver energy whilst helping to reduce calorie intake. In other words, a consumer who actively avoids artificial sweeteners in a breakfast cereal or family food product may be far more accepting of ingredients such as <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/04/30/efsa-declares-artificial-sweetener-acesulfame-k-safe/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/04/30/efsa-declares-artificial-sweetener-acesulfame-k-safe/">acesulfame-K</a> or sucralose at a time when performance benefits are prioritised. The result, says Mellentin, is a fragmented market in which different consumers, and even the same consumer at different moments, can have very different expectations of sweetness. This creates a significant challenge for manufacturers. Consumers increasingly want products that are lower in sugar, free from artificial ingredients and still deliver the same taste and texture as their full-sugar counterparts. Complicating matters further is the growing scrutiny surrounding sweeteners themselves. While these ingredients remain essential tools in sugar reduction, ongoing scientific debate have left some consumers questioning whether sugar alternatives are a healthier choice. The debate around sweeteners remains one of the industry’s most closely watched issues. While regulatory authorities continue to approve a range of sweeteners for use within established safety limits, emerging research continues to generate headlines and fuel consumer uncertainty. Studies have explored potential links between certain sweeteners and a range of health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders and cognitive decline. However, many scientists caution that the evidence remains mixed, with much of the research identifying associations rather than proving direct cause-and-effect relationships. As a result, the scientific debate is likely to continue, particularly as sweetener consumption increases and researchers seek to better understand their long-term effects. This disconnect between regulatory confidence and public perception is becoming increasingly important. Even when ingredients are deemed safe by authorities, social media discussions, wellness influencers and headline-driven coverage can shape consumer attitudes, creating reputational challenges for brands that rely heavily on sweeteners in their formulations. For manufacturers, perception can be just as important as science. Consumers are increasingly scrutinising ingredient lists and seeking products they view as natural, transparent and minimally processed. This has created growing interest in sweeteners such as monk fruit, honey and dates, while prompting some brands to reconsider their use of artificial sweeteners, regardless of their regulatory status. A decade ago, reducing sugar while maintaining taste and texture was one of the food industry’s biggest technical challenges. Not anymore. “Sugar reduction is now a normal everyday part of product development in every category,” says Mellentin. It’s embedded in innovation, with advances in ingredients and formulation helping brands overcome many of the barriers that once limited reformulation efforts. As a result, lower-sugar products are increasingly viewed not as a premium benefit but as a baseline expectation. That said, reformulation is rarely straightforward. Sugar contributes far more than sweetness, providing bulk, texture, mouthfeel, preservation and browning. Removing it often requires manufacturers to use combinations of sweeteners, fibres and bulking agents to replicate sugar’s functional properties. Cost also remains an important consideration. Some next-generation sweeteners and natural alternatives can be significantly more expensive than sugar, particularly in categories where margins are already under pressure. As a result, manufacturers are using a diverse range of sweetening solutions. If there’s one thing that’s set to define the sweetener market in the future, it’s diversity, says Mellentin. Rather than a single ingredient emerging as the dominant choice, manufacturers are drawing from an ever-increasing range of options. Natural options such as monk fruit, honey and dates are expected to continue gaining traction, while established low- and zero-calorie sweeteners such as stevia will remain important for manufacturers seeking to meet nutrition targets. Meanwhile, emerging ingredients like <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/21/why-allulose-is-gaining-industry-attention/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/21/why-allulose-is-gaining-industry-attention/">allulose</a> are also generating interest, although adoption remains dependent on regulatory approvals and market access in different regions. Many manufacturers view these next-gen sweeteners as an important part of future sugar-reduction strategies. Having said that, the industry’s next wave of innovation is expected to come not only from novel sweeteners themselves, but from the technologies that make sugar reduction more effective. Future growth in the category is expected to come not only from new sweeteners but also from new technologies. Ingredient companies are investing heavily in sweetener blends, taste modulation systems and fermentation-derived ingredients that can better replicate the taste profile of sugar while minimising off-notes. Advances in artificial intelligence and digital formulation tools are also helping manufacturers identify more effective combinations of ingredients, reducing development times and improving product performance. But technology and novel ingredients alone will not determine the future of the market, says Mellentin. Instead, success will depend on manufacturers’ ability to navigate a complex landscape of health concerns, regulatory pressures and changing consumer expectations. The companies that strike the right balance between these competing demands are likely to shape the next generation of sweetening solutions.
