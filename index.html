Su elaboración, explica la empresa, combina Kola Escocesa con Boticario Dry Gin, un gin artesanal elaborado a partir de una base de caña de azúcar del Valle de Majes, enebro macedónico y botánicos peruanos como molle y muña. Fernando Odiaga Arias, gerente general de Empresa Yura, señaló que la alianza permite a la compañía desarrollar una propuesta a partir de productos asociados con Arequipa. “Hacer equipo con Arequipa Distillery Company nos permite reinterpretar sabores muy nuestros y demostrar que la tradición también puede evolucionar sin perder su esencia. Arequipa Spritz es el resultado de esa apuesta: una colaboración entre dos empresas arequipeñas que comparten el orgullo por lo que hacemos aquí y las ganas de seguir innovando”, relató. Por su parte, Diego Jalsovec Rendón, gerente general de Arequipa Distillery Company, destacó que la colaboración reúne productos de diferentes categorías. “Para nosotros, Arequipa Spritz representa mucho más que crear un nuevo cóctel. Es la oportunidad de unir dos productos que tienen una historia y una identidad muy vinculadas a nuestra tierra, pero que vienen de mundos distintos. Kola Escocesa tiene un lugar especial en la memoria de los arequipeños y Boticario nació con la intención de demostrar que desde Arequipa también podemos crear destilados con identidad y proyección. Encontrarnos en una misma lata es una manera de celebrar esa capacidad de crear, innovar y trabajar juntos”, manifestó.

Flavor innovations, wellness benefits offer bottled water manufacturers opportunities

El agua embotellada consolidó su liderazgo como bebida más vendida en EE.UU. por décimo año consecutivo, representando el 45.5% del volumen de bebidas refrescantes en 2025. La International Bottled Water Association destaca oportunidades en innovación de sabores y beneficios de bienestar como drivers de crecimiento. Este trend global de preferencia por hidratación saludable impacta directamente la estrategia de portafolio y oportunidades de diferenciación para fabricantes locales en Argentina.

Texto completo del artículo
Hydration needs are keeping consumers reaching for bottled water data shows. In May, the International Bottled Water Association (IBWA) shared that for the tenth consecutive year, bottled water was the top packaged beverage sold, based on data from Beverage Marketing Corporation (BMC), Wintersville, Ohio. By volume share, bottled water sales in 2025 accounted for 45.5% of refreshment beverages. “It’s time to recognize bottled water as America’s official beverage of choice. Choosing water first helps keep Americans healthier because it contains one essential, zero-calorie ingredient: clean and refreshing water,” said Matt Herrick, International Bottled Water Association’s president and CEO, in a statement at the time of the release. “As our country works to reverse troubling trends in obesity, diabetes, and heart disease, encouraging Americans to choose water instead of sugary drinks is a simple step with real impact.” Gary Hemphill, managing director of research at BMC, offers greater details about the bottled water category. “Standard bottled water will always have a market, but the growth is in premiumization and functionality.” – Mitch Madoff, head of retail partnerships at Keychain “The bottled water category continues to experience modest growth, and is projected to grow 2.5% this year to more than 17 billion gallons,” he says. “It remains the largest beverage category in the U.S. on a volume basis.” Mitch Madoff, head of retail partnerships at Keychain, New York, also points to the notable performance for bottled water in the United States. “Bottled water has been one of the stronger-performing beverage categories over the last year, steadily stealing share from traditional soft drinks as consumers move toward zero-calorie, zero-sugar options,” Madoff says. “On Keychain, we’ve seen strong growth of flavored and sparkling waters specifically. A big part of that growth has come from the premium end. Brands like Voss, Acqua Panna, and Icelandic Glacial are seeing positive growth and momentum. Consumers want a real reason to reach for a specific water brand, so the ones with clear, differentiated identity are standing out on shelves.” In its January report, “Bottled Water in the US,” Netherlands-based Innova Market Insights notes that unflavored bottled water encompasses much of the U.S. market, but recognizes that the evolving market is offering an opportunity for flavored bottled waters that deliver on purposeful products. “Brands face a lot of competition in liquid refreshments in the U.S. and many different beverage products, flavors and functions that go beyond just hydration,” Innova’s report states. Earlier this year, oHy, Grand Rapids, Mich., announced the nationwide launch of two new flavors: Blackberry and Pineapple. With these additions, oHy's portfolio now spans six flavors: Blackberry, Pineapple, Strawberry Lemon, Cherry Lime, Lemon Lime and Raspberry. “From the start, we’ve been focused on innovating with purpose and meeting evolving consumer demand,” said Trent Hartwig, co-founder and CEO of oHy, in a statement. “Our ability to deliver hydrogen water at scale sets us apart in the industry, and these new flavors represent an important step forward. The launch of Blackberry and Pineapple is not only an expansion of our flavor lineup, but also serves as a foundation for future innovations across the functional hydration market.” Saratoga Spring Water, a brand of Primo Brands, also embraced flavor with the launch of Saratoga Collection, a new line of flavored sparkling waters. Saratoga Collection introduces four new flavors: Untitled Berry No.3, Abstraction of Lime, Anatomy of a Peach and Mango Dragon Fruit Perspective. Although flavor releases might be providing consumers with more bottled water options, experts note that it’s the category’s ability to support healthy hydration that is keeping it as a go-to beverage for consumers. “The electrolyte and supplement trend is reshaping the category pretty significantly and creating a two-fold effect,” Keychain’s Madoff says. “It’s driving demand for electrolyte-enhanced drinks, while also increasing demand for standard spring water, which consumers are pairing with their own electrolyte or supplement packets. So, both trends are feeding each other. “On Keychain, we’re seeing this reflected in the growth of brands like Propel+, an electrolyte-infused water from Propel,” he continues. “What it tells you is that people are willing to pay more for functional benefits even in a category as basic as water. Brands that can credibly offer that, like Smartwater’s Antioxidant Alkaline line or BodyArmor Flash I.V., are taking advantage of that.” BMC’s Hemphill notes how bottled water’s health attributes has benefited the category. “Bottled water’s overall positioning remains the same: it is the affordable, portable lifestyle beverage for today’s consumers,” he says. “It’s also the ultimate health beverage with no calories or sugar. This has continued to help propel category growth.” Innova’s report notes that the addition of value-added functional attributes has transformed water from a commodity to a wellness beverage. “These can include functional benefits such as beauty from within, gut health, energy, or others,” the report says. “Trendy ingredients like electrolytes, B vitamins, fiber, probiotics or prebiotics, and collagen add functionality to bottled water in the U.S. and make it better than just water.” Wave Kids is tapping into this with four new canned waters, featuring what it calls better-for-you ingredients. Berry Beach Party and Tropical Vacay are non-sparkling flavored waters offering hydration with organic coconut water powder and ocean minerals, along with organic agave, organic natural flavors, and organic fruit and vegetable juice for color. Meanwhile, Melon Berry Bliss and Apple Adventure are fizzy flavored waters with a dash of prebiotic fiber from organic acacia, plus organic agave, organic natural flavors, and organic fruit and vegetable juice for color. However, not all specialty factions of the bottled water market have fared as well, resulting in bifurcated performance. “Alkaline waters are seeing modest but positive growth, while purified and mineral waters are trending down,” Keychain’s Madoff says. “This pattern tells us that for specialty waters to stand out, they have to be offering something genuinely differentiated or be attached to a strong, larger brand. Smartwater Alkaline is a good example of this because the brand does a lot of the heavy lifting. Standalone specialty brands without that anchor are having a harder time.” Despite these challenges, Madoff still foresees premiumization to have a positive impact on bottled water going forward. “Standard bottled water will always have a market, but the growth is in premiumization and functionality,” he says. “Consumers are increasingly thinking about hydration as a part of a broader wellness routine, not just quenching thirst, and they’re willing to pay for products that back that narrative. Purified and mineral waters will need to jump on the specialization train as they lose traction against premium offerings and the rise of affordable, private label brands.” Looking for a reprint of this article? A technology may perform impressively in trials yet still fail when it reaches a commercial farm. According to Rob Meyers, PepsiCo’s VP of global agriculture procurement, the problem is rarely a shortage of ideas or an unwillingness among farmers to innovate. More often, it’s the difficulty of fitting one new practice into a complex farming system without creating fresh costs or risks elsewhere. PepsiCo made that challenge a central theme of its 2026 Global Agro University in Hannover, Germany, bringing more than 140 growers, suppliers, agronomists and agricultural experts from 35 countries together to examine how innovation works in practice. Sessions covered soil preparation, irrigation, plant nutrition, pest management, harvesting, storage and precision agriculture, combining classroom discussion with demonstrations in fields and storage facilities. The issue has direct commercial implications for the snacking giant. Potatoes are considered a specialty crop and those destined for Lay’s, Walkers and other crisp brands must meet exact requirements for quality, flesh colour and sugar content. Any disruption in the field can therefore affect processing performance, product consistency and the availability of suitable raw materials. Climate change, volatile input costs and shortages of skilled labour are adding to the pressure, but Meyers argues that manufacturers can’t respond simply by presenting growers with more technology. They need to understand why adoption is difficult; how local conditions alter the outcome; and what support farmers need before they can take the risk of changing an established system. Meyers challenges the persistent assumption that farmers are reluctant to embrace change. In his experience, growers who have remained successful have done so because they’ve repeatedly adapted their operations. “What strikes me is that farmers who are still successful today have had to innovate, often across multiple generations,” he says. “That spirit of innovation is at the core of a successful farmer.” He also rejects the idea that agriculture lacks possible solutions. The fundamentals of crop production remain constant – suitable soil, sufficient water, nutrients and sunlight – but growers now have a growing range of technologies and practices to help manage those resources more effectively. The difficulty begins when an innovation moves from a demonstration into an operating farm. A change to irrigation, machinery, crop monitoring or soil management can affect labour requirements, costs, production schedules and decisions across the rest of the operation. “Adoption at scale is one of the biggest challenges, whether we’re talking about new technologies or new practices,” says Meyers. “Any change in a farming system, even one small change, affects the whole system, so there’s a lot of uncertainty and risk for growers.” Those decisions are being made against an increasingly difficult backdrop. Severe weather events are becoming more frequent, growing seasons are less predictable and geopolitical disruption is raising the cost of agricultural inputs. Skilled labour is also becoming harder to secure in parts of the world. Potato producers feel these pressures particularly sharply because their crop doesn’t receive the same level of support in many countries as commodities such as corn, soybeans or wheat. “Potatoes are considered a specialty crop, and the potatoes we grow are a specialty within a specialty,” says Meyers. “We have very specific requirements for quality, flesh colour and sugar content to make a really good potato chip. Any disruption is therefore important for us to understand because we depend so greatly on growers and need to work with them to build resilience.” Even when a technology has already delivered results elsewhere, transferring it successfully is far from automatic. We try to create those opportunities for growers to come together because farming can be a solitary activity.” PepsiCo’s Global Agro University was designed to expand that exchange across markets. Participants examined 10 international case studies, including drip irrigation in China, grower capability development in Vietnam, mechanisation in Thailand and efforts to improve potato supply and quality in India. The intention wasn’t to replicate each intervention exactly, but to show how growers elsewhere had approached a problem and give participants the opportunity to question those involved. “If somebody has solved something in one part of the world, we need to ask how we can lift that idea and adapt it somewhere else,” says Meyers. “It saves time and effort, avoids unnecessary reinvention and allows growers to focus on delivering the impact they’re after.” A conference can generate plenty of enthusiasm, but PepsiCo wanted participants to leave Hannover with more than just notes and new contacts.
