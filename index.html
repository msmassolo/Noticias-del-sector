Big Food’s C-suite shake-up signals new era

La industria de alimentos y bebidas experimenta una transición masiva de liderazgo en los últimos 18 meses, con cambios significativos en posiciones C-suite de múltiples empresas. Según expertos en búsqueda ejecutiva, esta oleada de sucesiones refleja una industria enfrentando cambios fundamentales en el comportamiento del consumidor y modelos de negocio. La magnitud y naturaleza de estos cambios de liderazgo señalan transformaciones estratégicas profundas más allá de la rotación ejecutiva normal, con implicaciones para la dirección futura de competidores clave en el sector de bebidas.

Texto completo del artículo
The food and beverage industry is undergoing one of its most significant leadership transitions in decades. In the past 18 months alone, we’ve seen: And the list goes on... But, while CEO succession is a normal part of corporate life, the scale and nature of recent changes suggest something much bigger beneath the surface. According to executive search specialist Issy Perez of Boyden, the current wave of CEO and C-suite appointments reflects an industry grappling with fundamental shifts in consumer behaviour, technology, portfolio strategy and growth prospects. Rather than simply replacing retiring executives, boards are increasingly seeking leaders capable of reshaping companies for a new era. As Perez notes, “Some of this movement is routine, but the pace is not.” Roughly 15 of the world’s 50 largest consumer products companies changed CEOs in 2025. The turnover reflects mounting pressure on food and beverage businesses as they contend with volume declines, GLP-1 disruption, private-label competition, tariff uncertainty and portfolio restructuring. The shift is not only changing who gets appointed, but also where companies look for leadership talent. For decades, the path to the top of a Big Food company was relatively predictable. Executives typically rose through brand management, operations or commercial leadership roles, building expertise in scale, manufacturing and marketing along the way. Today, boards are looking for something different. “Retirements set the timing, but the skills gap is shaping who gets chosen to succeed them,” says Perez. “The playbook that once rewarded scale, brand-building, and operational discipline is no longer enough. Boards now want leaders who can reshape portfolios, navigate consumer and regulatory uncertainty, and guide organisations through data and technological transformation.” The appointments themselves reflect these changing priorities. Many of the new CEOs bring stronger experience in digital transformation, international growth, portfolio management and consumer insight than traditional manufacturing-led leadership pathways. This reflects a growing recognition that many of the industry’s traditional growth engines, including established brands, product line extensions and pricing power, are losing momentum. With consumer habits rapidly evolving and performance proving harder to find, companies increasingly need leaders capable of reinvention rather than simple operational excellence. “In an environment marked by flat volumes and heightened scrutiny, steady delivery alone is no longer enough to be seen as a success,” says Perez. And boards are demanding results faster than ever. The market’s expectations of leadership have changed significantly, says Perez, who goes on to explain that “the window for patience is narrowing considerably”. Food and beverage manufacturers are facing growing pressure from investors to improve performance and unlock value. As a result, boards increasingly expect incoming executives to arrive with a clear turnaround plan. “Boards are looking for a credible transformation agenda and expect to see progress within the first 18 to 24 months,” Perez says. Put simply, boards are looking for a different kind of CEO. And as expectations of the CEO evolve, so do expectations of the wider executive team. Perez believes the current leadership transition will ultimately be seen as a defining moment for the food industry. “We can already see the shift in the kinds of leaders being appointed.” That change is being reflected right across the executive suite, with greater value placed on adaptability, commercial agility and change leadership. Leadership searches are becoming more global and diverse, while companies are increasingly willing to look beyond their own industries for talent. As Big Food adapts to a more complex business environment, the structure of leadership teams is evolving too. Perez points to the emergence of roles such as chief transformation officer (CTO), chief data and AI officer (CDAIO), chief growth officer (CGO), and senior leaders responsible for health, nutrition and sustainability with direct commercial influence. At the same time, traditional executive positions are expanding well beyond their historical remit. “The traditional roles evolving fastest are the CFO, now a strategic partner on portfolio and capital allocation, the CMO who needs to be as comfortable with data and commerce as with brand, and the supply chain leader whose role is now tied directly to resilience and cost,” she says. These changes reflect the growing importance of technology, data analytics and portfolio management in driving future growth. However, one area where progress remains slow is gender diversity. The lack of gender diversity at the top of Big Food has become increasingly apparent in recent years. While industries such as finance, media and technology have made notable progress in appointing women to senior leadership positions, the food and beverage sector continues to lag behind. Female CEOs remain an exception among the world’s largest food manufacturers, raising questions about whether the industry’s leadership transformation extends beyond skills and experience to truly include diversity. There are, however, signs of progress. Hershey has emerged as a notable example, making a series of high-profile appointments that broaden both the diversity and external experience of its leadership ranks. In June 2026, the company appointed <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/06/24/hershey-appoints-former-pepsico-exec-as-us-president-to-accelerate-growth/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/06/24/hershey-appoints-former-pepsico-exec-as-us-president-to-accelerate-growth/">Heather Hoytink</a>, a former PepsiCo executive, as president of its US business, placing one of the company’s most important divisions under female leadership. And in September, it named <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/10/01/hershey-appoints-amanda-almond-as-international-president-as-cpg-sets-industry-standard/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/10/01/hershey-appoints-amanda-almond-as-international-president-as-cpg-sets-industry-standard/">Amanda Almond</a> as international president. Whether these appointments signal the beginning of a wider shift remains to be seen. Looking ahead, Perez expects leadership teams to become leaner, more cross-functional and more diverse in their backgrounds. He also expects executives from outside traditional food and beverage backgrounds to play a bigger role in shaping the industry’s future as technology and health increasingly influence consumer demand. Most importantly, the CEO role itself is evolving. “The CEO will be just as much a portfolio architect and change leader as an operator.” The leadership changes sweeping Big Food are about more than succession planning. They reflect an industry adapting to new consumer demands, technological disruption and intensifying competition. The result is a leadership model that prioritises transformation over stability. As Perez says, boards are no longer searching for executives who can simply run food companies. They’re looking for leaders who can redefine them.

Casi la mitad de consumidores RTD son nuevos, ¿categoría continuará en explosivo ascenso?

Texto completo del artículo
El mercado de bebidas RTD (ready to drink) gana terreno dentro de la categoría de bebidas alcohólicas, impulsado por una mayor demanda de productos prácticos y de consumo inmediato. Así, este producto ha ido captando nuevos consumidores y convirtiéndose en un segmento de creciente interés ¿que se mantendrá en los siguientes meses?. Un estudio de Impronta Research sobre la categoría revela sus principales tendencias. De acuerdo al análisis, la cerveza, el pisco, las bebidas RTD y el ron son los cuatro productos favoritos de los peruanos que consumen alcohol, quienes concentran la mayor frecuencia de consumo en la cerveza (60%), seguido de RTD (16%), ron (6%), entre otros. Las ocasiones para la prueba de estas bebidas varían según género, edad y nivel socioeconómico; por ejemplo, en mujeres y 18 a 25 años, una bebida RTD se disputaría ser la opción principal. “Los RTD ocupan la segunda posición como bebida frecuente de consumo, desplazando bebidas tradicionales como el ron, el pisco y otras, no obstante, aún no logra la penetración, tradición ni recurrencia de la cerveza. Otro motivo de peso se asocia a la “novedad” de esta categoría, pues casi la mitad de sus consumidores recién ingresa a este segmento y están construyendo hábitos y espacios de consumo“, manifestó José Oropeza, socio director de Impronta Research. Actualmente, la mayor prueba de bebidas RTD ocurre en Lima y el norte del país, específicamente, en consumidores de los niveles socioeconómicos A y B, entre 26 a 45 años, evidenciando brechas amplias con respecto a la compra del segmento D y de los habitantes de ciudades de la zona centro y oriente, quienes muestran menor ingesta de esta bebida. “En estas zonas hay una menor capacidad de gasto en los segmentos mencionados; además, de una distribución menos desarrollada y una oferta que todavía está muy vinculada al canal moderno, en negocios como supermercados y tiendas de conveniencia. Hay que entender que en dichas zonas, la bodega y el formato unitario resultan fundamentales para ampliar el acceso a la categoría”, destacó. El ejecutivo destacó que la categoría aún requiere de tiempo para desarrollar hábitos de consumos estables y lealtad, en sintonía también con diferentes segmentos, zonas, información clara y formatos de prueba. De hecho, Oropeza mencionó que un 46% de los que consumen RTD aseguraron que es su primer contacto con esta categoría. “La base todavía se está formando, especialmente, con personas entre 36 y 45 años que son la mayoría que se declaran nuevos”, precisó. Respecto a la frecuencia de consumo, el 40% de seguidores de los RTD lo toman una vez al mes, mientras que un 48% lo consumen entre una vez a la semana y cada 15 días. En detalle, la frecuencia semanal es mayor en la capital y la zona norte del país, en el sector económico A y en jóvenes entre 26 y 35 años. El estudio de Impronta Research también incidió en aquellas categorías que hoy son desplazadas de alguna manera por la propuesta del RTD. Así, entre los productos afectados están la cerveza (27%), pisco (17%), vodka (17%), vino (16%), entre otros. Aquellos que optan por los RTD encuentran una alternativa interesadante en este producto por su variedad de sabores, practicidad, precio accesible, disponibilidad, publicidad y estatus e incluso moda. “La propuesta de valor es funcional antes que aspiracional. Además, que convengamos que “precio accesible” es una percepción, no la disposición a pagar”, aclaró. Bajo ese contexto, Oropeza incidió en que si bien para el cierre del 2026 predomina la estabilidad en esta categoría, la balanza se inclina a la baja, a partir de un elevado número de consumidores aguardando reducir antes que aumentar su consumo de RTD. En cifras, un 51% de consumidores de RTD mantendrá la preferencia por este producto, mientras un 27% apunta a disminuir y solo un 22% contempla aumentar. El ejecutivo indicó que estas proyecciones expresan una moderación específica en la categoría, aunque la expectativa para el alcohol en general no muestra el mismo sesgo negativo. “El escenario más probable sería el de un crecimiento selectivo, no generalizado, el nivel socioeconómico A mantiene un saldo de expectativa positiva en su consumo, mientras que los segmentos C y D registran perspectivas negativas. La categoría puede seguir ampliando su base, pero enfrentará una desaceleración si no convierte la prueba en frecuencia”, añadió. Finalmente, Oropeza sostuvo que en este escenario conservador, los resultados son una declaración de intención, no una proyección de ventas o volumen. “La evolución de la categoría en el 2027 dependerá de la recompra, la innovación, el precio y la distribución. Los fabricantes deben priorizar la variedad y rotación de sabores, la comunicación centrada en la practicidad como beneficio, la disponibilidad del producto en frío, campañas que impulsen la segunda compra y formatos unitarios con precio de entrada”, culminó.

Texto completo del artículo
The combined value of the world’s 50 most valuable beer brands has risen to $118.8bn in 2026, according to Brand Finance. But while brands such as Corona Extra, Heineken and Modelo Especial have increased in value, some of the biggest names in US beer have gone in the opposite direction. Corona Extra has retained its position as the world’s most valuable beer brand, with its value rising 4% to $13.9bn. Heineken follows closely behind, up 7% to $13.8bn, while Modelo Especial has climbed to third place after increasing 14% to $8bn, overtaking Budweiser. The findings come as the wider beer market faces pressure on volumes, despite growing demand for alcohol-free alternatives. Brazilian beer brand Brahma recorded the biggest increase in the ranking, with its brand value rising 65% to $1.9bn, moving it up six places to 19th. Thailand’s Chang followed with a 50% increase to $1.4bn, climbing to 25th place, while China’s Tsingtao rose 30% to $4.7bn. Tsingtao was also named the world’s strongest beer brand, achieving a Brand Strength Index score of 95.7 out of 100 and an AAA+ rating. European brands also recorded gains. Guinness increased 26% to $4.3bn, moving into ninth place, while Desperados, Amstel, Tuborg and Peroni featured among the ranking’s fastest-growing brands. Several established US brands recorded significant declines. Budweiser’s brand value fell 35% to $7.7bn, while Bud Light dropped 19% to $4.7bn. Busch also recorded a 26% fall to $1.2bn. Henry Farr, global sector head for alcoholic drinks at Brand Finance, said the ranking showed a shift in beer brand growth towards Latin America, Asia and Europe, contrasting with declines among several traditional American lagers. He added that brands such as Brahma and Chang demonstrated the momentum of emerging markets, while Guinness and Desperados showed that established markets could still deliver growth when brands remained distinctive and relevant. The contrasting fortunes of beer brands come as the wider market faces pressure on volumes, despite growing demand for alcohol-free alternatives. According to IWSR, no-alcohol beer volumes grew 8% in 2025 across its 21 leading markets, while overall beer volumes fell 1%. The research also found that premium-and-above products accounted for 29% of no-alcohol beer volumes in 2025, up from 20% in 2019. Although the data does not establish a direct link between alcohol-free beer growth and individual brand values, it highlights how drinking preferences are changing as brewers look for new opportunities in a market under pressure. For manufacturers and suppliers, this raises questions about how product innovation can help brands remain relevant, from developing alcohol-free alternatives to exploring new ingredients, flavours, brewing technologies and packaging formats.
