Why relaxation could be the next functional beverage frontier

El mercado de bebidas funcionales está evolucionando hacia un nuevo segmento enfocado en relajación y bienestar mental, dejando atrás la saturación de bebidas energéticas y de rendimiento. Retailers como Walmart ya reconocen la oportunidad creando categorías dedicadas ('Mood and Mindset'), lo que señala un cambio en la demanda de consumidores estadounidenses. Esta tendencia representa una oportunidad estratégica de diferenciación y crecimiento para marcas que logren posicionarse temprano en este segmento de bebidas calmantes, reduciendo la competencia de precio frente a líderes consolidados en energéticos y proteicos.

Texto completo del artículo
After years of consumers reaching for beverages promising an energy boost, improved performance or an extra dose of protein, the functional drinks market could be entering a new phase: one focused on helping people slow down. In the US, relaxation beverages are beginning to attract greater mainstream attention, with retailers increasingly recognising the opportunity. Walmart is among those taking notice, introducing a dedicated ‘Mood and Mindset’ category. One of the brands looking to capitalise on this opportunity is Recess, founded in 2017 by Benjamin Witte. Initially built around the idea of providing consumers with a way to unwind, the US brand has developed a range of relaxation-focused beverages and mood-supporting products. In October 2025, Kyle Thomas joined Witte as president and co-CEO, coinciding with the company’s $30m Series B funding round. Thomas brings experience across some of the beverage industry’s established and emerging brands, having previously held senior commercial roles at Coca-Cola and Nutrabolt, the maker of C4 Energy, as well as working on Bloom’s energy and better-for-you soda businesses. With Recess looking to expand its retail presence and establish relaxation as a mainstream beverage category, Thomas believes the opportunity could follow a similar trajectory to energy drinks, which evolved from a relatively unfamiliar proposition into one of the industry’s biggest segments. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, brands such as Red Bull and Monster were helping establish a category that was still unfamiliar to many consumers. Since then, energy drinks have evolved beyond their original proposition, expanding into performance-led products and becoming an established part of the beverage landscape. Thomas believes relaxation could follow a similar trajectory, as consumers increasingly look for ways to balance stimulation with unwinding. “At some point you can only stimulate so much, where there has to be kind of the opposite end of the spectrum,” he says. He sees an opportunity for relaxation beverages to meet this emerging need, with greater consumer awareness and understanding of their benefits helping to unlock wider adoption. “I think you’ll see the same thing happen with relaxation, where it become an increased benefit,” he adds. For Thomas, the opportunity is underpinned by a wider shift towards functional nutrition, with consumers increasingly incorporating products designed to support specific health and wellness goals into their everyday routines. He points to the rise of protein as an example of how an ingredient once associated with specialist nutrition has become part of mainstream consumer habits. “I saw it in my previous life with how much protein jumped up in the last couple of years. Protein’s been around forever. It’s something that became a part of the everyday lifestyle journey,” he says. He sees a similar shift across active nutrition, with products containing ingredients such as creatine and colostrum becoming increasingly familiar to consumers. This growing engagement with functional products also reflects a greater understanding of the importance of mental as well as physical health. “The consumer is already there in so many different platforms, from stick packs and beverage to supplements that they buy online,” he says. However, while consumers are becoming more familiar with functional products, Thomas believes there is still an opportunity to establish how relaxation beverages fit into their everyday lives. Rather than focusing solely on what goes into a relaxation beverage, Thomas believes brands need to communicate why consumers might want to reach for one in the first place. For Recess, that proposition is built around the idea of taking a break. Its positioning encourages consumers to step away from the noise of everyday life, whether that’s during a busy working day or when they simply need a moment to reset. He argues that the opportunity is less about making people feel sleepy and more about helping them feel calm, focused and composed. “It was never so much about the energy. I think the consumer shift that’s happening and what Recess does is sort of give permission to the consumer to say, ‘Hey, it’s okay to ring the bell and take a recess’,” he explains. “That’s the big education part that we have to continue to push and communicate.” That focus on everyday occasions is also shaping Recess’s product development strategy, with the brand looking to extend its proposition beyond relaxation drinks and into new social settings, including the growing no/low-alcohol market. As consumers become more conscious of their drinking habits, Thomas sees an opportunity for beverages that allow people to socialise and unwind without alcohol. Recess is exploring this through its zero-proof range, which includes mini margarita packs, party packs and non-alcoholic martinis. For Thomas, the opportunity is not about replacing alcohol altogether, but offering consumers greater flexibility. “I don’t believe alcohol is dead by any stretch of the imagination,” he says. “There’s just a different awareness of the right time and fit for alcohol in your daily life.” He believes alcohol-free alternatives can help consumers feel part of social occasions without the effects of drinking, particularly the following morning. “I want to feel like I belong when I’m out,” he explains. However, expanding into new occasions and formats does not mean compromising on the fundamentals of beverage development. For Thomas, taste remains the biggest driver of repeat purchases, regardless of a product’s functional proposition. “Flavour and taste will always be number one,” he says. “If it doesn’t taste good, consumers ultimately don’t come back. So, no matter what you’re doing, if you don’t lead with taste and flavour, then you’re going to have a pretty long road.” For manufacturers, the message is clear: functional ingredients and health benefits may attract initial interest, but the drinking experience ultimately determines whether consumers return. “I’ve said this for a long time: products live on the shelf, but brands live in the hearts and minds of consumers, and you can’t get there unless you have a phenomenal taste.” As relaxation beverages move towards the mainstream, Thomas expects competition to intensify. Having witnessed the evolution of energy drinks and better-for-you soda, he believes established manufacturers will soon look towards relaxation as another opportunity for growth. His prediction comes amid a wider shift among major beverage groups towards becoming ‘total beverage companies’, expanding beyond their traditional categories to capture a broader range of consumer preferences and drinking occasions. Molson Coors, for example, has been building its portfolio beyond beer through investments in energy drink ZOA, non-alcoholic cocktail brand Naked Life and a US partnership with mixer brand Fever-Tree. Carlsberg has also expanded its soft drinks exposure through the acquisition of Britvic, while groups such as Suntory and Asahi operate across alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages, including soft drinks, energy drinks and ready-to-drink products. Diageo, meanwhile, has extended its portfolio with alcohol-free offerings including Guinness 0.0, Gordon’s 0.0 and Seedlip. Thomas expects established players such as Coca-Cola, PepsiCo and Keurig Dr Pepper (KDP) to take an interest in relaxation, while also pointing to the potential involvement of alcohol businesses looking to diversify their portfolios. “The next kind of consumer wave is already here, and it starts with relaxation. So, there’s no doubt in my mind that you’re going to see a very similar race to the finish,” he says. That said, he doesn’t necessarily see this as a threat to emerging brands. Instead, he believes the arrival of larger players could help legitimise the category and accelerate its development. “You haven’t really made it until you’ve shown up on a PowerPoint inside the four walls of Atlanta or Coca-Cola or a KDP or a Pepsi type business,” he says. “The more competitors that jump in is really good for the category.” Reaching this level of growth brings new challenges, however. “Once you get to that $100 million mark, it creates a lot of complexity, not just internally in your organisation, but also as you scale into more mainstream,” Thomas says. For Recess, the priority is to build the infrastructure, retail relationships and innovation pipeline needed to support its next phase of growth, while establishing itself as a leading name in the relaxation category. Looking three to five years ahead, Thomas expects relaxation beverages to have become a much more established part of the beverage landscape, with Recess aiming to remain at the forefront of that development. “Recess will be synonymous with this category,” he says.

Non-alcoholic beverage growth slows as category moves mainstream, pushing brands to consider next steps

Las ventas de bebidas sin alcohol para adultos crecieron 12.3% alcanzando USD 1.12 mil millones en los últimos 52 semanas, pero el ritmo se desaceleró tras años de crecimiento de doble dígito. Este ralentización refleja la maduración del segmento hacia el mercado mainstream, lo que genera oportunidades para que marcas reconsideren sus estrategias de posicionamiento y distribución. El movimiento señala una consolidación de categoría con potencial de largo plazo en mercados donde el consumo adulto de bebidas sin alcohol es cada vez más normalizado.

Texto completo del artículo
Adult non-alcoholic beverage sales slowed dramatically in 2026 after years of high double-digit year-over-year growth, but counterintuitively the downshift may not be cause for concern, but rather a reflection of the segment’s move into mainstream and its positive long-term potential, according to industry insiders. Sales of non-alcoholic beverages grew 12.3% to $1.12 billion in the 52 weeks ending Oct. 17, according to NielsenIQ data presented this week at the Adult Non-Alcoholic Beverage Association’s state of industry summit. While players across many food and beverage categories would be happy with such strong growth, it is significantly lower than the 21.1% year-over-year increase the category saw in 2025 to $993 million. The gap is even bigger compared to the 29.6% increase in 2024 and the 30.7% in 2023 over 2022, according to the data. A similar trend is playing out at the store level where sales of adult non-alcoholic beverage are outgrowing those of the rest of the store, but at a slower pace in 2026 compared to the past four years. Data from the retailer Total Wine & More showed adult non-alcoholic sales outpaced sales of the rest of the store by 15% in fiscal year 2026. By many accounts this is a promising figure, but again it is down from 20% in fiscal 2025, 30% in 2024 and a high of 50% in 2022. “Growth is slowing,” acknowledged Marcos Salazar, CEO of the Adult Non-Alcoholic Beverage Association. But, he added, it is “not stalling.” And notably, he added, “it is still growing despite beverage alcohol sales decreasing – allowing it to capture 1.2% of all sales at Total Wine” or about $75 million in the last 52 weeks. The slower, but still strong growth over the years, is partly due to an increasingly difficult economy and disruptions in distribution, Salazar said. It also “is a testament to the maturity of the category, where a lot of these products are becoming mainstream,” added Kaleigh Theriault, part of the beverage alcohol thought leadership team at NIQ. Non-alcoholic adult beverages are “no longer just a one-off product for when somebody doesn’t want to drink alcohol. Rather, they are being used in broader drinking occasions,” she added. The category’s expanding reach is also reflected in who is buying these products. In a survey of 782 US adults who had purchased adult non-alcoholic beverages in the previous six months, conducted by Dry Atlas, 93% said they had also consumed alcohol during that period. And 43% said they had no intention of cutting back on their alcohol consumption, suggesting that the products are finding an audience well beyond people looking to give up drinking. The survey also found that consumers are incorporating the products into a range of occasions. More than half said they drink non-alcoholic beverages at home in the evening (54%) or at parties and gatherings (50%). Another 31% said they consume them at times when they otherwise would not have had an alcoholic drink, such as having an NA beer at lunch, said Victoria Watters, co-founder of Dry Atlas. New usage occasions are driving demand for improved and additional products across beverage categories, and manufacturers that meet this demand are winning more market share. For example, the renaissance in NA beer in recent years has helped the category capture 1.7% of all beer sales in 2026 – almost double the 0.97% it held in 2023, according to NIQ. While this may still be a small segment of the total beer market, NA beer captures the lion’s share of NA sales, accounting for $896 million or 80% of the segment, according to NIQ. Even though NA beer dominates the segment, growth is increasingly coming from a broader mix of products, from dealcoholized wines to canned mocktails that offer consumers a more convenient way to recreate familiar drinking occasions. According to NIQ, NA wine sales grew 17% year-over-year in 2026 to reach $132 million, compared to NA beer, which is up 10.1% in the same period. The fastest growing segment, however, is NA spirits, which includes RTD mocktails and is up 30% year-over-year to reach $87 million. Distribution is both the biggest driver and the biggest hurdle for growth for NA brands, according to insights from NIQ and ANBA. According to NIQ, wider distribution is by far the biggest contributor to incremental sales, adding $143.1 million in adult non-alcoholic sales in the 52 weeks ending June 27. But Salazar notes it is also the biggest challenge for many brands. According to a survey by ANBA, 57% of adult non-alcoholic brands said retail distribution access is a top-three challenge. Part of the equation is established alcohol companies can use existing distributor relationships to get NA extensions on shelf, but independent brands don’t necessarily have that advantage. A patchwork of state laws also hinders distribution, with several states limiting liquor stores from selling non-alcoholic beverages, said Salazar, adding that ANBA is working with legislators to streamline distribution and access. ANBA also is educating retailers about the benefits of stocking non-alcoholic adult beverages, including where they might fit in the store and why consumers are buying them, he said. But getting products onto shelves is only part of the challenge. As distribution expands, brands also need to make sure products are turning at retail, rather than simply occupying more shelf space. NIQ data showed some softening in sales per point of distribution for NA beer and wine, which Theriault attributed in part to the natural effects of expanding into more stores and channels. Building repeat purchases will be particularly important for categories such as NA wine and spirits, where consumers may still be evaluating product quality and finding the brands and formats they prefer. NA beer, meanwhile, has had more time to establish itself as a regular purchase, Theriault said, expressing confidence that repeat rates for wine and spirits will improve as products continue to evolve. Ultimately, the category’s challenge is shifting from introducing consumers to adult non-alcoholic beverages to making them a regular part of the shopping basket. That will require continued investment in product quality, broader distribution and consumer education, industry leaders said. The double-digit growth of recent years may be difficult to sustain as the category matures, but its ability to keep growing even as beverage alcohol sales decline suggests there is still room to expand.

The rise of GLP-1s has had a profound impact on demand for dairy proteins. With users of the drugs requiring more nutrient-dense foods – essentially, more nutrients crammed into smaller portions – high-protein products have become particularly relevant for these consumers. This demand was not caused by GLP-1s – dairy ingredients companies such as Fonterra observe high-protein demand stemming from a variety of factors – but they are an important part of it. Whey proteins are “at the forefront” of GLP-1-driven demand, says Tom Booijink, senior dairy specialist for Europe and Africa at Rabobank. In particular, whey protein isolate and whey protein concentrate are seeing demand. Indeed, <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/12/19/whey-shortage-rocks-dairy/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/12/19/whey-shortage-rocks-dairy/">whey shortages</a> have happened in part due to pressure exerted by GLP-1 users, says Jasper Endlich, dairy market analyst at commodity analytics company Vesper. Whey has long been popular with gym goers and bodybuilders, Booijink points out, but now it has become more mainstream. GLP-1s have accelerated this trend. GLP-1 users need extra protein to mitigate the impact of muscle loss, which is often a side-effect of GLP-1 use. Whey, says Booijink, given its nutritional quality and amino acid profile (meaning that it is absorbed quickly), is particularly well-suited to GLP-1 users. However, the GLP-1 boom is not the only factor in boosting whey demand, as the market was tight before GLP-1 use took off. Manufacturers are also looking at <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/07/24/in-a-global-shortage-what-are-the-alternatives-to-whey/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/07/24/in-a-global-shortage-what-are-the-alternatives-to-whey/">alternatives to whey</a>, including milk protein concentrates, which contain about 80% casein and 20% whey. Using these, manufacturers can still claim to have added whey, Booijink says. Around 90% of products with added protein ingredients use milk protein concentrate, says Randy Steenbergen, senior market intelligence analyst at market intelligence platform Valona Intelligence. However, whey is more prominent in products such as gels, bars and snacks, where performance, convenience and protein density matter most. Enrichment of products with milk proteins is <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/03/25/high-protein-market-shift-from-fitness-to-lifestyle/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/03/25/high-protein-market-shift-from-fitness-to-lifestyle/">no longer confined to sports nutrition</a>, says Booijink, having migrated into products such as bars, RTD drinks, desserts, yoghurts and even the foam on hot drinks. As well as dairy proteins, GLP-1s are leading consumers towards a range of dairy-based foods, such as kefir and skyr, says Jemma Milne, senior nutrition scientist at ingredients company Arla Foods. “Dairy foods can offer high-quality protein alongside a range of naturally occurring vitamins and minerals, making them a useful way of adding nutrient density to the diet.” “GLP-1s have not created the dairy protein boom, but they have certainly put a thumb on the scale of an already tight market,” says Rabobank’s Booijink. GLP-1s are a “very powerful catalyst” of demand, but not the “sole driver”. The level of impact that GLP-1s have had is difficult to quantify precisely, he admits. Yet it is clearly seen as a driver by industry. Companies including Arla, Yili and Saputo have stressed the importance of GLP-1 as a driver for demand, explains Steenbergen. “On a company level ... a lot of agendas in dairy are centred around whey and protein.” GLP-1s are a key part of the drive for high-protein products, says a spokesperson for Danone, particularly because users are preoccupied with maintaining muscle mass. Nevertheless, points out Steenbergen, products with <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/04/07/glp-1-friendly-claims-how-theyre-regulated/" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/04/07/glp-1-friendly-claims-how-theyre-regulated/">explicit GLP-1-friendly claims</a> are still rare in European retail. For most products, suitability for GLP-1 users is only implicit. The dairy sector was already focused on protein before the advent of GLP-1s, with broad-based consumer demand edging towards it. Yet GLP-1s have accelerated this trend, with the drug’s users requiring the nutrient density that proteins like whey can provide them, especially to reduce muscle loss. GLP-1 demand is not at the centre of the dairy protein story, but it is an important character.

Marketing

Supply Chain
