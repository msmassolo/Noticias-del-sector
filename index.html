La cerveza sin alcohol gana terreno en el negocio de Anheuser-Busch InBev (AB InBev) y está atrayendo a nuevos consumidores. La cervecera informó que seis de cada 10 litros vendidos en esta categoría provienen de nuevas ocasiones de consumo y de nuevos consumidores, mientras que sus ingresos crecieron 27% durante el segundo trimestre de 2026. “Con un estimado del 60% del volumen procedente de nuevas ocasiones y nuevos consumidores, la cerveza sin alcohol es una oportunidad para desarrollar la categoría e impulsar el crecimiento incremental del volumen”, dijo el CEO de la empresa, Michel Doukeris. En su reporte financiero del segundo trimestre, informó que los volúmenes de cerveza crecieron 1.1%, mientras que los ingresos aumentaron 5.6% en el periodo citado. Michel Doukeris agregó que el desempeño del trimestre fue impulsado por las principales plataformas de crecimiento de la compañía, entre ellas, las megamarcas, la cerveza sin alcohol y la categoría Beyond Beer. "A medida que organizamos nuestra cartera, esta arquitectura que tenemos hoy es mucho más flexible porque tenemos crecimiento no solo en cerveza, sino en diferentes segmentos en cerveza desde el núcleo, premium, sin alcohol", dijo el directivo en conferencia con analistas. Particularmente en México se fortaleció la categoría de cerveza sin alcohol gracias al crecimiento de Model0% y al lanzamiento de Michelob Ultra Zero, además de que Modelo y Pacífico impulsaron el desempeño de su portafolio premium. En el mercado mexicano, la cervecera también amplió su mercado total al ofrecer a los consumidores más opciones para un mayor número de ocasiones de consumo. Esta estrategia impulsó el crecimiento de los ingresos en las categorías de cerveza premium, convencional, sin alcohol y Beyond Beer, con un incremento de entre un solo dígito medio y alto y mayor participación de mercado. Destaca que ocho de las marcas de cerveza de AB InBev se encuentran entre las 10 más valiosas del mundo, con Corona clasificada como número uno por tercer año consecutivo, de acuerdo al último ranking de Kantar BrandZ. La empresa, con sede en Lovaina, Bélgica, es una de las mayores cerveceras del mundo y comercializa las marcas Corona, Michelob Ultra, Budweiser y Stella Artois, entre otras.

As summer holidaymakers toast marshmallows over campfires, Sarah Neish asks marketing experts whether campers are a smoking hot demographic, and why this consumer segment is left out in the cold by many drinks brands. “Camping is a near-universal ritual, the outdoors, open stars, and a fire pulling people together, wherever you’re from,” enthuses Mark Neal, marketing director for New Zealand whisky brand Thunderdonk. “That makes it incredibly fertile ground for a brand to build a real story in, and it’s exactly the space we are claiming.” It is also why Thunderdonk has just launched a new Campfire Coffee whisky expression, pairing “rich Colombian coffee beans, toasted coconut flakes and aged New Zealand single malt for a bold, smooth, espresso-led sweetness.” Neal paints an evocative picture of sipping a Martini made using the whisky, garnished with a toasted marshmallow pulled straight off the fire. It’s an experience, he says, that’s “hard to beat.” In the summer months especially, the camping world is rich pickings for brands looking to capitalise on festival-goers, staycationers and intrepid explorers looking to throw a backpack (and a tent) over their shoulder and set off in search of either blissful solitude or reigniting connections with loved ones and nature. However, if you’re looking for brand longevity, camping can’t just be a seasonal marketing message, says Neal. “With Thunderdonk, the outdoors isn’t a theme we added, it’s where the brand already lives,” he said, explaining that the cornerstones for the whisky maker are “good company, tall banter and late nights by the fire.” Tim Dexter, business director for London-based food and drinks marketing agency Wonderworks, told db that from a drinks perspective, the opportunity lies in “understanding the behaviours, rituals and needs that emerge when people temporarily step away from everyday routines and consumption habits.” He points out that camping changes the role that food and drink play. “At home, food and drink can easily become functional; when camping, they become part of the experience itself,” he said. “Preparing food outdoors, opening the first drink after setting up camp, sharing drinks around a fire, watching the sun go down with friends, these aren’t just moments where food and drink happen to be present. They are often the moments people remember most.” The brands that succeed in tapping into this potential are the ones that “resist the temptation to become overly literal,” said Dexter. “I don’t think the answer is creating products that scream ‘camping’. The bigger opportunity sits in format and design.” Camping, he claims, “isn’t really about tents.” Instead, it’s about “freedom, exploration, slowing down and spending time with people whose company you enjoy. The visual cues I’d be drawn to are those moments that people romanticise and remember. The golden hour drink as the sun starts to set or the glow of the campfire. The sense of discovery that comes with being somewhere different and the feeling of escaping routine.” According to Dexter, “camping naturally rewards products that are portable, easy to chill, and easy to serve and share. The brands that win will make the occasion feel easier and more enjoyable, rather than simply decorating a pack with mountains and pine trees.” Weighing up the commercial potential of camping-led products, Tom Khan-Lavin, CEO of drinks marketing firm YesMore , takes a realist’s perspective. “Consumers stock up heavily before leaving home, and once at the camp site, drinks brands are fighting a losing battle against limited refrigeration space, passive coolboxes, and melted ice,” he told db . “When you factor in physical storage constraints, the potential spend once campers reach their destination shrinks fast.” That said, he admits, “when campers run out – and they usually do – they become a completely captive audience.” Khan-Lavin recalls a recent camping trip “where we ran out of beer mid-weekend.” Based in the middle of nowhere, “we couldn’t face packing down the campervan to drive to a supermarket,” he said. “There was no campsite shop, and Deliveroo and Uber Eats didn’t cover the area either. When you’re stranded like that, you will buy whatever cold drink is put in front of you.” Food and drink, Khan-Lavin said, “are the main event” when it comes to camping, which “provides a space for people to take their time cooking properly over an open flame. We ended up congregating around our adjacent pitches trading stories about whisky and smoke pairing while the kids ran around with water pistols.” Drinks facilitate that kind of social currency, but like Thunderdonk’s Neal, Khan-Lavin caveats this by saying “but only if they fit the occasion”. Small format wines are nothing new. But thanks to an “innovative” approach and bold branding, Poco Vino is satisfying a market segment whose needs “have never been stronger”, explains Emily Harper, chief marketing officer at Australian Vintage. With a colour-forward marketing campaign, plus a wholehearted embrace of the term ‘Sauvy B’ and the phrase ‘less faff, more fun’, it is fair to say that Poco Vino is fluent in vibes. The Australian Vintage brand launched last year – winning a Drinks Business Award in the process – and immediately leaned into informal stylings and social media-ready moments. These are, after all, the native languages of its Zillennial target market. Style and substance, however, are not mutually exclusive. Its foundations are recognisable – the single serve and pre-eminent grape varieties. Yet, in packaging them for the modern drinker, Poco Vino is addressing very real consumer concerns. “Yes, small format already exists in wine, but it’s in its infancy and hasn’t been done in an innovative way,” explains Emily Harper, chief marketing officer at Australian Vintage. “Whilst the size isn’t new, the consumer needs it is addressing have never been stronger. People are drinking differently today – they’re looking for convenience, moderation and quality. Poco Vino delivers a wine experience in a format that is stylish, sleek and suits modern lifestyles.” Before launching, Australian Vintage ran research panels with more than 3,000 consumers in seven markets. What the team found was a range of barriers to wine when choosing a 75cl bottle: it limits exploration, it ties several drinkers to one style, and it makes it difficult to moderate intake. Poco Vino therefore borrows from success elsewhere in FMCG retail – it makes a wine snackable. The bottles are small, light and even stack, making them easy to transport and to store. That lends itself to drinking at home, but equally applies to picnics or events. With bold colours each pertaining to a variety or style – Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Chardonnay, rosé, Pinot Noir, Malbec and Shiraz – the range is immediately accessible. Even if consumers are increasingly adventurous in their drinking, it is still recognisable varieties in classic expressions that drive attention – “a simple, trusted way for many consumers to choose a wine”, as Harper summarises. Indeed, in a sometimes opaque industry, the aim has been to reduce any feeling of overwhelm in the consumer. “Today’s shoppers are making decisions in increasingly busy retail environments, so a brand needs to capture attention quickly,” Harper says. “Poco Vino’s distinctive branding and strong shelf merchandising help the range stand out and make it easy for consumers to discover the product.” Still, any brand can only succeed if it retains customers. Texto completo del artículo
The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) has prohibited the sale of several spirits brands, including United Spirits’ Royal Challenge Whisky and McDowell’s No.1 Rum. The FSSAI, which oversees food safety in India, prohibited the sale of certain alcoholic manufacturers’ products due to non-compliant laboratory reports citing the use of unauthorised flavours and ‘misleading’ age-related claims. These issues included incorrectly labelling the alcoholic product, using external artificial or nature-identical flavours that ‘mask the product’s natural flavour and make it substandard’, and making ‘deceptive age claims’. The notice was issued by the FSSAI on 10 July, citing 2018’s Food Safety and Standards (Alcoholic Beverages) Regulations. Manufacturers flagged by the regulator include Diageo’s Indian arm , United Spirits. The company’s plant in Baramati, Maharashtra, was found to be non-compliant with regulations regarding McDowell’s No.1 Rum. Additionally, United Spirits’ distillery in Madhya Pradesh was cited for issues related to its Antiquity Blue and Royal Challenge whiskies. According to The Brand Champions 2026 report, Royal Challenge is currently the fifth best-selling Indian whisky brand in the world . McDowell’s No.1 Celebration Rum, produced by Associated Alcohol & Breweries in Madhya Pradesh, was also banned, along with Central Province Whisky. Another producer in Madhya Pradesh, Inbrew Beverages, faced non-compliance, resulting in a ban on the sale of Bagpiper Deluxe Whisky and Old Cask Deluxe Rum. Mohan Rocky Springwater, located in Khopoli, Maharashtra, received a ban on three variants of Old Monk Rum: The Legend, Gold Reserve, and XXX Matured Rum. In Goa, inspections and sampling were conducted at the premises of Mandexi Distilleries & Breweries. Notices were also issued to six other manufacturers in Maharashtra regarding this matter, with the FSSAI stating that “further necessary action will follow soon.” The FSSAI released a statement on 2 August addressing the matter, alleging that “a few manufacturers are found to add flavour externally, which mimics the product’s inherent aroma and taste”, therefore “misleading consumers”. The regulator has identified that these added flavourings undermine the expected standards for spirits. Instead of developing flavours naturally through maturation or from base materials like molasses, malt, or grapes, some manufacturers opt for artificial flavourings. The FSSAI deems such products not only substandard but also misrepresented by using the names of standard categories. According to the authority, these products should be labelled as ‘rum-flavoured’ or ‘whisky-flavoured’ spirits, or ‘flavoured/premix rum’. It also noted that in some products, the front of the pack “completely fails to disclose the true nature of the product”. For example, the FSSAI found Old Monk’s ‘seven years old blended’ rum to be misleading. The investigation revealed that the major ingredient of the rum is neutral (unmatured/unaged) spirit, while the matured rum spirit comprises less than 5% of the blend. Old Monk’s labelling represents “a clear violation of the existing [FSSA] regulations”, which state that the age claim of the product should reflect the youngest spirit in the blend. The FSSAI noted that “manufacturers are not prohibited from incorporating flavouring substances such as coffee, vanilla or other similar flavours, where such use is otherwise permissible under law and is supported by a legitimate technological justification. “The present matter, however, does not pertain to a prohibition on the use of flavouring substances. Rather, it concerns the practice adopted by certain manufacturers of adding the flavour of the standardised alcoholic beverage itself, for instance, the addition of rum flavour in rum or whisky flavour in whisky.” While the investigation uncovered several non-compliant manufacturers, the FSSAI emphasises that this issue does not reflect the entire industry. Additionally, it pointed out that there are no internationally recognised manufacturing practices that allow for the addition of rum flavour to rum or whisky flavour to whisky to create the defining sensory characteristics of standardised alcoholic beverages. United Spirits submitted a Writ Petition to the Bombay High Court on 1 August, challenging an order issued by the FSSAI on 29 June 2026, under the FSSA Act 2006. The challenge pertains to the sale of ‘one of the products’ manufactured at the company’s Baramati unit, which was deemed non-compliant with FSSA regulations regarding product labelling. Diageo India stated in its filing that ‘the product labels comply with the current legal and regulatory framework in India and are consistent with established industry practices’. The company has chosen not to provide further comments due to the sensitive nature of the ongoing legal matter. The Spirits Business has reached out to Inbrew Beverages and Mohan Rocky Springwater for a comment. In its statement on 2 August, the FSSAI noted that “based on the appeal by two manufacturers against the prohibition of sale, conditional revocation has been issued”, without naming them. Citing a conditional relaxation of the sales ban, the regulator has allowed the two manufacturers to sell their existing stock by ‘clearly stating the true nature of the product’ on the front of the packaging. However, for any future production, a clear directive has been issued to prohibit the addition of identical flavours, such as rum flavour or whisky flavour.
