In the Mile High City, the fight is on for the future of the middle tier. A brewer threw the first punch — or at least, the first one in the public eye. In early September, Constellation Brands, the country’s third-largest macrobrewer, told its wholesaler in Denver, Coors Distributing Company (CDC), that it was terminating its arrangement within 30 days. Mark Anthony Group, which ranks fourth behind the Modelo maker in off-premise sales, followed suit less than two weeks later. Sierra Nevada Brewing Company, the country’s second-largest craft brewer by Brewers Association-defined production volume, made its own move for the exit earlier this month. The dominoes fell so fast, and from a westward territory, that your humble Hop Take columnist heard echoes of Republic National Distributing Company’s collapse in California, which started with rapid-fire departures by major liquor suppliers at the top of 2025, and ended with the mega-distributor’s de facto demise earlier this year. Was it a social contagion? Blood in the water? Something else? It’s in vogue for beverage-alcohol industry c-suiters to blame abstractions like “headwinds,” and “Zoomers” whenever trouble arises. But my reporting on RNDC’s California cataclysm indicated bad business decisions, institutional arrogance, and infrastructural rot had pushed the firm to the brink right as the hangover in post-Covid booze sales began to bite. There are always causes, and some are always more proximate than others. So what’s the casus belli for Colorado’s middle-tier trench war? The militants aren’t saying much. “We’re exploring all legal and commercial options,” CDC tells Hop Take via email. “None of the parties provided any reason for their termination and doing so without cause is a direct violation of the Colorado beer franchise law.” The firm, a wholly owned distributorship of Molson Coors Brewing Beverage Co., has already asked Denver’s district court to enjoin Constellation and Mark Anthony from exiting; it is “evaluat[ing] next steps with Sierra Nevada.” “We have concluded our partnership with Coors Distributing Company and remain grateful for their partnership over the years,” says Ellie Preslar, Sierra Nevada’s chief growth officer, in an emailed statement. “Though this was a difficult decision, it is necessary for both the long-term and short-term needs of our business.” A spokesperson for Constellation declined to comment, citing the litigation. Mark Anthony did not respond. What gives? I doubt it’s frustration over facilities. After all, in a bit of incredibly unfortunate timing, CDC just cut the ribbon on a new state-of-the-art, 70,000 square-foot warehouse at the end of August, less than two weeks before Constellation’s pink slip. History doesn’t repeat itself, but often rhymes, and it’s rhyming in the Rockies right now. “How much RNDC blood is on Reyes’s hands in California?” I asked in a column the week I broke the news that the now-defunct distributor was exiting the market last year. “Maybe more than you think.” I was never able to verify whether RBG was in talks to acquire RNDC CA before its exit, as some sources had claimed. But there’s no doubt the mighty beer-turned-”total beverage” distributor’s fierce, many-fronted competition in California hastened RNDC’s failure there. Most of the suppliers who fled pre-collapse, fled to RBG. A few states east and 18 months later, Mark Anthony and Sierra Nevada have signaled their aims to move their CDC volume to RBG. Constellation has been playing coy, but it’s almost unfathomable that it would move to another house. In a remarkable turn of industry fate, RBG acquired its Colorado operation just this year from, you guessed it: RNDC. Its entry into the state, along with Southern Glazer’s Wine & Spirits’ acquisition of Eagle Rock Distributing Co. earlier this year, has shifted the middle-tier calculus there. Now, CDC is facing subtraction. (Laurel Slick, RBG’s vice-president of corporate affairs, provided statements to Hop Take welcoming Mark Anthony and Sierra Nevada to the fold.) But. But! While CDC execs may be uttering f-words privately, they’re uttering another f-word publicly: franchise. The history of franchise law is long, winding, and deathly boring, but the upshot is that beer distributors are nearly unique in the protections afforded to them by most states’ laws. The most crucial of these is the so-called “just cause” provision, which requires brewers to cite a legally defensible breach of their agreement with the house they’re trying to terminate. This provision is written into most states’ codes because wholesalers marched on statehouses to make it so in the ‘60s, ‘70s, and ‘80s. Critics argue it harms competition by making it difficult for good brewers to bail on bad distributors; boosters insist it protects competition by making it difficult for bad brewers to strong-arm good distributors. But it’s there. The protective power of this provision was tested most famously in Florida roughly a quarter-century ago, and distributors came away with the whip hand. In Maris Distributing v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., a wholesaler fought the brewer tooth and nail for a decade over a termination it believed unjust. The family — the kin of baseball great Roger Maris, who’d been bestowed an A-B house by Gussie Busch after finishing out his Hall of Fame career with the St. Louis Cardinals — won a $120 million settlement in 2005. As the mantra goes: there are 50 different states, and 50 different three-tier systems. A version of the just-cause clause is on the books in Colorado. But it’s a weird version. It dictates a brewer hoping to terminate a distributor has to cite “alleged noncompliance” and allow the offending firm 60 days to “cure” the infraction. If the distributor doesn’t shape up in that time, then the brewer can ship out. That’s all right at the top of C.R.S. § 44-3-408 . But in subsection three, the code appears to contradict itself, noting “the supplier shall have the right to terminate an agreement with a wholesaler at any time by giving the wholesaler at least ninety days’ written notice by certified mail.” To me, a not-lawyer, , the Colorado code appears to say that a brewer must have just cause to terminate a distributor after 60 days, but can terminate for any reason after 90 days. You will not be shocked to learn that this is not how CDC’s actual lawyers read the text. “Under Constellation’s novel interpretation of Colorado law, its termination will become final after 90 days,” the firm’s legal team wrote in a September 25 filing. “In the interim, there is a likelihood that CDC will face cascading terminations from its other suppliers.” For a trade that has long grasped the power of a united political bloc, CDC’s existential fight is cause for alarm. At the National Beer Wholesalers Association’s (NBWA) annual conference in Orlando in mid-October, president and chief executive Craig Purser sounded the klaxons. “The efforts to weaken the three-tier system are going to keep coming, and that’s why we need you to double down on your support and participation in NBWA and your state’s association,” he said, per a dispatch from Brewbound. Whether the suppliers’ assessments of Colorado’s code are innovative, or illegal, is a matter for the judicial system. But in the meantime, if you’re wondering who the hell would write a statute like that, well, the independent beverage-alcohol bar (ahem) is too. Colorado’s franchise law is “really badly written,” Drew Jaglom of Tannenbaum Helpern Syracuse & Hirschtritt LLP told a presumably sympathetic crowd at the NBWA confab, Brewbound reported . “What good is a statutory right to cure a breach if a supplier can bypass it simply by waiting another thirty days?” asks Ashley Brandt, the Tucker Ellis LLP attorney behind the prolific trade blotter Libation Law Blog, in a post about the CDC brew -haha. “A supplier that cannot establish a breach—or knows the distributor will fix it—could simply choose the ninety-day route. What happens during those additional thirty days that makes a performing distributor’s continued contractual compliance irrelevant?” It’s a good question. For CDC, it may wind up being the question. And with the pendulum of power lately swinging back towards big suppliers, you can bet the rest of the middle tier will be hanging on the answer. New beverage launches: Festive flavours meet premiumisation

La industria de bebidas está acelerado lanzamientos de productos basados en premiumización, conveniencia e innovación ligada a ocasiones específicas. Marcas utilizan ediciones limitadas, nuevos formatos y releases estacionales para generar demanda y prueba de producto en un mercado saturado. Las tendencias de consumo saludable siguen moldeando el desarrollo, con énfasis en reducción de azúcar, ingredientes limpios y propuestas simples, impactando especialmente en refrescos y bebidas funcionales.

Texto completo del artículo
Brands across the beverage industry are using premiumisation, convenience and occasion-led innovation to attract consumers in an increasingly competitive market. Limited editions, new formats and seasonal releases are helping producers create excitement and drive trial across a range of categories. Health-conscious consumption trends continue to influence product development, particularly in soft drinks and functional beverages, where reduced sugar, clean-label ingredients and simple propositions remain key. At the same time, wine, beer and spirits brands are highlighting provenance, craftsmanship and unique production methods to strengthen their premium credentials and appeal to consumers seeking more distinctive drinking experiences. Remedy has launched Merry Cherry, a limited-edition festive kombucha designed to tap into seasonal demand for lower-sugar soft drinks. The no-sugar kombucha blends cherry with notes of white chocolate, raspberry and biscuit, contains five calories per serve, and is available in a 700ml sharing bottle through Sainsbury’s, Ocado and the brand’s website. The company is also donating 10p from every bottle sold to homelessness and food poverty charity Social Bite. The launch comes as the UK kombucha category reaches £47.2m and grows 14% year-on-year, according to Remedy. The brand says it holds a 34% market share and generates £16.1m in retail sales. Bandero Premium Tequila has expanded into the aged tequila category with the launch of Bandero Reposado, its first barrel-aged expression. Made from 100% Blue Weber agave, the tequila is matured for just under 12 months in Hungarian, French and American white oak barrels before being blended by master distiller Lee Palleschi. The three-year development process was designed to balance savoury spice notes from the European oak with vanilla and caramel characteristics from the American oak. Produced in small batches of around 26 barrels, the additive-free tequila is bottled at 40% ABV and joins the brand’s existing Blanco and coffee-infused Bandero Café expressions. Bandero Reposado is now available through selected retailers, with wider distribution planned later in the year. Chapel Down has unveiled Kit’s Coty Blanc de Noirs 2021, the first single-vineyard Blanc de Noirs in its luxury Kit’s Coty collection and what the English wine producer describes as its most significant product launch in a decade. Made exclusively from hand-picked Pinot Noir grown at the Kit’s Coty vineyard on the Kent Downs, the sparkling wine has been produced in a limited run of just 1,100 bottles and joins the brand’s portfolio of premium single-vineyard wines. Wild fermented in oak and aged on lees for four years before release, the 2021 vintage offers strawberry and raspberry notes alongside hints of vanilla and a creamy finish. According to head winemaker Josh Donaghay-Spire, the release was created to showcase a different expression of the vineyard and the quality potential of English Pinot Noir. Theakston’s Brewery has expanded its ale portfolio with the launch of Special Reserve, a new 6.5% ABV beer and the strongest ale in the Masham brewer’s range. Rolling out in 500ml bottles, the product will debut with a national listing in Morrisons, with Waitrose set to follow in November as the brewery looks to strengthen its presence in the off-trade. Brewed using Fuggle hops, Special Reserve is described as a full-bodied traditional ale with subtle fruity notes. Theakston’s says the launch responds to renewed consumer interest in classic British ale styles with higher ABVs and richer flavour profiles. Chairman Simon Theakston said the new beer combines the brewery’s heritage and brewing expertise with evolving consumer demand for more distinctive premium ales. Eden Mill has expanded its seasonal spirits portfolio with the launch of Winter in St Andrews, a limited-edition gin designed specifically for Negroni serves. Following the release of Summer in St Andrews earlier this year, the new expression combines blood orange, cinnamon and clove to create a winter-inspired flavour profile. Production has been capped at just 500 bottles, reinforcing the small-batch, seasonal positioning of the range. The distillery is targeting the growing aperitivo and cocktail occasion, recommending the gin as the base for a festive Negroni made with equal parts gin, sweet vermouth and Campari. Senior Brand Manager Hamish Maclean said the launch builds on consumer demand for seasonal releases that capture different moments in St Andrews, with the flavour profile developed to offer a warmer, rounder take on the classic cocktail serve. Priced at £32, Winter in St Andrews is available from Eden Mill’s distillery, through its website and via selected specialist retailers in St Andrews. Aspen Vodka has expanded its packaging line-up with the launch of a new 375ml bottle, positioned for hotel minibars, travel occasions and smaller at-home serves. The mid-sized format joins the brand’s existing 1L, 750ml and 50ml offerings, and is rolling out across retail and hospitality channels in the US. Produced at a carbon-negative distillery in Aspen, Colorado, the vodka is made from Colorado red winter wheat and water sourced from the Roaring Fork River. Aspen Vodka says it achieved triple-digit growth in 2025 and is on track for similar growth in 2026, despite declining US spirits volumes. The brand also claims to have been the best-selling spirit by volume at Yankee Stadium this season and has expanded partnerships across a number of major US sports teams. The new 375ml bottle is available through the brand’s website, selected retailers and hospitality venues nationwide. Chicago-based Bare Brew is expanding its retail presence with a shelf-stable cold brew coffee made from just two ingredients: Honduran Arabica coffee and filtered water. Packaged in a 12oz can, the drink contains 320mg of natural caffeine, 20 calories and zero sugar, while requiring no refrigeration. Founded by Miles Schwartz, the brand has secured more than 40 retail placements across four US states and recently added new listings in Michigan and Florida. Bare Brew is available direct-to-consumer and through retail partners, with an RRP of $3.99.

The history of franchise law is long, winding, and deathly boring, but the upshot is that beer distributors are nearly unique in the protections afforded to them by most states’ laws. The most crucial of these is the so-called “just cause” provision, which requires brewers to cite a legally defensible breach of their agreement with the house they’re trying to terminate. This provision is written into most states’ codes because wholesalers marched on statehouses to make it so in the ‘60s, ‘70s, and ‘80s. Critics argue it harms competition by making it difficult for good brewers to bail on bad distributors; boosters insist it protects competition by making it difficult for bad brewers to strong-arm good distributors. But it’s there. The protective power of this provision was tested most famously in Florida roughly a quarter-century ago, and distributors came away with the whip hand. In Maris Distributing v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., a wholesaler fought the brewer tooth and nail for a decade over a termination it believed unjust. The family — the kin of baseball great Roger Maris, who’d been bestowed an A-B house by Gussie Busch after finishing out his Hall of Fame career with the St. Louis Cardinals — won a $120 million settlement in 2005. As the mantra goes: there are 50 different states, and 50 different three-tier systems. A version of the just-cause clause is on the books in Colorado. But it’s a weird version. It dictates a brewer hoping to terminate a distributor has to cite “alleged noncompliance” and allow the offending firm 60 days to “cure” the infraction. If the distributor doesn’t shape up in that time, then the brewer can ship out. That’s all right at the top of C.R.S. § 44-3-408 . But in subsection three, the code appears to contradict itself, noting “the supplier shall have the right to terminate an agreement with a wholesaler at any time by giving the wholesaler at least ninety days’ written notice by certified mail.” To me, a not-lawyer, , the Colorado code appears to say that a brewer must have just cause to terminate a distributor after 60 days, but can terminate for any reason after 90 days. You will not be shocked to learn that this is not how CDC’s actual lawyers read the text. “Under Constellation’s novel interpretation of Colorado law, its termination will become final after 90 days,” the firm’s legal team wrote in a September 25 filing. “In the interim, there is a likelihood that CDC will face cascading terminations from its other suppliers.” For a trade that has long grasped the power of a united political bloc, CDC’s existential fight is cause for alarm. Whether the suppliers’ assessments of Colorado’s code are innovative, or illegal, is a matter for the judicial system. Research from UK alcohol education charity Drinkaware and hospitality insights consultancy KAM found that people taking GLP-1 medication reported drinking less frequently after starting treatment. The study, Drinking Differently: How GLP-1 Medications Impact Alcohol Consumption, found that drinking frequency fell by 29%, from an average of 3.1 days per week to 2.2 days. Weekly alcohol consumption declined by 16%, while spending on alcohol for home consumption fell by 22%. The research also found that 34% of users reported reduced cravings for alcohol, while 18% experienced discomfort, including nausea, when drinking. The effects may also extend beyond individual users. Almost half of UK adults who had never used GLP-1 medication said they knew someone who had taken it. Of those, 30% said they drank less when socialising with that person, while 16% were more likely to choose low- or no-alcohol options. However, the research reflects reported changes among users and does not establish how much of the shift is directly attributable to the medication rather than wider changes in drinking habits. Drinkaware and KAM found that GLP-1 users spent 30% less on alcohol during visits to pubs, bars and restaurants, alongside the 22% decline in weekly spending on alcohol for home consumption. US purchasing data points to a similar trend. Research by market research firm Circana, reported by Reuters, found that households with a GLP-1 user spent around 7% less per month on alcohol than households without a user after two years. Spending on beer was approximately 13.5% lower. While the US figures do not establish that GLP-1 medication alone caused the difference, they offer a further indication of the potential commercial implications for drinks manufacturers. For beer, wine and spirits producers, the question is whether changing consumption habits could affect category demand and accelerate the shift towards lower-alcohol alternatives. Fue Gerson Añaños quien recibió el Premio a la Excelencia en EMA Empresas Familiares 2026, organizado por PwC Perú, G de Gestión y la Asociación de Empresas Familiares (AEF). Desde su posición, mira hacia los próximos años de una empresa que construyó su expansión internacional -dice- “con recursos limitados y una apuesta por llevar nuestros productos a nuevos mercados”. “La decisión más determinante en nuestra historia fue atrevernos a salir del Perú apenas un año después de haber llegado a Lima”, recuerda. Para él, esa decisión resume el espíritu emprendedor de la familia: “Somos una familia que no necesitó recursos económicos ni favores políticos para crear su propia realidad”. De cara al 2030, algunos retos son distintos, asegura. El consumidor cambia más rápido y busca productos para diferentes estilos de vida y ocasiones de consumo, además de prestar mayor atención a la sostenibilidad. Frente a ello, la estrategia de AJE -comenta- seguirá combinando la expansión en los mercados donde ya opera, la evolución de su portafolio y la exploración de nuevos negocios. “No se trataba solo de llevar productos, sino de entender a cada consumidor y adaptarnos a él”, dice el ejecutivo. Para los próximos años, anotó, “la prioridad de la empresa será ampliar el uso de la inteligencia artificial y los datos para tomar decisiones, mejorar su eficiencia y responder a los cambios del mercado”, dijo. La diversificación es parte de esa estrategia de crecimiento para el 2030. Tiendas 3A -que al cierre del 2025 tenía presencia en el 80% de los distritos de Lima-, su incursión en el formato hard discount, es uno de los ejemplos de cómo el grupo busca entrar en nuevos negocios, más allá de las bebidas, señaló Gerson Añaños. En su portafolio también figuran propuestas recientes como Hey Fit, Cielo con Antioxidantes, Dilyte y VOLT, con las que busca atender distintas preferencias de consumo. Añaños destaca Hey Fit como una de las propuestas recientes que ha registrado un crecimiento relevante desde su lanzamiento. El crecimiento internacional también ha exigido cambios en la organización. AJE continúa consolidando su gobierno corporativo y ha establecido un modelo de gestión —al que denomina Partenón— que articula su cultura, los pilares estratégicos y los objetivos de cada responsable. La compañía también ha institucionalizado, según Añaños, decisiones sobre asuntos como sostenibilidad e innovación mediante comités globales y locales, metas e indicadores. A ello se suma una política de movilidad internacional del talento: entre 2021 y 2026, reporta 115 movimientos internacionales de colaboradores. Según Añaños, “Perú es el principal exportador de líderes hacia las operaciones del grupo en otros países”. “El gran desafío ha sido crecer en estructura sin perder la agilidad y la cercanía de una empresa familiar. Ese equilibrio es, quizá, lo que más cuidamos”, explica. La empresa cuenta con alrededor de 15,000 trabajadores directos e indirectos, mientras que no más de 15 miembros de la familia Añaños trabajan en el grupo. Para los próximos cinco años, su estrategia de talento humano buscará atraer, desarrollar y potenciar profesionales de alto rendimiento y líderes alineados con la cultura de la compañía. El Premio a la Excelencia de EMA Empresas Familiares 2026 reconoció el desempeño de Grupo AJE en distintas dimensiones de gestión. La empresa destacó en tres categorías: visión transformadora y resiliencia; integridad y valor sostenible; y unidad y liderazgo con propósito. Entre las iniciativas que la compañía destaca está Amayu, su marca de bebidas elaboradas con superfrutos amazónicos, como camu camu y aguaje. La propuesta se basa en una idea: generar valor económico a partir del bosque en pie. Añaños vincula el concepto de excelencia con una influencia que marcó a la empresa desde sus inicios: la industria japonesa y la filosofía de mejora continua que conocieron en los años noventa, así como las ideas de Miguel Ángel Cornejo. Desde sus inicios, cuenta, la familia se propuso ubicar a AJE entre las 20 empresas más importantes del país, pese a no contar con grandes recursos materiales ni financieros. Bajo el liderazgo de Ángel Añaños, presidente del directorio, la compañía buscó convertir esa ambición en una operación internacional. “Hemos sido capaces de hacer mucho más con mucho menos, de borrar las fronteras internacionales y de desarrollar nuevos productos que atiendan a las necesidades de los consumidores”, sostiene. Para él, la excelencia no es una meta que se alcanza de una vez, sino una forma de gestionar el negocio sin perder de vista el origen de la empresa y las oportunidades que busca generar.
