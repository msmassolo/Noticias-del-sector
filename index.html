Coca-Cola Co’s new Topo Chico Colección signals a new era for sparkling water

Coca-Cola lanza Topo Chico Colección a partir del 5 de octubre, reposicionando el agua mineral con gas como categoría premium con sabores innovadores y potencial para ocasiones de consumo alcohol-free. La estrategia busca elevar el agua mineral gasificada más allá de alternativa básica zero-calorie, incorporando beneficios de salud y experiencias de sabor diferenciadas. Este movimiento refleja la apuesta de la compañía en premiumización dentro del segmento de bebidas sin calorías, un mercado en expansión donde Coca-Cola refuerza su portafolio de bebidas funcionales.

Texto completo del artículo
This week’s debut of The Coca-Cola Co’s new Topo Chico Colección signals a new chapter for sparkling water that moves the category beyond a basic, and sometimes boring, zero-calorie alternative to soda to become a platform for premiumization, bolder flavors, added health benefits and alcohol-free drinking occasions. Hitting store shelves beginning Oct. 5, Topo Chico Colección promises to bring “the brand’s distinctive taste experience to a convenient new format,” by blending a premium carbonated filtered water that is “crafted with signature minerals for flavor” with the convenience and elegance of a slim 12-ounce can in 8-packs. “By bringing the iconic Topo Chico taste experience to a premium canned format, we’re creating new ways for consumers to enjoy Topo Chico in more places, while staying rooted in the heritage and character that have made the brand a beloved favorite for generations,” Sue Lynne Cha, VP Water & Tea, North America at The Coca‑Cola Company, said in a statement. The launch comes at a time when the sparkling water category is growing fast and becoming increasingly crowded – placing pressure on companies to notably differentiate their offerings to secure or maintain market share and cultural relevance. Valued at $47.75 billion in 2025, the global sparkling water market is projected to grow at a compound annual rate of 12.4% to reach $136.58 billion by 2034, according to Fortune Business Insights. The report attributes that growth in part to rising demand for healthier alternatives to sugar sweetened beverages and alcohol but without compromising flavor, experience or cultural cachet. A flurry of new launches in the past 12 months – including most recently Topo Chico Colección – illustrate how brands are rising to meet these demands and secure their space on store shelves. One way that sparkling water brands are evolving is by increasingly promoting provenance, mineral content, fizz level and design to justify a higher price point than basic carbonated water can command. In the case of Topo Chico Colección, The Coca-Cola Co. says it is adding “signature minerals” to carbonated filtered water to create a flavor “inspired by the iconic taste experience” of the brand’s 130-year-old flagship product, which is sourced from Monterrey, Mexico. It also touts on the product website the “robust carbonation” of the new line – promising it delivers “vibrant bubbles from start to finish.” The callouts harken those made by Iris USA in late August when it launched Mount Fuji Sparkling Water, which it described at the time as “sourced and bottled near the base of Mount Fuji in Japan,” where “naturally occurring minerals contribute to its clean taste.” The water, available in 16.9-fluid-ounce plastic bottles, is labeled as “ultra fizzy,” which the company said is “designed for consumers who prefer a stronger level of carbonation” and “lively, persistent bubbles.” Nomina, which launched in the US in December 2025, also is promoting the provenance and pop of its fizzy water – noting it sources its “soft spring water” from Japan’s Southern Alps where the water “travels through layers of earth in the Oi River basin” to emerge with an “exceptionally pure and gentle profile,” which is then infused with “fine, extra carbonation,” according to the company’s listing on Faire. These examples underscore how brands are positioning the water – not just the flavor – as the differentiator, including where it comes from, how it feels to drink and what is in it. Sparkling water brands also distinguish their products with flavor profiles that suggest uses or experiences beyond simple refreshment. One increasingly popular use is as a zero-calorie mixer with alcohol or as a standalone mocktail. PepsiCo launched bubly Berry Sparkle Sangria and Paradise Paloma in early September as limited time offerings designed to build on the brand’s previous mocktail-inspired flavors, including bubly Bellini Bliss, Tiki Sunrise and Mojito Breeze. At the launch, PepsiCo noted that half of all Americans are trying to drink less alcohol, and its selection of mocktail-inspired bubly flavors reimagine classic favorites without the alcohol. The line also is free of artificial sweeteners and calories – helping to keep it squarely in the sparkling water category even as the lines blur between beverage types. Several brands are also using indulgent and nostalgic flavors to give sparkling water some of the emotional appeal of snacks, desserts and higher-calorie drinks. For example, Waterloo collaborated with cooking personality Guy Fieri to launch in May a trio of LTOs that riff on high-calorie summer treats, including Apple Pie a la Mode, Coconut Lime Cooler and Root Beer Float. While Waterloo touted the drinks as enjoyable “straight from a chilled can,” it also offered mocktail recipes that used them as a key ingredient – underscoring the multiple use occasions afforded by the flavor profiles. Likewise, Talking Rain Beverage Company and Mars brought to market in January a line of zero-sugar sparkling water in four candy-inspired flavors co-branded under the Sparkling Ice brand with Life Savers. The launched followed the “runaway success of Sparkling Ice Starburst last year,” which Talking Rain said at the time “provided just how much consumers are interested in seeing their favorite candy brands brought together with Sparkling Ice. These launches show how the appeal of familiar flavors, be they cocktail, candy or childhood treat, make an otherwise utilitarian beverage feel more experiential. In a bid to get ahead in the sparkling water arms race, several brands are layering health benefits consumers want on top of the bold flavors for which the category is known. Among those offering fun and function is Maison Perrier, which in February launched its first sweetened sparkling water line: French Kiss. Four SKUs blend “the signature grand bubbles of Maison Perrier with real fruit juice and prebiotics,” the company said. Available in Blackberry & Lemon, Peach & Cherry, Mango & Coconut and Raspberry & Lime, each can includes 6 g of fiber and less than 1 gram of added sugar. For Wildwonder, adding functional benefits to sparkling water doesn’t mean sacrificing flavor or indulgence. In January it launched Pink Pomelo Limeade and in August it launched Apple Yuzu. Both are infused with 1 billion live probiotics, 5 grams of prebiotic dietary fiber and botanical ingredients to support consumers’ health goals. Gut health was a gateway for many sparkling beverages to make a functional play, but more recent launches by Beyond Meat and oHy underscore the opportunity to support other health goals. Beyond Immerse, a sparkling protein water with fiber, antioxidants and electrolytes, launched in June. In September oHy Hydrogen Sparkling Water debuted at Target with four fruit-forward flavors that it says combine magnesium and purified water to “deliver antioxidant benefits at the cellular level” to support natural energy, focus and recovery. For brands, the appeal is that functional ingredients can give consumers another reason to pay a premium by moving sparkling water from a zero-calorie refreshment toward a beverage with a specific job to do. The sparkling water category’s push to combine health and experience also is opening the door to a new consumer: children. Several brands in the past year launched sparkling waters explicitly targeting children. Among them is NoBiggie, which in May introduced four carbonated drinks made with fruit juice and puree, which the brand says has 75% less sugar than the average juice box. Oodles of Doodles, an established European kids’ beverage brand, entered the US marketing in December 2025 with a line of low-sugar flavored waters designed to make hydration more playful. The brand packages its fizzy water in small cans featuring outlined designs children can color. These launches suggest that the same values reshaping the sparkling water category for adults now apply to children as well, including a desire for less sugar without compromising flavor, fun or identity. All together, these trends illustrate how sparkling water is becoming a diverse format that brands can use to chase almost any consumer need, including premium refreshment and cocktail alternatives to functional benefits and children’s treats. However, as the category becomes more competitive and fragmented, brands will need to give consumers a compelling reason to buy the same product again.

How airports are driving trial for beverage brands

Los aeropuertos se posicionan como canales estratégicos de prueba y distribución para marcas de bebidas, con creciente receptividad de viajeros hacia productos innovadores con propuesta de wellness. OTG, operador de retail aeroportuario, reporta que el 53% de viajeros Gen Z busca probar nuevas bebidas, convirtiéndose en demografía clave. Esta tendencia representa una oportunidad significativa para lanzamientos de productos y expansión de distribución en canales de alto tráfico con consumidor predispuesto a la innovación.

Leer nota original Ver en español
Texto completo del artículo
Airports evolved from traditional transportation hubs into retail and hospitality channels where travelers are increasingly receptive to new products, especially with a wellness angle, according to an expert. Younger travelers who are eager to try new products are increasingly a target demographic for airport retailers, added Melinda Smith, vice president of retail markets at OTG, an airport and hospitality operator that helps brands with merchandising and tasting events “Approximately 53% of Gen-Z and Millennials now fly more than three times per year,” Smith said, adding that these consumers are “highly receptive to discovering new brands, products and trends.” Younger shoppers’ behavior has influenced how airport retailers curate assortments on shelves. For example, OTG’s CIBO markets “continuously pulse products in and out of the assortment” on a monthly basis allowing operators to respond quickly to emerging trends and changing consumer demand instead of relying on annual or twice-yearly resets. Smith suggested the travel environment itself creates favorable conditions for discovery and trial due to surging airport traffic in recent years, TSA liquid restrictions and longer dwell times in airports. “Airports are no longer just a transportation hub to get from point A to point B but are increasingly commerce to the latest retail goods, entertainment and culinary experiences,” she said. Consumer preferences toward health, wellness and functionality across beverages are increasingly mirrored in airport retail. “Consumer beverage preferences are continuing to shift away from traditional carbonated soft drinks toward products that offer perceived health, wellness, functionality and convenience benefits,” Smith said. The most popular beverage categories are flavored sparkling water, bottled water, ready-to-drink coffee, enhanced hydration products and protein beverages. “The most significant beverage trends are increasingly centered around functional wellness, mindful low- and no-alcohol choices, and more complex flavor profiles,” Smith said. Interest in functional beverages continue to grow as consumers look for products with “energy, hydration, focus, recovery and other lifestyle benefits,” she explained. Energy drinks have grown about 8% year over year and now outpace traditional soft drinks, while overall functional beverage sales are experiencing double-digit annual growth, she added. She also pointed to growing opportunities for high-protein beverages and single-serve formats as the use of GLP-1 medications influences eating and drinking habits. “High-protein and functional beverages are particularly well positioned within this shift as consumers increasingly look for products that provide a specific benefit in addition to convenience and refreshment,” Smith said. Consumers are also seeking more adventurous flavor profiles, including combinations that blend sweetness, acidity, spice and heat, Smith said. Examples include “mango-habanero, guava-jalapeño, and pineapple-chili,” alongside tropical fruit blends, citrus-forward drinks and botanical beverages. For airport retailers, maintaining flexibility is essential because travelers may be seeking “functional benefits, higher-protein options, or unique flavors that provide an experience beyond traditional beverages,” Smith explained. While airport retail shares some of the same category management foundations as traditional retail, the operating environment is considerably more complex, according to Smith, who spent 23 years working in conventional grocery and retail channels. She noted that pricing often aligns with local market expectations through airport street-pricing requirements, where vendors cannot charge more than off-airport prices for food, drinks and retail but are allowed a small percentage markup for operating costs. Texto completo del artículo
It’s a hard time to run a beverage program. Costs are escalating due in part to an unhappy blend of tariffs, supply chain issues, and general inflation. The wholesale sector of the drinks industry is in a tremendous amount of flux following the collapse of Republic National Distributing Company (RNDC) over the last year, meaning that keeping steady supply of product is trickier than ever. Add to that concerns over labor costs, a public that keeps hearing that no one drinks any more, and oh yeah, a bewildering array of products and product categories that could easily overwhelm even the most seasoned professional. Yet savvy and experienced buyers and beverage directors are continuing to find ways to navigate the push and pull of excellent hospitality and the realities of business. Strategies abound for dealing with these challenges: some rely on economies of scale, or the ability to store large quantities of product, while others are contingent on knowing your clientele and how to deliver the experience they crave, even when the bottle they want most is either unavailable or has skyrocketed in price. It’s absolutely not news to anyone that the cost of basically everything in beverage alcohol has increased, often dramatically, over the last half-decade or so. “I cannot sell Premier Cru Burgundy for the same price we could five years ago,” laments Will Jones, wine director at The Hope Farm and Little Bird in Fairhope, Ala. “So for something approachable, I may sell a Hautes-Côtes de Beaune or Hautes-Côtes de Nuits, or this really beautiful Bourgogne Rouge or Bourgogne Blanc.” For the guest who maybe dreamed of a wine from an iconic region but is now suffering from sticker shock, “I can give someone a northern Italian or an Austrian or a German wine with as much of a story as I would for a Grand Cru Burgundy,” explains Felipe de Assis Villela, beverage director at Bluepoint Hospitality Group in Easton, Md. “I could give you a Grand Cru German wine for under $200, or you can get a Grand Cru Burgundy for $1,800, which, I’m not gonna stop you from doing, but that’s not what everyone’s able to do.” While those substitutes can still excite guests, no one is thrilled about paying more and more money for their drinks. That’s compounded by pushing margins or markups ever higher, either because their clientele can and will pay the higher price or in a desperate attempt to wring a bit more profit out of what may be the main profit center of the property. Yet when the price of a bottle of wine, say, has potentially already been bumped up by the producer, the importer, and the distributor, an aggressive restaurant markup of 200-300 percent can be absolutely punishing to guests. “I’m not so obsessed with a percentage mark. That’s a luxury I have in my position,” admits de Assis Villela. A mix of common sense and a desire to actually move certain wines to help him set prices as opposed to pushing prices as high as possible has resulted in a program that maybe doesn’t boast a gaudy profit margin but does serve both guest and restaurant. “I’m still making money, but the percentage may not look like what I had to do on Long Island, which, you know, apologies to all my guests back there,” he says. One of the more straightforward ways to be more profitable is to purchase in larger quantities. While different states and different distributors can have wildly different approaches to volume discounts, most of the time if you’re willing to buy a bunch of cases of whatever, you’ll get a much better price. It can also insulate against product shortages, but of course it also requires something that not every restaurant or bar can have: storage space. Being able to place product at multiple different outlets within the same property or even the same city can also make bulk buying more viable. “Sometimes distributors will set up quantity discounts for us,” notes Amanda Reed, director of beverage at E3 Co. Restaurant Group in Seattle. “We’ve gotta buy five cases at a time or 10 cases at a time — that’s usually our limit for storage in [most of our] properties. But if we’re coming up on a really good deal and there is plenty of supply, I might just try to work something out where we commit to X amount, but we’re only gonna bring it in five cases at a time.” Part of the challenge is the tremendous upheaval in the wholesale category over the last couple of years. The complete collapse of RNDC from the second-largest beverage wholesaler to Chapter 11 bankruptcy in less than two years has played no small part, with tariffs contributing not just to higher costs but also more challenges getting products into the U.S. in the first place. Time, too, is at a premium, especially in a landscape where beverage professionals are often expected to perform any number of additional administrative and managerial tasks, even when that’s not exactly what attracted them to the job in the first place. “When you first become a buyer, you’re so excited to sit down with reps and get to be this figure of authority,” says Johannus Grevelink, beverage director for José Andrés Group. “The reality is that if you open up my inbox every day, I have 40 to 60 emails from people about their product and how it could be a great fit at José Andrés Group or at one of our outlets with the name spelled wrong. Sifting through all that — I don’t know anyone that could have the time to do that.” It’s relatively easy to understand much of what drives up prices at restaurants and bars, but there are plenty of less-obvious costs as well. Maintaining consistent placements doesn’t just make life easier for the beverage director or allow for lower prices on the product itself, it cuts down on training and printing costs, too. In an era of ever-increasing labor costs, gathering staff to taste and train on new products is expensive. But that investment — in time, money, and effort — can also help new products thrive, especially in light of the alternative. “If I place a new spirit, I can send an email to a GM who will forward it to the AGM, who can print the tech sheet for pre-shift and talk to the staff about it,” says Grevelink. “Realistically, now that information has come through the supplier to the buyer to the GM to the AGM to maybe like a floor manager who’s doing pre-shift on a Tuesday. My passion for that bottle is not gonna translate.” Regular staff education and training can help move product, of course, but paying that hourly wage across the entire service team has never been so pricey. Menu changes can also come with significant costs. “We don’t print our menus in house, we outsource,” says Reed. “So I have to be really selective about what I choose to put in the cocktails; I confirm that the inventory and availability is gonna be there. Once I’ve designed my cocktails, there’s a lot of back and forth between me and my reps about supply, because I don’t want to have to change one ingredient in the middle of a season when nothing else on the menu needs to be changed.” This, again, is where larger buys (where possible) can come in handy. A program that changes less frequently can get by with less staff education, fewer menu changes, and require less admin time from frequently-overworked beverage directors and other managers. Yet every such person interviewed for this piece bemoaned that they were not able to spend as much time on some of those aspects of the job — staff training and increasing their own product knowledge most of all. Just as the relationship with the guest is crucial to a great restaurant experience, everyone I spoke to was emphatic that their relationships with producers and suppliers was key to their work, both in terms of quality and in terms of job satisfaction. Cultivating and nourishing them is an active and intentional choice. “When we first opened at the Hope Farm, I was unable to get the large majority of any allocations that I wanted because we didn’t have that history,” says Jones. “Now thankfully, we do. I love meeting new winemakers. I love maintaining our connections with the ones that we love.” For Jones, it’s about far more than just getting access to product or even having something new and exciting to offer to guests. “I’m here to create a relationship with the producer, to host them in town and open a beautiful bottle of wine when they’re here, and to continue to build that relationship.” That kind of relationship also just makes the day-to-day operation of a restaurant smoother. “I’m trying to avoid constantly running out of stuff and having to find a new vendor to fill a placement,” says James Baugh, beverage director for TMC Hospitality. “We want to build a long-term partnership with someone who supports us and has available product for us. And sometimes there’s just days where you blow through some product and it’s nice to have someone you know to ask, ‘hey, can you drop me a couple of things?’” The overwhelming sense from beverage directors right now is that their job involves a consistent fight to keep their heads above water, to make sure that their programs can still meet guest needs while being leaner, more cost-conscious, and more resistant to staff and product turnover than ever before. They’re still passionate and motivated, but they’re also feeling the pressure and struggling to deal with it.
