Big Food warns growing scrutiny of ultra-processed foods could hit sales and costs

Las grandes empresas de alimentos procesados advierten en documentos de la SEC que el creciente escrutinio regulatorio sobre ultraprocesados (definiciones legales, legislación estatal, política federal y litigios) representa un riesgo material para sus negocios. El impacto potencial incluye reducción de demanda, incremento de costos operacionales y nuevos requisitos de cumplimiento normativo. Esta tendencia afecta directamente a la industria de bebidas de consumo masivo, donde muchos productos enfrentan presiones similares de reformulación y repositionamiento estratégico en mercados regulados.

Growing scrutiny of ultra-processed foods is emerging as a significant business risk for some of the biggest packaged food companies, which warn in financial filings that efforts to define UPFs, new state legislation, federal policy and pending litigation could dampen demand, increase costs and trigger new regulatory requirements. Among the companies warning in filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission of negative fallout from the ongoing debate about ultra-processed foods are Conagra, Kraft Heinz and JM Smucker, which caution in recent financial filings about ongoing or potential litigation alleging UPFs cause negative health effects. PepsiCo and The Campbell’s Co also warn investors that negative consumer perceptions about UPFs could hurt demand, while reformulation or relabeling efforts in response could raise costs. Among the most explicit warnings is one in Campbell’s lates 10-K, filed with the SEC Sept. 24 and documented Aug. 2. “Heightened scrutiny of ‘ultra-processed’ foods, including policy proposals outlined in reports by the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) Commission as well as state legislative activity, could result in new definitions, labeling requirements, marketing restrictions or reformulation mandates that increase our compliance costs or adversely affect consumer demand for certain of our products,” it says in the <a href="https://investor.thecampbellscompany.com/sec-filings/sec-filing/10-k/0000016732-26-000026" target="_blank" rel="">filing</a>. The MAHA Commission’s May 2025 <a href="https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/WH-The-MAHA-Report-Assessment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="">“Make Our Children Healthy Again”</a> assessment <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/09/10/maha-child-health-report-calls-for-reforms-lacks-specifics/" target="_blank" rel="">identifies ultra-processed foods as a potential driver of childhood chronic disease</a>, arguing that their increased consumption is associated with “nutrient depletion, increased caloric intake and exposure to harmful additives.” While the report was presented as based on scientific research, subsequent reviews found citation problems, including incorrect references, broken links and citations to studies that did not exist. HHS acknowledged the errors, but characterized them as formatting and citation problems and maintained the report’s conclusions. The Commission subsequently called for changes that could directly affect food manufacturers, including dietary guidance to “eat real food” and avoid “highly processed foods,” as well as federal action to define ultra-processed foods. Following the report, FDA and USDA issued a formal request for information on ultra-processed foods. Originally, the agency’s intention appeared to be promulgating a rulemaking to define ultra-processed food, but that effort appears to be downgraded to a white paper, <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/08/06/fda-upf-white-paper-could-reshape-food-policy/" target="_blank" rel="">which FDA submitted to the White House</a> Office of Management and Budget’s <a href="https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eoDetails?rrid=1495413" target="_blank" rel="">Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs for review on Aug. 3</a>. While FDA’s effort to define UPFs slowly moves forward and, as a white paper that will lack the enforcement teeth of a regulation, some states are advancing laws that include more binding definitions. Among them is California, which <a href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/09/01/california-passes-non-upf-food-certification-bill/" target="_blank" rel="">recently passed legislation that would create a voluntary ‘non-ultraprocessed certified’ state seal for food packaging</a>, and broadly defines UPFs as any food or beverage containing certain functional additives, including emulsifiers, stabilizers, colors, flavor enhancers and non-nutritive sweeteners, as well as products exceeding specified thresholds for saturated fat, sodium and added sugars. California Gov. Gavin Newsom has until Sept. 30 to sign the legislation into law or veto it. The MAHA Commission also takes aim at several food additives that it says studies link to “increased risk of mental disorders, ADHD, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome and even carcinogenic effects.” The MAHA Commission’s scrutiny of these ingredients is fueling reformulation efforts, including voluntary removal of certain synthetic food dyes by CPG manufacturers, <a href="https://www.thecampbellscompany.com/newsroom/news/the-campbells-company-removes-fdc-colors-from-national-portfolio/" target="_blank" rel="">including Campbell’s</a>. While the extent and impact of efforts by MAHA and some states to restrict UPF consumption and spur reformulation remains uncertain, Campbell’s and other CPG players warn they could impact their businesses and financial results. “We are continuing to monitor changes in laws and regulations that affect the food industry and evaluate their impact on our business, financial condition and results of our operations,” Campbell’s writes in its in 10-K filing. It adds the exposure goes beyond UPF and colors and could include, but is not limited to, products containing bio-engineered ingredients or ingredients previously determined to be Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS). Likewise, it adds that Campbell’s faces exposure to changing labeling and packaging laws at the state and federal level, “along with related changes in consumer expectations and behavior.” Regardless of future regulatory outcomes based on the MAHA Commission’s report or state legislation, the ongoing public conversation about UPFs already is changing how consumers think about food safety and the products they buy. If negative consumer perceptions about UPFs, whether or not scientifically-grounded, are left unchecked by a CPG company, a brand’s reputation and sales could be harmed. Among the companies warning of this risk is PepsiCo, which tells investors in its <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/77476/000007747626000007/pep-20251227.htm" target="_blank" rel="">fiscal year 2025 10-K filed in February</a> that “our reputation or brand image has in the past been, and could in the future be, adversely impacted by a variety of factors,” including, “concerns or perceptions regarding whether certain of our products are ‘ultra-processed,’ contain certain ingredients or substances or otherwise contribute to obesity and other health conditions or an increase in public health costs.” PepsiCo also warns that state efforts to limit marketing and sale of “so-called ‘ultra-processed’ foods’” could reduce consumption and sales – adversely impacting PepsiCo’s business and financial performance. For example, it cites a Texas law effective Jan. 1, 2027, that will require warning labels on products containing certain ingredients, including artificial colors. Other “jurisdictions have imposed or are considering imposing color-coded labeling requirements where colors such as red, yellow and green are used to indicate various levels of a particular ingredient, such as sugar, sodium or saturated fat, in products, and other jurisdictions, including the United States, have imposed or are considering imposing restrictions on so-called “ultra-processed” foods,” it adds in the financial filing. It adds, “The imposition or proposed imposition of additional limitations on the marketing or sale of our products has in the past reduced and could continue to reduce overall consumption of our products, lead to negative publicity or leave consumers with the perception that our products do not meet their health and wellness needs.” The risks posed by negative consumer perceptions of UPFs go beyond just reputational to include litigation that could lead to fines, lawsuits, taxes, additional regulation and operational changes, according to other CPG players. For example, <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/23217/000110465926083905/tmb-20260531x10k.htm" target="_blank" rel="">Conagra warns in its Fiscal Year 2026 10-K filed in July</a> that it faces litigation alleging some of its products should be considered ‘ultra-processed’ and consumption of them allegedly causes “negative health impacts.” While Conagra says it cannot predict the outcome of these or other legal proceedings, it does not expect them to have a material adverse effect on its financial conditions, results of operations or business. Kraft Heinz, on the other hand, cautions similar allegations against it could change how it does business. In its <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1637459/000163745926000009/khc-20251227.htm" target="_blank" rel="">Fiscal Year 2025 10-K filed in February</a>, Kraft Heinz discloses it faces “pending litigation alleging that certain of our products are ‘ultra-processed’ and consuming them causes adverse health impacts.” It notes that it “strongly disagrees” with the allegations, but the developments “may cause us to change the way we operate.” JM Smucker also cautions that litigation by consumers alleging harm from a product that is ultra-processed or contains certain ingredients could pose a risk. It adds in its <a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/91419/000009141926000050/sjm-20260430.htm" target="_blank" rel="">Fiscal Year 2026 10-K filed</a> in June that “adverse publicity from such allegations could materially adversely affect us, regardless of whether such allegations are true or whether we are ultimately held liable.” Taken together, the filings suggest the packaged food industry’s exposure to the UPF debate is broader than any single regulatory proposal. For Campbell’s, the potential impact could range from higher compliance and reformulation costs to weaker demand for products affected by new definitions or labeling requirements. For companies such as Conagra, Kraft Heinz and JM Smucker, meanwhile, the risk is already showing up in litigation disclosures, while PepsiCo’s filing highlights the potential for consumer perceptions alone to affect sales. How those risks ultimately translate into changes on grocery shelves, and whether the federal government establishes a definition of UPFs with regulatory consequences, remains to be seen. But the disclosures make clear that the debate has moved beyond a public-health discussion and into the risk-management plans of some of the industry’s largest companies.

It’s been around 20 years since Chobani arrived on the US market, securing a spot in the sprawling yogurt aisle. The brand is still widely associated with its Greek yogurt heritage, but investment in other formats and categories has introduced it to a diverse range of shoppers. Marketing initiatives such as coffee creamer ‘flavour drops’ – limited-edition flavours sold for just one month – have sparked interest among younger generations of coffee drinkers. Meanwhile, Chobani’s acquisition of Daily Harvest, a company specialising in nutritious ready meals, has opened the door to an entirely new category. In recent years, Chobani has found another profitable niche: coffee. The company entered liquid creamers and cold coffee, the latter through the acquisition of La Colombe. At the same time, resilient interest in high-protein diets and the increased uptake of GLP-1 weight-loss medications have supported sales in its established categories, particularly yogurt. Chobani says it is experiencing rapid growth, and that expanding manufacturing capacity and increasing flexibility are key to maintaining this trajectory. The company recently announced a $1.2bn investment to acquire and expand a 1.5m sqft manufacturing hub in Allentown, Pennsylvania. The plant is strategically located near several major markets and provides operational capacity, access to manufacturing labour and close proximity to hundreds of dairy farms. Allentown is also geographically close to La Colombe’s plant in Michigan. Together, the two campuses give Chobani a resilient supply, production and distribution network in the midst of a US dairy heartland. A company spokesperson said: “Both states have hundreds of farms that will supply high-quality milk which is essential for Chobani and La Colombe products. From Greek yogurt to high-protein innovations, Chobani continues to see strong demand for products that help consumers incorporate natural, protein-rich products into their routines in convenient formats.” Looking ahead, Chobani is focused on advancing innovation around natural, high-protein offerings that fit into modern lifestyles, the spokesperson added. La Colombe has been ahead of the shift for more than a decade, beginning with Draft Latte on tap in cafés and eventually bringing that signature experience into retail. “That history gives La Colombe a unique credibility in cold coffee and a strong foundation from which to continue innovating as the category evolves.” The company is facing stiff competition in the category, however, with the likes of Nestlé and Danone also vying for consumers’ attention. Hydration needs are keeping consumers reaching for bottled water data shows. In May, the International Bottled Water Association (IBWA) shared that for the tenth consecutive year, bottled water was the top packaged beverage sold, based on data from Beverage Marketing Corporation (BMC), Wintersville, Ohio. By volume share, bottled water sales in 2025 accounted for 45.5% of refreshment beverages. “It’s time to recognize bottled water as America’s official beverage of choice. Choosing water first helps keep Americans healthier because it contains one essential, zero-calorie ingredient: clean and refreshing water,” said Matt Herrick, International Bottled Water Association’s president and CEO, in a statement at the time of the release. “As our country works to reverse troubling trends in obesity, diabetes, and heart disease, encouraging Americans to choose water instead of sugary drinks is a simple step with real impact.” Gary Hemphill, managing director of research at BMC, offers greater details about the bottled water category. “Standard bottled water will always have a market, but the growth is in premiumization and functionality.” – Mitch Madoff, head of retail partnerships at Keychain “The bottled water category continues to experience modest growth, and is projected to grow 2.5% this year to more than 17 billion gallons,” he says. “It remains the largest beverage category in the U.S. on a volume basis.” Mitch Madoff, head of retail partnerships at Keychain, New York, also points to the notable performance for bottled water in the United States. “Bottled water has been one of the stronger-performing beverage categories over the last year, steadily stealing share from traditional soft drinks as consumers move toward zero-calorie, zero-sugar options,” Madoff says. “On Keychain, we’ve seen strong growth of flavored and sparkling waters specifically. A big part of that growth has come from the premium end. Brands like Voss, Acqua Panna, and Icelandic Glacial are seeing positive growth and momentum. Consumers want a real reason to reach for a specific water brand, so the ones with clear, differentiated identity are standing out on shelves.” In its January report, “Bottled Water in the US,” Netherlands-based Innova Market Insights notes that unflavored bottled water encompasses much of the U.S. market, but recognizes that the evolving market is offering an opportunity for flavored bottled waters that deliver on purposeful products. “Brands face a lot of competition in liquid refreshments in the U.S. and many different beverage products, flavors and functions that go beyond just hydration,” Innova’s report states. Earlier this year, oHy, Grand Rapids, Mich., announced the nationwide launch of two new flavors: Blackberry and Pineapple. With these additions, oHy's portfolio now spans six flavors: Blackberry, Pineapple, Strawberry Lemon, Cherry Lime, Lemon Lime and Raspberry. “From the start, we’ve been focused on innovating with purpose and meeting evolving consumer demand,” said Trent Hartwig, co-founder and CEO of oHy, in a statement. “Our ability to deliver hydrogen water at scale sets us apart in the industry, and these new flavors represent an important step forward. The launch of Blackberry and Pineapple is not only an expansion of our flavor lineup, but also serves as a foundation for future innovations across the functional hydration market.” Saratoga Spring Water, a brand of Primo Brands, also embraced flavor with the launch of Saratoga Collection, a new line of flavored sparkling waters. Saratoga Collection introduces four new flavors: Untitled Berry No.3, Abstraction of Lime, Anatomy of a Peach and Mango Dragon Fruit Perspective. Although flavor releases might be providing consumers with more bottled water options, experts note that it’s the category’s ability to support healthy hydration that is keeping it as a go-to beverage for consumers. “The electrolyte and supplement trend is reshaping the category pretty significantly and creating a two-fold effect,” Keychain’s Madoff says. “It’s driving demand for electrolyte-enhanced drinks, while also increasing demand for standard spring water, which consumers are pairing with their own electrolyte or supplement packets. So, both trends are feeding each other. “On Keychain, we’re seeing this reflected in the growth of brands like Propel+, an electrolyte-infused water from Propel,” he continues. “What it tells you is that people are willing to pay more for functional benefits even in a category as basic as water. Brands that can credibly offer that, like Smartwater’s Antioxidant Alkaline line or BodyArmor Flash I.V., are taking advantage of that.” BMC’s Hemphill notes how bottled water’s health attributes has benefited the category. “Bottled water’s overall positioning remains the same: it is the affordable, portable lifestyle beverage for today’s consumers,” he says. “It’s also the ultimate health beverage with no calories or sugar. This has continued to help propel category growth.” Innova’s report notes that the addition of value-added functional attributes has transformed water from a commodity to a wellness beverage. “These can include functional benefits such as beauty from within, gut health, energy, or others,” the report says. “Trendy ingredients like electrolytes, B vitamins, fiber, probiotics or prebiotics, and collagen add functionality to bottled water in the U.S. and make it better than just water.” Wave Kids is tapping into this with four new canned waters, featuring what it calls better-for-you ingredients. Berry Beach Party and Tropical Vacay are non-sparkling flavored waters offering hydration with organic coconut water powder and ocean minerals, along with organic agave, organic natural flavors, and organic fruit and vegetable juice for color. Meanwhile, Melon Berry Bliss and Apple Adventure are fizzy flavored waters with a dash of prebiotic fiber from organic acacia, plus organic agave, organic natural flavors, and organic fruit and vegetable juice for color. However, not all specialty factions of the bottled water market have fared as well, resulting in bifurcated performance. “Alkaline waters are seeing modest but positive growth, while purified and mineral waters are trending down,” Keychain’s Madoff says. “This pattern tells us that for specialty waters to stand out, they have to be offering something genuinely differentiated or be attached to a strong, larger brand. Smartwater Alkaline is a good example of this because the brand does a lot of the heavy lifting. Standalone specialty brands without that anchor are having a harder time.” Despite these challenges, Madoff still foresees premiumization to have a positive impact on bottled water going forward. “Standard bottled water will always have a market, but the growth is in premiumization and functionality,” he says. “Consumers are increasingly thinking about hydration as a part of a broader wellness routine, not just quenching thirst, and they’re willing to pay for products that back that narrative.
