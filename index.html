Why functional drinks aren’t translating into sales

Las bebidas funcionales enfrentan un desafío crítico en Reino Unido: el interés del consumidor no se traduce en ventas reales en bares y restaurantes. Aunque la conciencia sobre bebidas posicionadas en salud, bienestar e hidratación crece, la categoría sigue siendo marginal en el mercado de bebidas sin alcohol fuera del hogar. El gap entre awareness e intención de compra señala oportunidades de innovación en posicionamiento y distribución que las marcas de bebidas deben resolver para monetizar la demanda emergente en canales on-premise.

Texto completo del artículo
Consumer interest in functional drinks is outpacing category performance in Great Britain’s pubs, bars and restaurants, leaving brands with the challenge of turning growing awareness into actual purchases. Functional beverages refer to non-alcoholic drinks positioned around benefits for physical or mental health, wellbeing or specific bodily functions, such as hydration, energy and gut health. Despite growing interest, the category remains a small part of the on-premise soft drinks market. According to NielsenIQ (NIQ), functional drinks account for just 0.1% of soft drinks volume share in GB, despite distribution growing by 3.5%. That wider availability has not, however, translated into stronger sales. Volume rate of sale per outlet has declined by 16.7% year on year, pointing to a gap between getting products into venues and persuading consumers to buy them. “The challenge is more so execution rather than demand not being there,” says Cerys Littlejohn of NielsenIQ. One of the barriers is visibility. Unlike beer and cider, which benefit from prominent positioning on bars and draught pumps, functional drinks are often kept in fridges behind the bar, making them less visible to consumers. How brands communicate their benefits also matters. NIQ says consumers respond better to straightforward, easy-to-understand propositions such as hydration, energy, sleep and relaxation, rather than more complex or specialist functional claims. There is also some evidence that consumers are prepared to pay a premium for these benefits. NIQ’s GB BrandTrack survey, conducted in February 2026, found that 51% of respondents would pay more for a soft drink offering additional health benefits. Health benefits alone are unlikely to win over consumers. When choosing a drink, the fundamentals still count, with brands needing to balance their functional proposition with taste and value for money. According to NIQ, price and value for money, alongside taste and flavour options, are among the most important considerations for consumers. Around half of respondents identified each as important factors in their decision-making. Taste is also key to encouraging repeat purchases. As Littlejohn explains, consumers are not buying functionality for its own sake. “They also buy the products because it tastes good,” she says. So where might functional drinks find their place in the on-premise market? NIQ points to casual social occasions, including quick drinks and catch-ups with friends, as potential opportunities for brands to make these beverages a more natural part of the experience. Functional drinks could also appeal to consumers moderating their alcohol intake. However, NIQ says it is too early to determine whether they would directly replace traditional drinking occasions. For manufacturers, the opportunity is not simply to expand distribution. It is to make products visible, accessible and easy to understand, while delivering on the taste and value consumers expect. Until then, consumer curiosity may continue to run ahead of category sales.

Beyond the health halo: Why functional claims aren’t enough

Según investigación de NielsenIQ, las bebidas funcionales enfrentan un desafío clave: aunque los beneficios funcionales (energía, hidratación, inmunidad) atraen interés, el sabor y la relación precio-valor son los factores decisivos en la compra. Este hallazgo cuestiona la estrategia de diferenciación por claims funcionales y advierte sobre la dificultad de justificar premios de precio basados únicamente en beneficios alegados, relevante para empresas que desarrollan portfolios de bebidas funcionales en mercados competitivos.

Texto completo del artículo
Functional drinks are built around the promise of doing more than simply quenching thirst, with brands increasingly highlighting benefits ranging from energy and hydration to gut health and immunity. But while these added benefits may attract consumer interest, they aren’t necessarily what seals the deal. NielsenIQ (NIQ) research suggests taste and value for money remain the leading considerations when consumers choose functional beverages, raising questions about how brands can justify a premium without compromising on the fundamentals. Despite the growing focus on functional ingredients, consumers’ priorities when choosing these products remain rooted in the fundamentals of a good drink. NIQ data shows that taste and value for money top consumers’ priorities, both cited by 50.1% of respondents, while health and wellness benefits rank lower at 29%. “It’s about price and value for money. That’s what consumers feel are important factors for choosing a functional drink. It’s joint top with taste and flavour options,” says Cerys Littlejohn of NIQ. “The fundamentals need to win first.” While health claims might differentiate a product, the findings suggest they don’t replace the basic expectations consumers have around taste and price. Taste and value remain central to purchasing decisions, but NIQ research suggests consumers also recognise the added value of functional benefits. It found that 51% of consumers would pay more for a soft drink with additional health benefits. “Value doesn’t necessarily translate as cheap. Consumers are happy to pay more because there’s this added benefit or addition into their drink that means it’s worth that cost,” Littlejohn explains. For manufacturers, the opportunity isn’t necessarily to make functional drinks cheaper, but to demonstrate why their additional benefits justify a higher price point. For brands looking to command a premium, convincing consumers to pay more is only part of the challenge. Functional drinks must also deliver on taste if they are to secure repeat purchases. That can be easier said than done. Adding functional ingredients can bring formulation challenges, particularly when it comes to flavour and texture. Whey protein, for example, can contribute to astringency in beverages – a drying or puckering sensation in the mouth, particularly in acidic formulations. Research published in the<a href="https://www.journalofdairyscience.org/article/S0022-0302(20)30391-X/fulltext" target="_blank" rel="" title="https://www.journalofdairyscience.org/article/S0022-0302(20)30391-X/fulltext"> Journal of Dairy Science</a> has examined how whey proteins can interact with saliva to create this sensation, highlighting one of the sensory challenges manufacturers face when developing protein-fortified drinks. For brands, this creates a balancing act: delivering the desired nutritional benefits without compromising the drinking experience. “If it doesn’t taste good, the repeat purchasing won’t happen,” Littlejohn says. “We can’t override the importance of functionality and let that hinder the taste profile of the drink. It needs to still taste good.” Ultimately, functional benefits may give consumers a reason to try a product, but taste is what gives them a reason to come back.

Iconic bourbon brand continues to bring its “Perfectly Unreasonable” campaign to life through new visual world LORETTO, Ky., November 17, 2026 /PRNewswire/ – Maker’s Mark®, the iconic Kentucky bourbon, today revealed its refreshed global brand identity with an updated color palette, typography, photography and graphic elements that take Maker’s Mark’s unmistakable red wax center stage. Beginning today with the launch of the brand’s new website, consumers will experience the new design across digital, social, advertising and retail channels, as well as events and experiences around the world. “My grandparents weren’t thinking about ‘branding’ when they created Maker’s Mark. They cared about a hand-made process in service to flavor and design that honors that vision,” said Rob Samuels, eighth-generation whisky maker and managing director. “That deeply personal approach created some of the most recognizable symbols in bourbon. This new and exceptional work brings that same intention forward honoring what they created and ensuring Maker’s Mark feels as personal and distinctive today as it did from the start.” The new brand identity brings to life the remarkable lengths Maker’s Mark has gone to make its whisky since the beginning. Founders Margie and Bill Samuels Sr. built Maker’s Mark by doing things differently—burning the 170-year-old family recipe to create a more balanced bourbon; choosing their homeplace in outlying Loretto for its water source and proximity to grain growers; and hand-dipping every bottle in signature red wax. That challenger spirit serves as inspiration for the new visual world. Custom typography inspired by Margie’s handwriting illustrates her wit and wisdom, while tactile wax marks created from natural elements found across Star Hill Farm—wheat stalks, oak rings and native flora—connect the design to the people, land and craft behind Maker’s Mark. Developed in partnership with creative agency Turner Duckworth London®, the resulting visual system reimagines the thoughtful, handcrafted sensibility behind the bourbon, bottle and brand in a modern, more minimal way that remains unmistakably Maker’s Mark. “In a world increasingly driven by speed and automation, we still believe some things are worth making slowly, by hand,” said Chloe Lloyd-Jones, vice president of global brand marketing, Maker’s Mark. “We returned to Star Hill Farm, working with natural materials from the land and our signature red wax to create a visual identity that reflects the ‘Perfectly Unreasonable’ spirit behind our bourbon. The result gives us a powerful way to tell our story around the world, while creating experiences that feel personal and relevant in each market.” Elements of the brand identity have already come to life through experiences in the United States and China and out-of-home advertising in Australia and the UK. The brand identity will continue to roll out across markets and channels throughout 2027, giving consumers new ways to experience Maker’s Mark.
